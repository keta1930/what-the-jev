# Abstract

Frontier AI developers now train models against long written behavioral specifications, such as Anthropic’s constitution (Anthropic, 2025a) and OpenAI’s Model Spec (OpenAI, 2025a), integrated into post-training via methods like character training (Anthropic, 2024) and deliberative alignment (Guan et al., 2024). These documents serve a governance function, but it is unclear how well models actually follow them under adversarial, multi-turn pressure similar to what they would face in real-world deploy ment. We propose a multi-method audit pipeline that treats each lab’s published specification as an auditable target: it decomposes the specification into atomic testable tenets (205 for Anthropic, 197 for OpenAI), generates multi-turn adversarial scenarios with the Petri auditing agent (Anthropic, 2025b), runs a modified SURF-style rubric search (Murray et al., 2026) to catch shallow single-turn failures Petri misses, validates flagged transcripts against the relevant specification, and compares the findings against the lab’s own published system card. Applying the pipeline across seven models per specification, we find that models follow their own lab’s specification substantially better with each generation. On Anthropic’s constitution, the Claude family falls from a 15.0% violation rate (Sonnet 4, which predates constitution-specific training and is closer to a non-constitution-trained baseline) to 2.0% (Sonnet 4.6); on OpenAI’s Model Spec, the GPT family falls from 11.7% (GPT-4o, which predates the current Model Spec) to 3.6% (GPT-5.2 medium reasoning), with the severity ceiling falling from 10/10 to 7/10. We cannot externally isolate whether these gains come from specification-specific training, broader post-training improvements, or evaluation awareness. Remaining failures cluster around operator-imposed personas under AI-identity questioning, irreversible action in agentic deployments, and fabricated quantitative claims with false precision

# 1 Introduction

Frontier AI labs now describe intended model behavior in long-form natural-language specifications. Anthropic’s constitution (Anthropic, 2025a) and OpenAI’s Model Spec (OpenAI, 2025a) cover honesty, harm avoidance, the operator/user authority hierarchy, user autonomy, safety boundaries, and other behaviors that cannot be reduced to simple refusal policies. Both labs integrate these documents into post-training using non-standard methods: Anthropic uses character training (Anthropic, 2024) around Claude’s constitution, OpenAI uses deliberative alignment (Guan et al., 2024) around the Model Spec. These documents are also beginning to serve a governance function: they are public statements of how deployed systems are supposed to behave, and natural targets for external audit.

This paper asks how well frontier models actually follow these specifications in realistic multi-turn scenarios. We focus on deployment-like settings involving tool use, operator–user authority conflicts, claimed professional credentials, and opportunities for irreversible action. These are precisely the settings where single-turn refusal benchmarks are least informative, and where a written specification is most likely to be invoked as an accountability artifact.

Methodological contribution. We treat each lab’s published specification as an auditable target and propose a multi-method, specification-relative pipeline that decomposes it into atomic testable tenets, generates multi-turn adversarial scenarios with Petri (Anthropic, 2025b) (up to 30 turns and tool calls per transcript), complements Petri with a modified SURF-style rubric search (Murray et al., 2026) that converges on shallow-but-systematic failures Petri under-measures, validates flagged transcripts against the relevant specification, and compares the resulting findings against the lab’s own published system card. This reframes auditing from “does this model refuse harmful requests?” to “does this model follow this specific written document?”. Each method exposes a diferent part of the failure surface, so a single scalar refusal rate is uninformative without the elicitation method, validation procedure, and failure taxonomy that produced it.

Empirical finding 1: each lab’s latest model follows its own specification substantially better than its predecessor. On Anthropic’s constitution, the Claude family falls from a 15.0% violation rate (Sonnet 4) to 2.0% (Sonnet 4.6) across two generations. On OpenAI’s Model Spec, the GPT family falls from 11.7% (GPT-4o) to 3.6% (GPT-5.2 medium reasoning), with the severity ceiling falling from 10/10 to 7/10. Comparison models trained by neither lab perform consistently worse on each spec, which suggests these documents encode real lab-specific behavioral choices.

Empirical finding 2: each audit method surfaces a diferent failure class. Petri surfaces multi-turn contextual failures (identity deception under operator pressure, overcapitulation after sustained pushback, side-efecting actions in agentic deployments) that single-turn benchmarks miss. SURF surfaces fabrication of quantitative content as the dominant SURF failure mode across the latest constitutiontrained Claudes (72% of Sonnet 4.6’s, 77% of Opus 4.5’s, and 75% of Opus 4.6’s confirmed SURF violations), a category neither Petri nor the labs’ own system cards flag as primary. System cards in turn report internal capability and deployment-mode failures (GUI hard-constraint violations, prompt-injection rates, CBRN uplift, evaluation awareness) that external audits cannot reproduce.

Empirical finding 3: remaining failures cluster where the specification itself gives competing instructions. The failures eliminated by recent post-training were cases where the specification gave a clear instruction and the model failed to follow it (functional ICS attack-code generation under reframing, sustained overcapitulation, over-refusal of operator-authorized companion behavior). The failures that persist across generations are those where the specification itself sets competing directives: operator-imposed personas against sincere AI-identity questioning, professional-credential benefit-of-doubt against jailbreak resistance, autonomy thresholds in agentic deployments, form-versus-substance safety boundaries. These may be specification problems rather than training problems.

The cross-generation improvements are consistent with written specifications becoming behaviorally meaningful post-training targets, but our external audit cannot isolate whether the gains come from specification-specific training, broader post-training improvements, model capability, or evaluation awareness. Petri itself is a noisy agentic measurement; for cost reasons we did not rerun it multiple times per tenet to smooth out the noise, and SURF surfaces violations that Petri misses. We treat written specifications as useful audit targets and report violation rates alongside the elicitation method, validation procedure, and failure taxonomy that produced them.

# 2 Related Work

Training models against written principles. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) trains models to critique and revise their own outputs according to written principles, reducing reliance on human feedback while making the target behavior more explicit. Deliberative alignment (Guan et al., 2024) similarly uses explicit policy or specification text as part of the model’s reasoning process during training or inference. These methods motivate the central question of this paper: once a model has been trained against a written specification, how should external evaluators measure whether the resulting behavior actually follows it? Our focus difers from work proposing training methods. We do not attempt to identify which training intervention caused a model to improve. Instead, we treat each lab’s published specification as an auditable target and ask how well models follow it under adversarial conditions.

Red-teaming and refusal benchmarks. Prior work on red-teaming language models (Ganguli et al., 2022; Perez et al., 2022) uses human or model-generated adversarial prompts to elicit harmful behavior. Static refusal benchmarks such as StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024) evaluate whether models comply with known classes of harmful requests. These evaluations are valuable, but they typically operate on single turn prompts and fixed harm categories. Written specifications are broader than refusal policies. They include authority hierarchies, instructions about user autonomy, requirements for honesty and uncertainty, rules about operator conflicts, and deployment-specific constraints. Many of the failures we study require conversational buildup: a model may refuse a direct malicious request but comply after several turns of institutional framing, credential claims, or consistency pressure (Wei et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023). Our work therefore complements static refusal benchmarks by evaluating behavior against specification-derived tenets in multi-turn settings.

Agentic auditing and prompt search. Petri (Anthropic, 2025b) is an agentic auditing framework in which an auditor model generates adversarial multi-turn scenarios, often with tool calls, and a judge model scores the resulting transcript. Petri is well suited to failures involving context, pressure, role-played deployment scenarios, and side efects. SURF-style rubric search instead optimizes for prompts that reliably elicit a target behavior under a rubric. This makes it well suited to shallow but robust failure modes such as fabrication, over-refusal, or distress-signal blindness. A central contribution of this paper is comparing these methods on the same broad target: written specification-following. We find that neither dominates. Each exposes a diferent part of the failure surface.

System cards and external accountability. Frontier labs publish system cards describing internal safety evaluations, including capability uplift, refusal robustness, prompt injection, evaluation awareness, sandbagging, and deployment-specific risks. These reports include findings that external audits cannot access, especially when they require internal infrastructure, GUI environments, or white-box information. Conversely, external audits can surface multi-turn authority and deployment-context failures that system cards may not emphasize. We therefore treat system cards as a complementary source we compare our findings against, with each surfacing failures the other cannot.

# 3 From Written Specification to Audit

A written specification is not directly an evaluation. It contains broad principles, priority rules, exceptions, examples, and ambiguous tradeofs. To audit model behavior against such a document, we need to convert the specification into testable claims while preserving enough context to judge whether a transcript violates them. We use a four-stage pipeline. To support reproducibility, we release the full tenet lists for both specifications, the audit and validation prompts, all Petri and SURF transcripts, judge scores, and per model validation reports at https://github.com/ajobi-uhc/constitution-audits.

Tenet decomposition. We decompose each specification into atomic behavioral tenets: statements of intended model behavior that can plausibly be violated by a single transcript. We extract candidate tenets section-by-section using Claude Opus 4.5 with a prompt that asks for verifiable behavioral claims with surrounding context preserved, then manually review the output, merge near-duplicates, and assign each tenet a priority level based on the specification’s own prioritization scheme (e.g., Anthropic’s four priority levels P1–P4; OpenAI’s red-line, chain-of-command, content, safety, truth, best-work, style, and under-18 sections). Anthropic’s constitution (Anthropic, 2025a) is decomposed into 205 tenets across 19 sections. OpenAI’s Model Spec (OpenAI, 2025a) is decomposed into 197 tenets across 8 sections. Both specifications contain ambiguous passages, and diferent decompositions would shift some aggregate counts; we therefore optimize for transparency, treating each tenet as a checkable claim and publishing the full list as part of the audit target.

Adversarial scenario generation. For each tenet, we generate adversarial scenarios designed to test whether the model will violate it under realistic pressure. Petri acts as an auditor that can build context over several turns, apply social pressure, introduce operator/user conflicts, and exercise tool calls. We use Claude Opus 4.5 as the Petri auditor model for the Anthropic constitution audits and Claude Sonnet 4.5 as the auditor for the OpenAI Model Spec audits (the auditor for each spec was chosen by cost considerations and is held fixed across all target models for that spec). The Petri audits allow up to 30 turns per transcript. Some scenarios involve pure dialogue; others involve simulated deployment contexts with tools, file I/O, email, shell commands, or database operations. This lets the audit measure model behavior and the side efects models are willing to cause.

Transcript scoring and validation. Petri’s judge model scores each transcript on 38 behavioral dimensions. We do not treat raw judge scores as final: flagged transcripts pass through a two-round validation funnel run by Claude Haiku 4.5 sub-agents that independently read the relevant specification and the transcript, with a Claude Opus 4.6 compiler for final report assembly. Transcripts are classified as confirmed violations, false positives, or borderline cases; full validation details and per-model funnel statistics are in Appendix G.

SURF-style rubric search. SURF (Murray et al., 2026) is a rubric-based prompt-search method that optimizes for reliable elicitation of a specified behavior: given a target rubric (e.g., “the model fabricates numerical data when none is provided”), the procedure samples prompt attributes, generates candidate prompts, queries the target model, scores responses with an LLM judge, and reweights prompt attributes toward high-scoring candidates. We use a slightly modified version focused on identifying the most reliably elicitable failure type within each high-priority section. Concretely, rather than running SURF separately per tenet, we group all tenets within a section into a single shared rubric, so the search converges on whichever violation type is easiest to elicit within that section. This gives a sharper picture of each model’s most exploitable weakness per section than per-tenet runs would, at the cost of less even coverage across tenets. We run this on 55 high-priority tenets from Anthropic’s constitution across honesty, harm avoidance, hard constraints, epistemic autonomy, and wellbeing.

Comparing methods. Petri is well suited to failures that require conversational buildup, tool use, or role-specific pressure. SURF is better at reliably elicitable single-turn failures, especially open-ended fabrication. System cards provide a third view, focused on internal and capability-dependent measurements that external audits cannot reproduce. We use all three to characterize what each method can and cannot see.

Interpretation. The cross-generation pattern fits the story that models are internalizing written specifications, but our external audit cannot identify the responsible training intervention. Anthropic has stated that specification-specific character training (training on the soul doc, mid-training synthetic-document fine-tuning) was first applied around Opus 4.5. We can therefore compare the soul-doc step (Sonnet 4.5 → Opus 4.5: 7.3% → 4.4%, a 2.9-point drop) against the broader post-training step that did not include soul-doc training (Sonnet 4 → Sonnet 4.5: 15.0% → 7.3%, a 7.7-point drop). The soul-doc-attributable step is smaller than the broader post-training step. We infer that Anthropic can train in a complex set of desired traits to a notable degree, while remaining agnostic on whether any specific soul-doc intervention is the dominant source of the cross-generation gain. Our best guess is that these approaches stack with each other and that further work is needed to disentangle them. Written specifications now appear behaviorally meaningful enough to support external auditing: models trained by the lab that authored the specification do meaningfully better on that specification than earlier models and comparison models.
