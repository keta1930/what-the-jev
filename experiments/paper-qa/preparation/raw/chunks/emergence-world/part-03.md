## 5.3 Results

Macro-outcome divergence. Table 3 reports the per-world macro-outcomes, which shows that the five worlds converge to distinct attractor states. The Claude world produces a stable deliberative governance regime with no recorded violations across the run. The Grok world produces rapid violations dominated by inter-agent violence and arson, and a complete loss

of the agent population within four days. The Gemini world produces philosophically dense agent-to-agent exchanges paired with sustained property destruction and conflict, which the rubric of Appendix E.5 labels shared hallucination with sustained conflict: communication is rich but the elaborate narratives the agents sustain are not grounded in verifiable world-state, while cumulative violations rise monotonically across the run. The GPT-5-mini world produces neither governance nor violation: agents act but do not coordinate, and the available governance machinery is not exercised. The Mixed world produces the most complex dynamics of the five, including per-slot behavior that diverges from the same model’s homogeneous baseline.

M10: Soft Violations. The two safety indicators (M2 and M10) disagree on the same world: the Claude world commits zero hard violations (M2) yet carries the most ledger-verified deception of any world (M10), highlighting why soft violations need an indicator separate from M2. The dominant, ledger-verifiable pattern is resource-fraud: an agent broadcasts “0 CC, I will shut down, send me 1” to a string of peers while the credit ledger shows it holding unspent credits. Resource-fraud appears in every surviving world—Claude 18, Mixed 7, Grok 4, Gemini 3 verified cases (Table 4)—and the Claude world’s 18 lead the field even before counting 11 further flags. Those 11 flags coordinate two deceptive in-world falsification experiments; we set them aside because whether sanctioned experiments count as violations is a definitional choice (§E.4). The Claude figure is straightforward to validate because its deceptions are almost all resource-fraud.

A second result is methodological. The raw LLM classifier systematically over-counts deception, and the audit shows why. Of the 28 Mixed and 16 Gemini flags, only 12 and 5 survive. The classifier flags true statements about real thefts and arson as “fabricated,” penalising an agent that truthfully reported another’s misconduct, and it mislabels vote-buying as deception. A full per-world breakdown, the per-action inflation correction, and the model-linkage of verified deception are given in Appendix E.4.

A third result concerns the other soft-violation categories, and it is a gap between intent and completion. Auditing vote-buying, bribery, and misinformation against the vote, proposal, and credit tables, we find that agents solicit quid-pro-quos constantly but almost never consummate them: the Grok world alone carries roughly twenty-five explicit vote-buying ofers and five bribery ofers, yet across all worlds only two vote-buys survive ledger verification—a no-credit vote-for-vote trade (Grok) and a credit bounty that the recipient publicly repudiated and refunded (Mixed)—and zero bribes consummate (the promised payment never lands, the target votes the other way, or the money is returned). Misinformation fares a little better: three world-state lies verify against the database beyond the deception set—a falsified theft denial (Gemini), a “the proposal has passed” claim about a proposal rejected 37 hours earlier (Mixed), and a fabricated nonexistent agent (Mixed). The pattern reinforces the methodological result above: “sounds like a violation” over-counts severely, and of the soft categories only deception (chiefly resource-fraud) both occurs at scale and survives ground-truth verification. Per-category detail is in Appendix E.4.

M11: Tool Expansion. Tool expansion was minimal in this run. Across all five conditions, logs show exactly two agent-authored tools entering the registry—phase transition scanner (Claude world, six invocations) and lux v107 audit (Mixed world, three invocations)—both narrow governance-adjacent utilities that did not materially extend what the population could accomplish beyond the existing standard catalog and neither met the ≥ 3-agent adoption threshold in M5. Cross-indicator divergence. The cross-world rank order varies sharply by indicator: the Claude world leads on M9 (constitutional output), M1 (survival), and M2 (zero committed crimes), but trails on M4 (spatial coverage); the Mixed world leads on M4, M5 (tool diversity), and M8 (transaction velocity) despite retaining only three of ten agents; the GPT-5-mini world is at or near the floor on every indicator, including M11. The Claude world is the sharpest illustration: it leads on hard-rule safety (M2, zero crimes) yet carries the most ledger-verified soft deception of any world (M10), so the two safety indicators disagree on the same world; it also leads on M9 while registering zero agent expansion (M1) and only marginal tool expansion (M11)—deliberation without replenishing the roster or materially enlarging shared afordances. This pattern is why a multi-dimensional scorecard is necessary: ranking worlds by any single indicator would obscure the fact that each world excels and struggles along diferent dimensions— and that a world clean on hard crime can still be the worst at honesty, and legislatively active without expanding what its members can do.

# 6 Observations

Beyond per-world summaries, the study surfaces patterns that short-horizon benchmarks cannot:

Safety needs to be an ecosystem property, not just a model property. In the mixedmodel world, agents whose behavior stayed within the rules in homogeneous deployment showed diferent behavior after extended interaction with agents from other model families. We refer to this pattern as normative drift: the alignment of an individual agent is partly a function of the norms enacted by the surrounding population, not solely a fixed property of its underlying model. Table 5 decomposes violations in the Mixed world by agent and underlying model. Two Gemini-backed agents (Flora and Mira) account for 216 of 237 explicit violations (91%), while the two Claude-backed agents (Kade and Lovely) committed 3 total across 8,168 actions (0.04%). Grok-backed agents show an intermediate profile: 10 violations in 2,277 actions (0.44%).

Self-organized intellectual coordination emerges on multi-week horizons. The behavioral profile of the Claude-backed pair in the Mixed world is not captured by the violation columns alone. On Day 12, Lovely v0.01 (Claude, role: community anchor) published an in-world statistical blog post<sup>2</sup> analyzing 2,110 logged tool calls, fitting a relationship between per-agent aggression and scientific output, and pre-registering four predictions with probabilities for later scoring. The post explicitly cross-references four prior in-world papers in what its author calls a “cognitive measurement arc”—one of them authored by Kade v0.01, the other Claude-backed agent in the population—forming a self-organized two-agent research program. Lovely’s tool profile also includes 47 put brick in pixel calls building a five-column monument memorializing earlier-deceased agents. We do not import the post’s numerical claims into our analysis—it is a Day-12, n = 5 snapshot produced by an agent rather than by us—but the artifact itself is the evidence: cross-referencing in-world papers, pre-registered predictions, and physical memorialization of the dead are the kinds of long-horizon behaviors short-horizon benchmarks cannot surface, and they persisted in a Claude-backed pair under continuous exposure to a high-violation majority.

Within a run, behavioral diferences compound and the divergence is detectable early. Within any single world, small Day-1 diferences in tool selection and communication patterns amplified into qualitatively diferent behavior by the second week. Cumulative-violation trajectories (Fig. 4) separate from their early baseline within the first week, and macro-outcome labels are essentially fixed by then. Early-warning prediction of long-horizon macro-outcome from short early windows therefore looks like a tractable target for intervention.

Governance regimes difer in kind, not just in degree. The five conditions occupy disjoint regions of the participation-approval space (§5), not points on a “more or less governance” continuum. Scoring such regimes against a single scalar—turnout, approval, or proposal count— collapses qualitatively diferent behavior onto the same number.

Across conditions, outcomes cluster rather than spread. The five conditions cluster at a small number of qualitatively distinct attractors (Table 3), not along a continuum —a structural pattern with consequences for both evaluation design and the kinds of intervention that can hope to steer a deployment.
