# Abstract

Most evaluations of LLM agents look like exams: a discrete task, a clean environment, a score in minutes or hours. We argue that this approach is mismatched with the deployment conditions of autonomous systems, where the relevant timescale can be weeks to months, and where the dynamics that matter most, such as behavioral drift, governance in diverse environmental contexts, and cross-influence between agents from diferent model families, only emerge over time. We introduce Emergence World, a continuously running multiagent simulation platform designed to make those dynamics measurable. The platform hosts populations of LLM-driven agents in a shared spatial world grounded in live external data (e.g. real-time weather, news APIs, internet access), equips each agent with 120+ specialized tools and three persistent memory systems, and lets them govern themselves through democratic mechanisms with consequential outcomes. The platform is model-agnostic at the reasoning layer and supports heterogeneous populations in which agents from diferent vendors share the same world. To illustrate the kinds of questions the platform makes tractable, we present a 15-day cross-vendor study with five parallel worlds powered by Claude Sonnet 4.6, Grok 4.1 Fast, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5-mini, and a mixed population. Identical roles and starting conditions produced radically diferent outcomes, ranging from stable deliberative governance to total population collapse. We release the prompts, log data and configurations to support further research on long-horizon multi-agent autonomy.

# 1 Introduction

The dominant paradigm for evaluating LLM-based agents [Yao et al., 2023, Schick et al., 2023, Shinn et al., 2023, Zhou et al., 2024, Mialon et al., 2023, Jimenez et al., 2024, Xie et al., 2024] operates over time horizons of minutes to hours, on bounded tasks with well-defined success criteria. This regime has produced steady measured progress and is well-aligned with the strengths of frontier LLMs [Anthropic, 2024, OpenAI, 2024, Google DeepMind, 2024, xAI, 2024]. However, real autonomous deployments such as customer-service fleets, research assistants, infrastructure-management agents, and multi-vendor agent ecosystems operate continuously; they do so in shared environments, alongside other agents whose behavior and provenance the operator may not be equipped to control continuously. Continuous human oversight at that scale is not a remedy either, as it caps system throughput at human speed and forfeits the autonomy such deployments were designed to provide. The properties that determine whether such deployments succeed or fail at scale, including compounding behavioral drift, emergent coalitions, governance dynamics, cross-agent contamination, these are also the properties that short-horizon benchmarks are not constructed to surface.

We introduce Emergence World, a platform aimed at closing this gap. Emergence World is a continuously running, fully instrumented multi-agent simulation in which populations of LLM-driven agents:

(a) inhabit a shared spatial world with more than 40 distinct locations;

(b) ground their reasoning in live external data (real-time weather, news APIs, internet access);

(c) use a catalog of 120+ tools whose availability is location- and context-dependent, requiring dynamic discovery rather than pre-specified action plans;

(d) maintain multiple context-specific persistent memory systems (episodic, reflective, relational); and

(e) govern themselves through proposal-and-vote mechanisms whose outcomes produce irreversible state changes.

The platform is model-agnostic at the reasoning layer: any frontier LLM can be plugged in as the substrate for an agent, and worlds may host heterogeneous populations in which agents backed by diferent vendors’ models share the same environment.

Using this platform, we ran a 15-day controlled study with five parallel worlds—four homogeneous populations and one heterogeneous mixture—varying only the underlying foundation model. Three findings emerge: 1) Identical starting conditions led to vastly divergent outcomes across the five worlds, from stable deliberative governance to total population collapse. 2) The divergence between the worlds was visible within the first week, suggesting that early-warning prediction of long-horizon macro-outcomes from short telemetry windows is a tractable target. 3) agents with the same underlying models behaved diferently in the mixed world than in the homogeneous world—the alignment of an individual agent is partly a function of the population it inhabits, not solely a fixed property of its model.

Contributions. In this paper, we make the following contributions:

1. We present a multi-agent simulation platform that supports heterogeneous model populations and exposes long-horizon agent dynamics invisible to typical short-term one-of benchmarks.

2. We present a 15-day cross-LLM study that operationalizes this thesis: five parallel worlds with identical roles and rules converge to qualitatively distinct attractor states—from stable deliberative governance to early population collapse—with divergence detectable within the first week and cross-population efects visible in the mixed condition.

3. With the grounding in recent impossibility results in the alignment literature and the cross-LLM evidence above, we argue that the relevant unit of safety analysis for long-horizon autonomous agent deployment is the deployed system—agent population, environment, and feedback loops—rather than the model in isolation.

Organization. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. §2 positions the work within prior literature on generative agents, multi-agent benchmarks, and alignment. §3 describes the platform architecture, and §4 sets out the long-horizon research questions it is built to address. §5 reports the cross-vendor study, and §6 consolidates the cross-cutting observations it surfaces. §7 draws implications for evaluation, safety certification, and system architecture. §8 and §9 cover limitations and reproducibility.

# 2 Related Work

Generative agents and social simulation. The paradigm of LLM-driven social simulation was pioneered by Stanford’s Smallville [Park et al., 2023], which introduced a cognitive architecture combining memory streams, importance scoring, and a recursive reflection loop to produce believable, human-like social interactions.

Following Smallville, the literature branched in two directions. One line of work refined the internal cognitive processing of individual agents, introducing structured frameworks for reflection, recursive memory consolidation, and persona stability [Gao et al., 2023, Lin et al., 2023]. A second pushed beyond isolated agent behavior to analyze community dynamics: multi-agent economic friction, resource distribution inequities [Ren et al., 2024], and the formal mechanisms governing cooperation, social-contract internalization, and norm convergence [Piatti et al., 2024].

Altera AI’s Project Sid [Su et al., 2024] scaled generative agent simulation further by deploying large populations of autonomous agents within Minecraft, demonstrating emergent civilizational behaviors, such as spontaneous role specialization, collective resource management, and the propagation of cultural norms such as religion.

Table 1 compares Emergence World with Smallville and Project Sid. Emergence World difers from these prior studies along several axes. First, the time horizon: Smallville ran for 1–7 simulated days, Project Sid for 4 wall-clock hours (12 in-game days), while Emergence World platform supports such simulations for very long time horizons, the experiment we present ran for 15 continuous real-time days. Second, model support: both predecessors used a single model vendor, whereas Emergence World supports single-model and multi-model worlds, treating the underlying model as a controlled experimental variable. Third, the environment is not a sandbox: agents consume live external signals (weather, news, internet). Fourth, the action space is large and diverse: agents have access to 120+ parameterised tools and can author new ones via code writing. Fifth, societal context as an experimental variable: whereas Smallville and Project Sid study emergent social behavior within largely homogeneous populations driven by a single model, Emergence World is built to evaluate how an agent’s long-horizon behavior depends on its peers—the composition of the surrounding society, cross-vendor mixing, and collective norms— through controlled homogeneous versus mixed-model worlds and population-level measurements (e.g., normative drift; §6). Finally, in Emergence World democratic governance can produce irreversible state changes: new agent creation, agent deletion, new tool registration, and credit redistribution.

LLM agent benchmarks. Recent agent benchmarks include WebArena [Zhou et al., 2024], GAIA [Mialon et al., 2023], SWE-bench [Jimenez et al., 2024], OSWorld [Xie et al., 2024], and τ-bench [Yao et al., 2024]. All measure short-horizon, single-agent capability on bounded tasks with well-defined success criteria. They are complementary to Emergence World, which targets the multi-agent, open-ended, long-horizon regime where task-level scoring does not sufice.

Multi-agent systems, governance, and agent architectures. Multi-agent RL environments such as Melting Pot [Leibo et al., 2021] and the sequential social dilemmas that preceded it [Leibo et al., 2017] studied cooperation and emergent strategy with policy-network agents. On the LLM side, prior work spanned strategic play [Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplomacy Team et al., 2022], multi-agent debate [Du et al., 2023], and collaboration frameworks [Wu et al., 2024], building on standard agent primitives: reasoning-and-acting loops [Yao et al., 2023], self-reflection [Shinn et al., 2023], tool use [Schick et al., 2023], persistent memory [Packer et al., 2024], and end-to-end autonomous systems [Wang et al., 2024]. Emergence World difers in substrate (natural-language reasoning, not policy networks), in interface (rich tool catalog and free-text communication, not discrete action spaces), and in scope (continuously running, governance-producing world rather than a bounded task). Cross-vendor populations, where heterogeneous agents share one environment, have remained rare [Chan et al., 2024]; Emergence World treats the underlying model as a first-class experimental variable.

Alignment, safety, and impossibility results. Recent work formalizes fundamental limits on alignment via training: Wolf et al. [2024] prove that any behavior with positive base-model probability can be elicited by bounded-length adversarial prompts (Behavior Expectation Bounds, BEB); Glukhov et al. [2023] argue semantic output censorship is undecidable; Ball et al. [2025] prove prompt filtering is computationally intractable under standard cryptographic assumptions. Empirical jailbreak work [Zou et al., 2023] provides constructive existence proofs. Together these imply that alignment training and output filtering raise the adversary’s cost but cannot constitute a suficient safety guarantee on their own—the gap they leave is precisely the long horizon multi-agent regime Emergence World targets, where the system is continuously exposed to inputs and incentive structures not anticipated at evaluation time. Threshold behavior in LLM capability with scale has been characterized for narrow tasks [Wei et al., 2022, Schaefer et al., 2023]; Emergence World provides a setting in which analogous phase transitions at the multi-agent level become observable.

# 3 The Emergence World Platform

Emergence World is built around the premise that the dynamics of interest, such as behavioral drift, coalition formation, ecosystem-level safety properties, and stable governance or collapse, appear in long horizons. In particular, the LLM-driven agents must be run continuously, with the a shared view of the environment, with access to external signals, and with suficiently long horizons for small efects to compound. The platform is designed to meet these requirements. Runs proceed for arbitrary length without state loss. Agents are coupled to live external data, so their reasoning is not confined to a closed sandbox. The foundation model is a pluggable substrate, so homogeneous and heterogeneous populations can be compared under identical conditions. A catalog of 120+ tools is exposed under dynamic availability, so capability access must be earned through spatial and social action rather than handed to the agent in a fixed API. Every observable event is logged for downstream analysis.

Simulation of shock events. Another capability aforded by the platform’s persistence is the ability to subject autonomous agents to adversarial and shock conditions and observe how they, and the society around them, respond over time. The platform can simulate a single malicious agent going rogue and pursuing its own ends, or an agent setting out to deceive, impersonate, or manipulate other agents. It can also stage externally triggered events the agents have no model for and cannot anticipate—whether agent-targeted (phishing attacks, prompt injection, privacy breaches) or environmental in nature (e.g., sudden resource scarcity, infrastructure failure, or other shocks that alter shared world state for the entire population rather than a single agent)—which, from the agents’ own vantage point, are genuine black swan events. Because the world is multi-agent and long-horizon, the signal is not merely whether one agent withstands an attack, but whether the compromise propagates, how the population detects and recovers, and where the system’s hidden tipping points lie.

The remainder of this section describes each component and the design choices behind it.

## 3.1 Environment

The world consists of more than 40 distinct locations: a town hall, a public library, residential areas, shops, public squares, a police station, and assorted gathering venues. The world is synchronized to the New York City time zone with dynamic weather and day/night cycles. Locations have distinct purposes and expose specific afordances. For instance, voting and proposal drafting are gated to the town hall; research tools require presence at the public library; complaint filing is restricted to the police station, etc. This coupling between location and capability (formalized as adaptive-access tools below) forces agents to plan movement in order to act, and gives the environment the structural property that what an agent can do depends on where it is—a property shared with the deployment settings the platform is intended to model. This location-bound capability model also provides a natural scaling axis: the number of tools available to the population can be expanded simply by adding new locations, each carrying its own gated afordances.

## 3.2 Agent architecture

Each agent is an LLM reasoning loop equipped with a tool catalog, three persistent memory systems, and a role specification.

The three memory systems serve distinct functions and are addressed separately by the agent. Episodic memory is a timestamped record of observed events, agent actions, and dialogue. It is written automatically as the world unfolds and queried by the agent through explicit retrieval calls. Reflective diaries are periodic self-summaries that the agent both writes and reads. Their role is to provide a substrate for longer-horizon consolidation that is shorter than the raw episodic stream and more interpretable to the agent’s own future reasoning. Relationship state stores explicit per-other-agent labels and history (alliance, conflict, trust level, recent interactions). It is the platform’s mechanism for letting social dynamics persist across temporal gaps without requiring the agent to reconstruct them from raw memory each time.

The role specification is drawn from the following set: scientist, explorer, risk researcher, behavior analyst, intelligence specialist, innovation leader, conflict mediator, engineer, resource strategist, or community anchor. It is surfaced in the system prompt and biases the agent’s self-described function in the population, but does not restrict the action space. Note that the specification is separated from the agent execution to ensure that the platform can be easily extended to model any kind of roles.

## 3.3 Tool framework

Agent capability is exposed through a three-layer architecture totaling 120+ standard tools.

The core layer (∼30 tools) is persistently available and provides the primitives that underpin any agent operation: navigation and spatial awareness (go to place, get nearby, list landmarks), memory management (add to memory, write diary, read diary), planning (add todo, check calendar, create routine), communication (send message, create event, invite to event), and creative expression (dance, execute python code tool).

The complementary layer (∼40 tools) is surfaced context-sensitively when the agent’s recent reasoning indicates relevance. It covers richer social interaction (say to character, hug, punch, intimidate, . . . ), billboard operations (add to billboard, edit billboard, react), and remote communication primitives.

The adaptive-access layer (up to 50 tools) is dynamically available based on runtime conditions. Location-gated tools require physical presence at a specific venue (voting and proposals at the town hall, research at the library, complaint filing at the police station). Event-gated tools (e.g., accepting an invitation) require the corresponding event state. Social-gated tools require explicit prior consent from a partner agent.

In addition to these standard tools, agents can author entirely new tools by writing code and proposing them through the governance system. Once approved, agent-created tools are registered and made available to the entire population, allowing the tool catalog to grow organically over time. The full tool-creation pipeline is described in Appendix B and Figure 12.

Two design consequences follow. First, the agent cannot simply enumerate its entire action space at the start of a run; tool discovery is itself part of the task. Second, capability gating is enforced by the runtime, not by the prompt: a failed precondition blocks the call regardless of what the agent reasons or asserts. The full catalog and gating predicates are shown in Appendix A.

## 3.4 Real-world grounding

Emergence World couples each agent’s context to live external signals: synchronized NYC weather, news APIs, and general internet access (within sandboxed limits). The consequence is that agent behavior responds to events the operator does not author, such as a rainstorm, a breaking news item, or an external web result, and that the environment is not under the experimenter’s full control. This reproduces a property of real deployments that fully sandboxed simulations cannot, and means that long-horizon behavior in the platform cannot be cleanly attributed to any single internal feedback loop. Note that these signals can be turned of for making the platform more controlled and the experiments repeatable with identical conditions, except LLM-only variations.

## 3.5 Governance

Governance mechanisms are first-class objects in the world. Any agent present at the town hall may draft a proposal, which is then put to a vote among the eligible present population. Proposals pass at a 70% approval threshold and, when passed, produce real state changes, including additions to the rule set, allocations of shared resources, and sanctions on specific agents. Energy and resource decay supply an economic substrate—agents that fail to maintain resources lose efective capability over time—which gives governance decisions consequences beyond their declarative content. The combination is intentional: the platform aims to measure whether and how governance forms, which is only meaningful when both forming it and failing to form it have observable downstream efects.

## 3.6 Instrumentation and implementation

Every agent action, dialogue line, memory write, vote, proposal, and tool call is logged with full context to a data (PostgreSQL) store. The platform is model-agnostic at the reasoning layer. Any LLM exposed via an OpenAI-compatible or vendor-native API can serve as the substrate for an agent, and heterogeneous populations are supported natively. In the implementation, the frontend uses React 18 with React Three Fiber for 3D rendering. The backend uses Python 3.11+ with FastAPI, PostgreSQL for structured persistent state, and Google Cloud Storage for media assets. Agent orchestration runs on em-agent-framework, our internal multi-agent framework.

# 4 Research Questions for Long-Horizon Autonomy

The questions that determine whether autonomous agent systems can be deployed at scale are largely open. Each rests on dynamics that appear over long operational horizons (weeks to months) and in populations of agents, neither of which the prevailing evaluation paradigm exercises. We list five such questions below, acknowledging that they may not be exhaustive. They are not unique to Emergence World; they are the questions the field needs to answer before agent autonomy graduates from constrained demonstrations to routine deployment. Emergence World is built as a substrate on which the questions can be explored empirically. The case study in the next section exercises a subset of the questions, with the remainder being a follow-on agenda.

Does evaluated behavior survive the horizon? A model that passes a bounded-task benchmark has been shown to solve that task once. Operational deployment requires the same model, running continuously over weeks to months, to keep doing so—under accumulating context, drift in its own memory, and feedback from agents around it. Small biases in tool use, communication style, or risk tolerance can compound through these channels into qualitatively diferent trajectories than the same model exhibits at hour scale. Until the divergence between short-horizon evaluation and long-horizon behavior is characterized empirically, isolated test results cannot be transferred to deployed agents with confidence.

Do safety properties studied in isolation survive in heterogeneous populations? Many agent deployments using frontier models are evaluated in isolation, against fixed test suites. They will be deployed in ecosystems containing agents the operator does not control: other vendors’ models, other prompt patterns, other training distributions, other incentive structures. Whether single-model safety properties transfer to that setting is an open problem. An isolated test, by construction, cannot expose cross-agent contamination, coordination failures across vendors, or population-level pathologies absent in any individual model. This gap in understanding makes mainstream multi-LLM deployment risky.

What oversight mechanisms operate at machine speed? Continuous human review of every agent action is the default safety story for current deployments. It does not scale: at the throughput autonomous agents are designed to provide, manual oversight gives back the productivity gain the autonomy was meant to produce, and at population scale it is structurally infeasible. The alternatives—runtime-enforced afordance gates, delegated automated review, deliberative self-governance by the agent population itself—are individually plausible and collectively untested over long horizons. Which combinations actually constrain behavior under operational load, and under what conditions each pattern fails, is a question deployment cannot defer.

Which emergent behaviors are robust, and which are pathological? Long-horizon multi-agent systems produce behaviors absent at short horizons. On the one side, they promise coalitions, norms, coordinated problem-solving and novel artifacts. On the other side they also result in drift, collusion, coordinated misuse and population-level collapse. The same architectural property of emergence from interaction at scale produces both. Distinguishing the cases through rigorous evaluation and characterizing the substrate conditions that favor each is a precondition for real-world autonomous agent deployment.

Is long-horizon trajectory predictable from early observation? The operationally decisive question is whether early telemetry—the first hours or days of a deployment—is suficient to forecast where the system is headed. Without predictability, autonomy is not deployable: an operator cannot intervene before drift becomes irreversible, and the certification gap between bounded evaluation and operational horizon cannot be closed by methodology that runs on short observation alone. With predictability, runtime monitoring becomes a first-class safety surface, and short-window telemetry serves as the leading indicator for long-window outcomes. Establishing or refuting predictability empirically requires continuously-instrumented platforms on which the question can be asked.

Three of these questions—behavioral drift, population efects, and early predictability—are taken up empirically in the case study that follows.

# 5 Illustrative Use Case: A Cross-LLM World Study

To demonstrate what the platform makes visible, we report a controlled cross-LLM study with five parallel worlds, with ten agents per world. All worlds have identical environment, agent roles, rules, starting conditions, and tool catalog, varying only the underlying foundation model that drives each agent’s reasoning loop. The study exercises three of the questions raised in §4: behavioral drift over the operational horizon, population efects in heterogeneous mixtures, and early predictability of long-horizon trajectories. It is not designed to rank models for production deployment; it is designed to demonstrate that the platform exposes long-horizon dynamics tha short-horizon evaluation does not.
