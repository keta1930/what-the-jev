Consider the humble lamp-post, a seemingly trivial object that evokes distinct sentiments in different cities. In London, it elicits humor; we envision a tipsy gentleman affectionately holding onto it, reminiscing about old friendships. In contrast, the lamp-post in Paris conveys a sense of tragedy, reminding us of tyrants meeting their fate and the culmination of an era. There once existed a bitter Republican publication in Paris named LA LANTERNE, which invites the whimsical thought of a similarly named Progressive journal in England titled THE LAMP POST. This juxtaposition illustrates the essence of the French character; they are the quintessential people of the street, capable of dining and even meeting their end outdoors. Should I wander through Paris and witness someone preparing for bed in the open air, I would take it as a testament to the spirit of their culture. In France, both the noble and the ignoble coexist amidst an outdoor lifestyle. When compared with a café, a public house feels like a confined space.

Moving to the town of Belfort, one can easily understand why such reflections arise among its streets. This town is positioned near the border of France and Germany, and borders are often the most beautiful entities in existence. The act of loving something inherently involves cherishing its limits; children, for instance, are drawn to the edges of all things—they construct castles by the sea and are only deterred from the grassy brink through societal constraints or force. When we encounter the end of a matter, we simultaneously approach the beginning of something new.

Thus, Belfort appears even more distinctly French, situated right on the edge of Germany. Despite the noticeable German influences present—such as German names, larger beer mugs, and overly theatrical barmaids dressed in exaggerated Alsatian attire—the town's inherent French essence becomes more pronounced in the face of these foreign elements. Day and night, one can see weary, sun-kissed soldiers marching through the streets, their demeanor exuding a stubborn disdain. German soldiers project an air of superiority, while French soldiers seem to carry a deeper disdain, directed both at others and themselves. This complexity, perhaps, feeds into the realism that characterizes a nation capable of excelling in war, science, and other pursuits that require the merging of necessity with unpleasant realities. Both soldiers and civilians often sport closely cropped hair and possess a distinctly rugged appearance, which can seem almost harsh to an English observer—referred to as a bullet-head. This nomenclature is not without merit, as the intellectual journey of France can be likened to explosive projectiles, embodying a forceful and transformative essence.
