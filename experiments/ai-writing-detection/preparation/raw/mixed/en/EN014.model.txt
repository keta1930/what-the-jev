As I gaze upon the Belfry, a smile crosses my face as I recall some friends in London who firmly believe that a child's development is entirely determined by what they refer to as “the right environment.” Environment is a complex matter; it can exert an influence that is both beneficial and detrimental, or often, it can fall somewhere in between. A beautiful setting might inspire a child to appreciate beauty, yet it could equally lead to a sense of ennui regarding it. More likely than not, these conflicting outcomes will balance each other out, suggesting that the environment may have little impact overall. In the scientific approach to history, which once enjoyed popularity and still holds a conventional place, we often encountered lists of nations whose traits were attributed to their geographical conditions.

It was commonly asserted that the Spaniards were passionate due to the warm climate of their homeland, while the adventurous nature of Scandinavians was linked to their colder environment. Englishmen were deemed seafaring because they are surrounded by water, and the Swiss were thought to embody freedom because of their mountainous terrain. This reasoning is appealing in its own way. However, I am convinced that I could compile an equally extensive list that challenges these notions and argues against the significance of geographical factors. For instance, Spaniards have explored and discovered more continents than their Scandinavian counterparts, despite the heat of their climate discouraging vigorous activity. Similarly, the Dutch have shown equal bravery in their struggle for freedom, despite lacking mountains, just like the Swiss. Moreover, ancient Greece and Rome, along with various Mediterranean cultures, often expressed a disdain for the sea, even when they possessed the most navigable waters. This list could easily be extended indefinitely.

Nevertheless, should one attempt to determine whether a person will align with or rebel against their environment, it remains uncertain. Thus, I find little solace in believing that contemporary discussions around environment hold much practical significance. Rather than delve further into these modern theories, I prefer to continue my contemplation of the Belfry of Bruges. My interest in these theories may wane, especially considering that I am fairly certain they will fade into obscurity long before the enduring presence of the Belfry itself.
