As I entered the fog of London, I encountered a friend who works at a bank, to whom I shared my poetic suggestions intended for his coworkers. However, he did not seem particularly optimistic about their reception. He clarified that his lack of enthusiasm was not due to any underappreciation of the verses or a critique of their form. Rather, he sensed an elusive quality in the very fabric of our society that renders it spiritually challenging to express oneself musically in a banking environment. I found his perspective compelling, even though it remained somewhat enigmatic. 

In contemplating this, I couldn’t help but think that the Socialists might be miscalculating the roots of our societal woes. They attribute our struggles not to moral failures but to the disarray of private enterprise. Banks, being private institutions, stand in stark contrast to post-offices, which embody a more collectivist, socialistic approach. Thus, I assumed that the post-office would embody the spirit of a collective song. Imagine my astonishment when the woman at my local post-office, whom I encouraged to sing, met my suggestion with even greater indifference than the bank employee had shown. In fact, she appeared to be in a more profound state of gloom than he.

If anyone were to think that the lack of enthusiasm was a reflection of the quality of the verses, they would be mistaken. The sample verse from the Post-Office Hymn went as follows: “O’er London our letters are shaken like snow, Our wires o’er the world like the thunderbolts go. The news that may marry a maiden in Sark, Or kill an old lady in Finsbury Park.” The chorus, filled with zest, declared: “Or kill an old lady in Finsbury Park.” However, the more I pondered this situation, the clearer it became that many quintessentially modern endeavors seem incompatible with a joyful chorus.

For instance, one cannot be a prominent financier while singing, since the essence of financial success often lies in silence and discretion. Similarly, being a public figure in many contemporary contexts requires a certain level of privacy and restraint, which further stifles the potential for song. One would never envision a chorus comprised of bankers; the tale of the solicitors’ volunteer corps illustrates this absurdity—when faced with the command “Charge!” on the battlefield, their collective response was the mundane “Six-and-eightpence.” While soldiers can sing as they charge into battle, such a notion feels almost ludicrous when applied to the legal arena.

Ultimately, my reflections led me back to the underlying sentiment expressed by my banking friend: there is something inherently stifling about our existence—not just concerning our laws but regarding the very essence of life itself. Bank clerks lack songs not due to their economic status, but rather because of a deep-seated sorrow. In contrast, sailors, despite their poverty, often sing heartily. On my way home, I passed a small tin building associated with a religious group, resounding with exuberant shouting. They were singing with abandon, prompting me to momentarily entertain a thought I had often considered: it seems that the realm of the transcendent is the only space where one can truly encounter the essence of humanity. It appears that human nature is pursued relentlessly, retreating into a sanctuary away from the burdens of daily life.
