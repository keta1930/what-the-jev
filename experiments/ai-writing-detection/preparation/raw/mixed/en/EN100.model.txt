While driving in a hansom cab down one of the steep streets leading to the Strand yesterday afternoon, I found myself engrossed in reading one of my own excellent articles, feeling both delight and a sense of wonder. Suddenly, the horse stumbled forward, momentarily losing its footing on the rough stones, but quickly regained its balance and continued moving. This occurrence was not unusual for the horses I encountered in my cabs, and over time, I had grown accustomed to enjoying my writing at any angle of motion the vehicle provided. Therefore, I didn’t find anything particularly strange about the horse's abrupt movement. However, the expressions of the pedestrians on the sidewalk caught my attention; their faces reflected an abrupt wave of fear, as if struck by a sudden, blinding light. One man even dashed into the road, raising his arm as if to fend off an impending blow, attempting to halt the runaway horse. It was then that I realized the reins had slipped away, and in the next instant, the horse surged ahead like a bolt of lightning. I strive to depict events precisely as they unfolded; it’s likely I overlooked or misrepresented certain aspects, and some details may have gone awry in the chaos of our descent down the street. I once referred to one of my experiences in this essay as “A Fragment of Fact,” and while this may also be a mere fragment, it undeniably represents a moment of truth that could not be more fragmented than what I anticipated encountering at the street's end.

I firmly believe in addressing those who are already convinced, as I have often found that they frequently lack a true grasp of their own beliefs. I have consistently asserted in this piece that democracy encompasses a significance that exceeds the comprehension of those who advocate for it; that commonplace sayings and proverbs often harbor deeper meanings that elude most who utter them. Take, for instance, the notion that a person facing imminent peril experiences their entire life flash before their eyes in an instant. In the stark, literal sense, this claim is undeniably a grand exaggeration. No one can honestly assert that, during a crisis or an accident, they meticulously recalled every train ticket they had collected or every time they had served brown bread and butter.

However, during those fleeting moments while my cab careened toward the bustling traffic of the Strand, I unearthed a kernel of truth behind such phrases, as is often the case with popular expressions. In that brief, tumultuous interval, I experienced a rapid succession of profound perspectives. Within a matter of seconds, I felt as though I had traversed through five distinct belief systems. My initial response was a primal form of Paganism, which, among sincere individuals, can be succinctly described as overwhelming fear. This was soon followed by a mindset that, while genuine, lacks a precise label. The ancients referred to it as Stoicism, and it might also align with what some German thinkers convey when they speak of Pessimism. This state was characterized by a stark acceptance of whatever fate might befall, as if I had risen above the significance of the situation. Intriguingly, this was succeeded by a powerful counter-emotion—an awareness that everything held immense significance, yet also transcended tragedy. It was not that life was trivial; rather, it was the realization that life was far too vital to ever be anything less than life itself. I hope this revelation reflected a Christian understanding, as it emerged just at the moment we collided with the omnibus.
