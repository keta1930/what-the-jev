In my search for a suitable space to sketch, I encountered a moment of clarity during an extended period spent lying in bed. As I reclined, the brilliance of that expansive white light flooded my senses, a vision that seemed to embody the essence of Paradise, symbolizing both purity and liberation. However, as is often the case with such ethereal visions, its allure proved to be deceptive and remote; it appeared more severe and further away than the azure sky visible through my window. Despite my whimsical notion of painting upon this celestial canvas using the coarse bristles of a broom—a suggestion that was met with disapproval by an individual lacking any political influence—my even simpler idea of turning the broom's other end into charcoal for artistic purposes was also rejected. It is my conviction that it is individuals in circumstances like mine who inspired the original artists to adorn the ceilings of grand palaces and cathedrals with chaotic scenes of fallen angels and triumphant deities. I am convinced that it was only due to Michelangelo's own indulgence in the venerable activity of lying in bed that he envisaged how to transform the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel into a striking representation of a divine narrative fit for the heavens.

The contemporary view of the act of lying in bed is fraught with hypocrisy and an unhealthy mindset. Among the various indicators of modernity that signal a decline in societal values, none are as alarming as the undue emphasis placed on trivial behavioral norms at the expense of significant moral principles and deep human connections. If there exists a greater threat than the erosion of core ethics, it is the bolstering of inconsequential morals. Society seems to find it more severe to label someone as lacking in taste than to accuse them of ethical failings. The idea that "cleanliness is next to godliness" appears to have reversed, with cleanliness deemed paramount while genuine moral values are treated as an inconvenience. A playwright may critique the institution of marriage, provided he adheres to social conventions, and I have encountered Ibsen-inspired pessimists who argue against consuming beer while endorsing the ingestion of prussic acid. This trend is particularly evident in discussions surrounding hygiene, especially regarding the act of lying in bed. Rather than being perceived as a personal matter of comfort and preference, many now view it as a moral obligation to rise early each day. While adhering to this practice can indeed reflect practical wisdom, neither it nor its contrary holds intrinsic moral value.
