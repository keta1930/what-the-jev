It is surprising to encounter individuals who genuinely believe that fairy tales are detrimental to children. I do not refer to the man in the green tie, as I cannot regard him as truly human. However, a woman recently sent me a heartfelt letter asserting that fairy tales should not be presented to children, no matter their veracity. She argues that such stories are cruel as they instill fear. This perspective is akin to claiming that it is unkind to provide girls with sentimental novels simply because they evoke tears. Such sentiments stem from a profound misunderstanding of the child’s nature, a misunderstanding that has underpinned numerous educational theories. If children are shielded from goblins and bogeymen, they will only conjure them from their own imaginations. A single child, left in the dark, can fabricate more terrors than a seasoned writer might envision. The capacity of a young mind to envision monstrous creatures is boundless—these imagined beings can easily defy the limits of any illustration and possess names too bizarre and discordant to have ever been uttered by the most deranged individual. Children are drawn to horrors, often immersing themselves in them even when they find them unsettling. Identifying the precise moment when pure pain begins for them is as complex as it is for adults who willingly enter the depths of a tragic narrative. The source of fear is not fairy tales; rather, it emerges from the vastness of the human soul.

The apprehension experienced by children, much like that of primitive peoples, is entirely rational; they are frightened by their surroundings because the world can be a terrifying place. The discomfort of solitude is entirely justified, as the thought of being alone can be genuinely daunting. Just as barbarians fear the unknown, Agnostics revere it—this fear is rooted in reality. Consequently, fairy tales should not be blamed for instilling fear or its many manifestations in children; rather, these stories do not introduce the concepts of evil or ugliness, which already exist in the child, as they reflect the nature of the world itself. Fairy tales do not create the child’s initial notion of monsters. Instead, what these tales offer is a clear vision of the potential triumph over those fears. From the moment a child begins to imagine, they are well-acquainted with the dragon. What fairy tales furnish is a heroic figure, akin to St. George, who can vanquish that dragon.
