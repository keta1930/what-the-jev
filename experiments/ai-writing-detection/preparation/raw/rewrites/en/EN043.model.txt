On the first day of May, I found myself seated outside a café in Place de la Bastille in Paris, gazing at the triumphant column topped with a lively figure. This monument commemorates the spot where the populace dismantled a prison and ushered in a new era. It serves as a fascinating illustration of how much of human history is steeped in symbolism. In terms of physical reality, the Bastille was hardly a dreadful prison when it fell; it was barely a prison at all. Yet, it became an emblem, and people possess an innate knack for recognizing symbols. For example, during the last General Election, the figure of the Chinaman or President Kruger’s hat in the preceding election showcased this instinct. The Chinaman, with his distinctive pigtail, embodies precisely what the people resented about African policy—the foreign and bizarre nature of wealth, the unsettling truth that money lacks roots, that it operates not as a natural force but rather as an insidious magic that summons monsters from distant lands. The populace harbored a deep-seated aversion towards the mine owner who could effortlessly transport a Chinaman across oceans, much like they detested the sorcerer who could conjure a flying dragon. Similarly, Mr. Kruger’s hat was not merely a joke; it symbolized precisely what stirred impatience and anger among the people—a drab, archaic, Republican simplicity, the unappealing dignity of the bourgeoisie, and the weighty platitudes of political morality. While people may sometimes err in practical political matters, they are seldom mistaken when it comes to the artistic realm.

This phenomenon undoubtedly applied to the Bastille as well. The act of demolishing the Bastille transcended mere reform; it represented a more profound iconoclasm, the shattering of a stone idol. To the people, the structure loomed like a giant with countless eyes, and they struck at it as if it were a carved reality. Of all the various manifestations of the enormous illusion known as materialism that can terrify the human spirit, few are as stifling as imposing edifices. Individuals can feel insignificant, like a mere fly in the web of something they themselves have constructed. It takes a monumental effort of the spirit to remember that humans created this daunting structure and that they possess the power to dismantle it. Thus, the very action of the ragged citizens in the streets taking down a colossal public building carries a spiritual, almost ritualistic significance that far exceeds its immediate political implications. It resembles a sacred observance. For instance, if Socialists were bold enough to seize and demolish the Bank of England, one could endlessly debate the futility of such an act and how it may not directly address the core economic issues. Nonetheless, humanity would not forget it; such an event would alter the course of history.

Architecture serves as a reliable gauge of the true resilience of a society since the most treasured aspects of a community are those that are irrevocable—marriage, for instance. Architecture comes closer than any other art form to achieving this permanence, as it is remarkably challenging to dismantle. One can easily turn a painting to face the wall, but it would be a considerable nuisance to rotate a Roman cathedral out of sight. A poem can be ripped to shreds; however, it is only in moments of profound emotion that one would consider demolishing a town hall. Buildings, akin to dogmas, assert their permanence; they are as audacious as any religious belief. People often ponder why modern society lacks a distinctive architectural style akin to Impressionism in painting. The answer is evident: we simply do not possess enough dogmas; we are unable to accept anything in the heavens that is solid and enduring, anything that does not shift like the clouds. Yet, the decision to erect a building involves a similar resolution to that of razing one. The two actions are intrinsically linked. Few cities have witnessed the construction of as many remarkable public buildings as Paris, nor have so many been dismantled. When people fall into the unfortunate habit of preserving structures, they often lose the inclination to create new ones. In London, one finds oneself mingling grief with the joy of witnessing so few buildings being torn down.

As I continued to contemplate the column of the Bastille, adorned with the inscriptions of Liberty and Glory, a sudden and silent procession of horsemen emerged from a corner of the square, which, like many such public spaces, felt simultaneously bustling yet tranquil. Their uniforms were a dull blue, quite unremarkable, yet the sunlight ignited the brass and steel of their helmets, intricately designed in the fashion of Roman headgear. I had encountered them in pairs before and had seen countless depictions of them navigating the snows of Friedland or charging through the squares at Waterloo. But now, they appeared in organized ranks, a seemingly overwhelming force, and something about their numbers, coupled with the evening light illuminating their faces and crests, compelled me to rise and exclaim, “The French soldiers!” These were the small men with the sun-kissed faces who had so frequently traversed the capitals of Europe with the same ease they now displayed in their homeland. Glancing across the square, I observed that the opposite corners were filled with clusters of infantry clad in blue and red; the city was fortified against a potential uprising.

Naturally, I was aware of the ongoing strike, primarily from a baker who shared his thoughts with me. He expressed that he was not going to “Chomer.” When I inquired, “What is chome?” he explained, “They do not want to work.” I replied, “Neither do I,” which led him to perceive me as a class-conscious, collectivist member of the proletariat. The situation was intriguing, and the underlying lesson from it was not easily grasped by us as a nation, as our own shortcomings lie in an entirely different direction. As an Englishman, deeply rooted in the optimism and aversion to harshness characteristic of my culture, I perceived the whole affair as a fuss over trivialities. It appeared to me as though a top-tier army in Europe was being mobilized against ordinary citizens merely going about their day. The cavalry charged at us a couple of times, but to little effect. Yet, the challenge lies in recognizing how far my criticisms might stem from an erroneous assumption of the French populace being as compliant as the English. However, the more profound essence of the situation reverberated through the clamor of that vibrant night. This nation possesses an inherent ability to sense when it stands on the brink of something significant—be it the Bartholomew, the Revolution, the Commune, or even Judgment Day. This acute awareness of impending crisis is what keeps France eternally youthful. The nation is in a constant state of construction and destruction, just as it tore down the Bastille to erect the column in its place. In this perpetual cycle, France has discovered its unique method of achieving immortality; it dies daily yet rises anew.
