"We have all encountered that individual who recounts peculiar experiences yet remains skeptical about their supernatural nature. My stance, however, is quite the contrary. I hold a belief in the supernatural grounded in logic and intellect rather than personal encounters. I do not perceive ghosts; instead, I recognize their inherent likelihood. This conviction stems solely from a rational perspective, disconnected from physical sensations; my body and nerves remain firmly rooted in the tangible world. Nonetheless, those with a similar disposition can often find a single bizarre occurrence leaving an indelible mark. The strangest event I have ever encountered took place not long ago, which involved nothing less than my involvement in a game, and to my utter surprise, I played it quite proficiently for a continuous stretch of seventeen minutes. The appearance of my grandfather’s ghost would have been less astonishing.

On one particularly bright and sweltering afternoon, I discovered, to my utter disbelief, that I was engaged in a game of croquet. I had always thought of it as a pastime from the era of Leach and Anthony Trollope, and I had neglected to don the long, elaborate sideburns that seemed essential for such an occasion. I played alongside a gentleman I shall refer to as Parkinson, with whom I engaged in a semi-philosophical debate that persisted throughout our match. I am quite convinced that I held the upper hand in the discussion; nonetheless, it is undeniable that I performed poorly in the game itself.

“Oh, Parkinson, Parkinson!” I exclaimed, affectionately tapping him on the head with my mallet, “how distant you truly are from the genuine spirit of the game—you, who can play it so well. It is only those of us who play ineptly who genuinely cherish the Game itself. You revel in glory; you seek applause; you yearn for the resounding triumph of victory; you do not truly love croquet. True appreciation for croquet emerges only when one embraces the notion of being defeated in croquet. It is we, the bunglers, who celebrate the activity in its purest form. We find ourselves enamored by the art for art’s sake. If we were to glimpse the visage of Croquet herself (if I may be so bold), we would be satisfied to see her countenance directed toward us in displeasure. Our performances may be deemed amateurish, yet we proudly wear the title of amateur, for ‘amateur’ is simply the French term for ‘lover.’ We welcome all adventures from our beloved Lady, be they disastrous or dreary. We linger outside her iron gates (referring to the hoops), attempting in vain to enter. Our devoted balls, brimming with zeal and chivalry, refuse to be confined to the rigid parameters of the croquet court. They yearn for glory across distant lands; they turn up in flowerbeds and conservatories, appearing in front gardens and even in the neighboring street. No, Parkinson! The skilled painter possesses technique. It is the unskilled painter who cherishes his art. The accomplished musician finds joy in being a musician, while the less adept musician adores music itself. With such an earnest and unyielding passion do I revere croquet. I cherish the game itself. I adore the grassy parallelogram outlined with chalk or tape, as if its borders represent the sacred frontiers of my homeland, the four seas of Britain. I relish the simple swing of the mallets, and the sound of the balls colliding is symphonic to my ears. The four colors evoke in me a sense of the sacramental and the symbolic, akin to the red of martyrdom or the white of Easter Sunday. You miss all of this, dear Parkinson. You must find solace in the meager comfort of being able to navigate hoops and strike the stick."
