"Recently, I found myself in a surprising situation: I managed to catch a train heading towards the Eastern Counties at the last moment. As I hurried alongside the train, I was struck by the sight of an unusually high number of carriages marked “Engaged.” In fact, five, six, seven, eight, or even nine carriages bore this sign, and at just as many windows, I could see large, unyielding men gazing out with an unmistakable air of pride. Their bodies appeared particularly solid, and their faces wore a serene expression. It was not the Derby, as it was both the wrong day and heading in the opposite direction. It couldn’t possibly be the King or the French President either, as these notable figures, while they do prefer privacy during their travels, still attract public attention for a few moments. Here, however, there was no throng of onlookers or any ceremonial presence from the police.

I pondered the identity of these peculiar individuals who occupied more of the train than a lively gathering yet seemed to embody a rarefied elegance surpassing even that of the King’s entourage. Who were these figures, larger than a mere crowd yet more enigmatic than royalty? Could it be that instead of our Royal House visiting the Tsar, the Tsar was, in fact, visiting us? Or perhaps the House of Lords had a morning meal to attend? My musings continued until the train came to a halt at a station heading toward Cambridge. At that point, the large, impenetrable men disembarked, followed by those seated in the engaged compartments. They all wore coordinated attire, sported neatly trimmed hair, and were linked together by chains.

As I glanced across the carriage, my eyes met those of another passenger, a small, weary-looking man who, as I later discovered, hailed from Cambridge. He appeared to be a local tradesman, possibly a journeyman tailor or a clock repairer. In an effort to spark a conversation, I expressed my curiosity about the destination of the convicts. A smirk crossed his face, revealing the irony that often accompanies hardship, and he replied, “I don’t s’pose they’re goin’ on an ’oliday at the seaside with little spades and pails.” I found his wit delightful and, continuing the playful banter, I speculated that perhaps the university dons were being transported to Cambridge in chains as well. Given his familiarity with several dons, he appreciated the jest. However, our laughter soon faded into silence, and I noticed the little man’s bleak, grey eyes grow more sorrowful, reflecting an emptiness akin to the open sea. I understood his thoughts, as they mirrored my own; amidst all our cleverness, we were still mere mortals. Finally, as if concluding a melodic phrase, he remarked, “Well, I s’pose we ’ave to do it.” In this exchange—his initial jest, our ensuing silence, and his final statement—lay the essence of English democracy: a profound sense of humor, an enduring sense of pathos, and an overarching sense of helplessness."
