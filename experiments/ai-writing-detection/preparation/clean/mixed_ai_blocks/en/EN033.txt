A few weeks ago, I relocated to a new house, and naturally, my collection of books accompanied me. Muscular, sweating movers loaded them into boxes and transported them to the van, grumbling about Caxton as they went. Upon reaching my new home, they carried the boxes into the designated library space, removed the lids, and awaited my instructions. The immediate task was to clear the floor, so we quickly transferred the books to the pristine white shelves that awaited their arrival. The specific arrangement of the volumes was not a concern at that moment; the priority was simply to elevate them off the ground. We piled them up, one armful after another, only pausing when the foreman unexpectedly noticed my name on one of the spines. “Did you write this one, sir?” he inquired. I replied affirmatively. “H’m,” he responded with a nonchalant tone, proceeding to inspect the titles in each load. He seemed slightly astonished by the number of books that were not authored by me, revealing the laid-back nature of my profession.

Eventually, the books found their way onto the shelves, where they currently remain. I promised myself that I would organize them properly on a rainy afternoon. However, when that soggy day finally arrived, I convinced myself that I would tackle the task during one of the upcoming mornings. As it stands, I now find myself scanning each shelf in search of the desired volume. Just because I stumble upon Keats does not guarantee that I am any closer to Shelley. If Shelley hasn’t been misplaced along the way, he is likely wedged between How to Be a Golfer Though Middle-aged and another title.

Having written this much, I felt compelled to rise and discover Shelley’s true location. To my dismay, he’s nestled between Geometrical Optics and Studies in New Zealand Scenery. Ella Wheeler Wilcox, whom I seem to have been unwittingly entertaining, is positioned next to Anarchy or Order, which was evidently “sent in the hope that you will become a member of the Duty and Discipline Movement”—a futile hope, it appears, since I have yet to pay my subscription. What I Found Out, penned by an English Governess, shares shelf space with The Recreations of a Country Parson, followed by Villette and Baedeker's Switzerland. Clearly, something must be done to rectify this chaos. Yet, I find myself pondering what that “something” might entail. If I gave the impression that my books were meticulously organized in their previous arrangement, I must confess I was mistaken. They were lined up in what can only be described as “all anyhow.” Perhaps they were slightly less “anyhow” than they are now, as copies of the same work were at least grouped together back then, but that is the extent of their order. For years, I have postponed the task of straightening them out, just as I am doing now. This delay isn’t due to sloth; rather, it stems from my uncertainty about where to begin.
