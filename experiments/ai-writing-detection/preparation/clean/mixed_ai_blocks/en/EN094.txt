How do rumors begin to circulate? There are numerous avenues through which they can spread. To illustrate this, I would like to reference a recent critique of the war by Mr. Belloc, who remarked:  

"The official German communiqué which appeared in print last Saturday is a very good example upon which to work. I quote it as it appeared in the Westminster Gazette (which has from the beginning of the war, and even before its outbreak, been remarkable for the volume of its German information), and as it was delivered through the Marconi channel."  

Following this statement is the communiqué itself. Upon reading it, I couldn't help but chuckle at the cleverness of Mr. Belloc. He cites a document that was published in every major newspaper across the nation, yet he chooses to specifically mention the Westminster Gazette. The reason for this selective citation? It is clearly to make that particular remark I have italicized.  

The standing of the Westminster Gazette is too esteemed to be tarnished by the insinuation that it is "remarkable" for its German insights—an assertion that is, in fact, unfounded. However, consider the hypothetical situation where you, an average citizen, are rumored to have special connections to Germany during these times. Even if you are as innocent as a Suffolk schoolmaster, such claims could lead to suspicion from your community and perhaps even scrutiny from law enforcement.  

To provide another example, a friend of mine, a journalist aligned with the Liberal viewpoint, attended a private dinner recently where he met M. Painlevé, the French Academician, Senator Lafontaine from Brussels, and a couple of other deputies from France and Belgium. The next day, the Daily Express (under the editorship of Mr. Blumenfeld) reported that he dined with "three or four foreigners" to discuss peace. Subsequently, the London Mail inquired, "Who were the foreigners with whom ——— dined?" This illustrates the insinuations at play and how rumors can morph and grow. He chose not to respond, as some publications are simply not worth one’s attention. Yet, I share this anecdote to highlight how legends are born.  

What can we learn from this? It echoes the wisdom of my quoted friend. We should approach all rumors—whether about events or individuals—with skepticism. When Napoleon’s marshals claimed victory, he famously replied, "Show me your prisoners." Similarly, when confronted with a rumor, resist the urge to accept it without question. Instead, ask, "Show me your facts." And before you accept those facts, ensure they are complete and not mere fragments of truth.
