Cities established, the challenge arose: how to illuminate them. How could citizens navigate their neighborhoods when the sky had drawn back its primary source of light? Or, to phrase it in a more modern context, how could they find their way when the Earth had turned its back on the sun? The moon occasionally offered assistance, while the stars twinkled cheerfully above the rooftops. In addition, the occasional cresset perched on a church or fortress created an appealing visual display, guiding those who found themselves in uneven terrain. Yet, when the sun, moon, and stars were obscured, the nighttime dweller had no choice but to rely—according to historical illustrations—on towering lanterns, standing two stories tall. The conical turret of this wandering lighthouse featured numerous holes that projected beams of light into the eyes of its bearer. As he ventured out into the eerie darkness, this individual carried his own miniature sun, which swung back and forth as he walked. Shadows loomed around him, and as the curfew rang out, he was left with no illumination other than that which he brought into the township.

Following the age of these portable lanterns, which flickered in a world overshadowed by darkness, came the period of oil lamps. These were notoriously difficult to ignite and easily snuffed out, casting a pale, flickering light during their brief existence. The winds howled, playful youths would extinguish them with ease, and before long, the night reclaimed its oppressive domain, leaving the citizens to navigate their surroundings blindly, with nothing but the filth of the streets beneath their feet. To add to the chaos, it became customary to suspend these fragile lights from one house to another across the thoroughfare. They swung precariously on invisible cords, and if a long-necked general happened to gallop by on a spirited steed, hurrying to alter the course of history, the scene could quickly turn disastrous—with military chaos, shattered glass, and a return to complete darkness that engulfed the street, leaving it abandoned and bewildering.

Meanwhile, the conservative observer, reflecting on both the past and the present, finds solace in this duality. From the era of gas lighting, he dismisses the dimness and flickers that once defined his ancestors' experience, feeling a swell of joy at the stark contrast with contemporary illumination. His heart swells with pride for progress, and he cannot help but sing praises in lofty verses, celebrating the advancements made. With the advent of gas lamps illuminating the city at dusk, a new chapter for communal life and enjoyment commenced, marking a significant transformation that felt inherent to the progress of civilization. Prometheus's legacy had taken another leap forward; humanity and its social gatherings were no longer at the mercy of distant fogs. With the sun setting, promenades remained lively, and the day could stretch to accommodate every individual's desires. The urban dwellers possessed their own stars—reliable, domesticated sources of light.
