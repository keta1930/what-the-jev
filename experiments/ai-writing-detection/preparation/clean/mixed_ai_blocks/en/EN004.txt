In the woods on Midsummer Day, enchantment abounds, or so the stories go. Titania revels in her festivities, and while many may choose to join her court, I must politely decline. My spirit lacks the joyfulness typically associated with such celebrations. On any other holiday, I would gladly partake in merriment, but on this longest day of the year, I feel a heavy weight on my heart, for it signals the gradual onset of shorter evenings. Winter is on the horizon.

Around Midsummer Day, the cuckoo alters its call, aware that the pinnacle of summer has passed and that soon it must take its leave. I would prefer to write an in-depth piece on "The Habits of the Cuckoo," yet I suspect that by the end, my affection for the creature might diminish. It is more beneficial to recognize just one intriguing aspect of its behavior: the cuckoo's tendency to lay its eggs in the nests of other birds—a rather charming notion. Beyond this, it is best to appreciate the cuckoo for what it is, embracing its most significant trait: its joyous cry of "Cuckoo."

The nightingale embodies sorrow, while the thrush heralds promising times ahead with its unsettling melodies. The blackbird offers a crisp, refreshing tune that pairs perfectly with a frosty February dawn, and the skylark ascends into the skies, singing a tune that seems unattainable for humanity. As for the lesser white-throat, its song remains a mystery to me. Nevertheless, the cuckoo represents the joys of the present; it accompanies us during summer's leisure, its song resonating beneath the sun in a vibrant landscape of blue and green. I believe that only those filled with happiness can truly hear its call. The cuckoo graces us during moments of delight, remaining silent when clouds obscure the sun, or if it does sing then, it whispers gently to preserve its melody. When it finally proclaims "Cuckoo!" aloud, one can be assured that warmth and brightness have returned.
