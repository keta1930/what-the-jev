Reflect once more on the Richard, or the "dicky." Is there anything more perfectly stylish, or more genuinely practical? Yet, despite its merits, neither you nor I would consider donning one. I recall a distressing scenario from a tale penned by Mr. W. S. Jackson. The protagonist found himself stranded in a foreign hotel, his luggage nowhere in sight. To his dismay, the girl he secretly admired arrived at the same hotel with her father. After receiving an invitation to join them for dinner, he resorted to borrowing whatever garments he could from accommodating waitstaff, which regrettably included a dicky. The evening commenced splendidly; our hero made a wonderful impression, and the atmosphere was cheerful. However, calamity struck when a candle tipped over, igniting the heroine's dress. In that critical moment, the hero instinctively understood the need for action. He knew that gallant heroes typically remove their coats to shield the burning damsel. He sprang up like a shot (or whatever moves the fastest) and — then he remembered.

He was wearing a dicky! Without his coat, he would inevitably reveal this secret to the one person he desperately wanted to keep it from. Yet, if he chose to keep his coat on, the girl could meet a tragic fate. It was an excruciating predicament. I cannot recall which option he ultimately chose, but I suspect you and I would have safeguarded the secret of the Richard at all costs. In truth, what is inherently wrong with a faux shirt-front? The only issue is that it reveals the financial limitations of the wearer. We, who can afford a new straw hat daily, are not burdened by laundry expenses; however, the mere suspicion of financial struggle is daunting.

Our gentlemanly disdain for the pre-tied tie seems to stem from an entirely different rationale; I find the psychology behind it somewhat perplexing. Certainly, it is a form of deception, but such deception only acquires significance when it masquerades as something of actual importance. No one considers a self-tied tie to be of consequence; no one genuinely boasts about their ability to fashion a cravat from a strip of silk. Perhaps it is merely the fact that a pre-tied tie saves time that damns it; the safety razor faced similar scorn for that very reason. We, belonging to the more privileged classes, can indulge in hours of grooming; thus, we are all too eager to look down upon those who cannot.
