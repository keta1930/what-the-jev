We often engage in a game of hide-and-seek with our own moral compass. Simply avoiding detection by others is insufficient; we also evade self-recognition. Even those individuals who appear to lead impeccable lives can be burdened with their own set of ethical contradictions. A notable example is a well-respected preacher who was discovered deceased in a first-class train compartment, yet he had a third-class ticket in his possession.

When it comes to literature, who truly maintains their integrity? I recall a few years back when the collection of a renowned theologian and literary analyst was auctioned off following his passing. His library was a magnificent assortment of rare volumes, primarily focused on seventeenth-century authors, a field in which he held significant expertise. Numerous titles bore the marks of libraries from across the nation, revealing that he had borrowed them and never found the right moment to return them. These books clung to him as if they were legal precedents. Despite being a devout individual who delivered inspiring sermons—a fact I can personally attest to—he struggled to part with the volumes he cherished.

In fact, the most sensible approach to lending books was exemplified by a man who, when asked to lend a particular title, replied, “I’m sorry, but I can’t.” When his friend pressed him, inquiring if he owned it, he confirmed, “Yes, I've got it, but I have a rule against lending books. From my own experience, no one ever brings them back.” He then invited his friend to see his library. “Look,” he said, “four thousand volumes. Every—single—one—of—'em—borrowed.” Thus, the lesson is clear: never lend books. Even your closest friends may not return them. I know this too well. Where have those volumes of Gil Blas gone? And that Silvio Pellico? Yet, why go on? He understands. HE KNOWS.
