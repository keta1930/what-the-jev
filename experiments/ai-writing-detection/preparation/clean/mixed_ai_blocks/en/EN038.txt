"It appears to be a natural principle," he elaborated, "that two identical faces cannot coexist simultaneously among contemporaries. This is a fundamental truth. Odd as it may seem, despite the countless faces that feature two eyes, a centrally positioned nose, and a mouth below, no two are exactly alike. There are always subtle variations." (At this point, he was genuinely relishing the rhythm of his own speech.) "This holds true, as I mentioned, for those living at the same time: in the present moment I am describing. However," he added, "I fail to see any reason why, after numerous years have passed, Nature could not begin to reproduce faces with precise accuracy, thereby alleviating herself of the perpetual need to create diversity. Given this premise, which I believe is not overly imaginative"—his companion interjected, "Not at all—oh no!"—"if this is the case, then why," the artist continued, "shouldn't there be someone today, more than a century later, whose likeness to Nelson is indistinguishable? This individual need not necessarily belong to the naval profession—indeed, it is likely that he wouldn't, as Nelson's features weren't particularly emblematic of a sailor—but regardless of his identity, even if he were an archbishop, I," declared the painter with conviction, "would have no reservations about approaching him to request a sitting."

The friend concurred that this stance was entirely appropriate and indicative of genuine intent.

"Nelson's visage," the painter expounded, "was quite unique. Such a large and expressive mouth is indeed a rarity. Nevertheless, it is entirely plausible that a duplicate exists, and irrespective of who possesses it, even if he were an archbishop, I would not hesitate to approach him and invite him to pose for me."
