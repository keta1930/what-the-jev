I find myself seated beneath towering trees, where a powerful wind churns around their tops, creating a tumultuous sound that embodies both joy and suffering. It strikes me that I am akin to a mariner lying at the ocean's depths, surrounded by nothing but anchors and ropes, while the eternal rhythm of waves crashes above, accompanied by the labor and destruction of massive vessels.

The wind pulls at the trees with such force that it seems capable of uprooting them as effortlessly as one might pull up a handful of grass. To convey this indescribable energy through another metaphor, I envision the trees as a fierce assembly of dragons, each tethered by its tail and writhing in tumult.

As I observe these towering sentinels being buffeted by an unseen, tempestuous force, a memory flickers in my mind. I recall a young boy whom I once encountered, strolling through Battersea Park under similarly turbulent skies and swaying trees. He was decidedly displeased with the wind; it assaulted his face relentlessly, causing him to squint and even dislodging his cherished hat. To the best of my recollection, he was around four years old. After numerous complaints regarding the chaotic weather, he finally turned to his mother and asked, “Well, why don’t you take away the trees, and then it wouldn’t wind?”

This reasoning is both astute and completely understandable. A newcomer to the scene might easily perceive the trees as colossal fans, agitating the air for miles merely through their movement. It is a completely logical and forgivable notion to assume that the trees are the source of the wind. In fact, this belief is so relatable and justifiable that it reflects the views of approximately ninety-nine out of a hundred philosophers, reformers, sociologists, and politicians of our contemporary era. My young friend, in truth, bore a striking resemblance to these prominent modern thinkers—only with a more charming disposition.

In the modest fable or parable that he inadvertently created, the trees symbolize all that is tangible, while the wind represents the unseen forces. The wind embodies the spirit that moves freely; the trees represent the material world that bends to the will of this spirit. Wind signifies aspects like philosophy, religion, and revolution, while trees denote cities and entire civilizations. We become aware of the wind's existence when we witness the trees on a distant hillside suddenly erupting in frenzied motion. Similarly, we recognize a genuine revolution only when we observe the chimney pots across the urban skyline going into a frenzy.
