"Monday.—I woke up at nine and found a letter from Mary waiting for me. It's astonishing how little we understand about those we consider true friends. Behind a façade of kindness, there can often lie the hidden sting of jealousy. Mary mentioned that she wouldn’t be able to contribute anything for my stall at the bazaar since she has her own stall to manage. I ate my breakfast in a daze, my mind elsewhere. What is the essence of life, really? I pondered deeply about the inner universe until lunch. Afterward, I took an hour to rest and clear my thoughts. This morning, I felt a surge of anger towards Mary. How trivial that seems! Will I ever break free from my inherent tendencies? Will my higher self ever achieve the noble heights of selflessness of which it is capable? At four, I sat down and wrote to Mary, offering my forgiveness. It has turned out to be a remarkable day for my spirit."

Indeed, it seems that many diaries recount the journeys of the mind and spirit, often in the absence of exciting physical exploits. If a person cannot write, "Today, I was attacked by a lion on Bond Street," they might still express, "Today, I wrestled with doubt in St. Paul's Cathedral." While some might prefer to remain silent rather than document anything less than thrilling—like an unpleasant encounter with a hairdresser—there are others who feel compelled to write, finding that their emotional experiences are the only truly unique stories they have to share.

Yet, there is always a flicker of hope among diary keepers that their entries might one day be unveiled to the public. Future generations may stumble upon them, surprised by the everyday lives of those living in the twentieth century, or the next cohort might demand to learn about the inner thoughts of a recently deceased prominent figure. Ideally, these reflections might even be disclosed by the writers themselves in their autobiographies.

Thus, the diarist inevitably keeps in mind the prospect of writing an autobiography. "I recall," they may pen in that monumental project, conveniently forgetting the details, "I distinctly remember"—and here they will reference their diary—"having lunch with X on a Sunday and saying to him…"

What they said may not hold significant weight, but it will certainly showcase the impressive memory that the esteemed author retains in their later years.
