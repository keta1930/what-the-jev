Consider, for instance, the distinguished politician, Mr. Sutherland Bangs, M.P., encountering himself at a dinner gathering one evening. Mr. Bangs holds a favorable view of himself as a charming and attractive individual, possessing a knack for conversation, a lighthearted demeanor, a fashionable appearance, and a refined accent. However, as he sits next to his own reflection during the meal, he would come to the disheartening realization that he is, in fact, a tiresome and pretentious individual, with his conversations filled with banalities, his lightheartedness perceived as insufferable, and his polished accent seen as an unwelcome affectation. He would likely declare his desire to never encounter that version of himself again. Understanding that this reflection is how he is perceived by others, he would return home feeling as humiliated and defeated as Mr. Tom Lofty in The Good-Natured Man did when his deception was unveiled. "You ought to have your head stuck in a pillory," remarks Mr. Croaker. To which Mr. Lofty replies, "Stick it where you will, for by the lord, it cuts a poor figure where it sticks at present." Mr. Bangs would resonate with that sentiment.

While the experience of meeting our own selves might bring a mix of surprise and discomfort, it could also serve a beneficial purpose for most of us. The poet Robert Burns articulated this notion poignantly in his well-known lines: 

O wad some pow'r the giftie gie us To see oursels as others see us: It wad frae monie a blunder free us An' foolish notion.

Each of us would likely uncover insights that could be advantageous, even if they are initially disconcerting. You, for instance, might discover that the argumentative talent you pride yourself on is simply viewed by me as frustrating obstinacy, while I might realize that the cleverness I believe I display merely leaves you exasperated. Jones might find his eyeglass falling from his eye upon recognizing how foolish it makes him appear, while Brown could become aware of the unattractiveness of his preachy demeanor, and Robinson might regret his bullheaded nature and prickly attitude. Gaining this objective perspective on ourselves could prove to be greatly beneficial.

However, this perspective is not necessarily the most accurate or comprehensive view. Recall the clever notion presented by Holmes regarding John and Thomas. They are conversing but struggling to connect, and Holmes notes that it is hardly surprising since there are actually six individuals engaged in this dialogue. "Six!" you might exclaim in disbelief. Indeed, he clarifies, there is John’s ideal self—how John perceives himself; there is Thomas’s ideal John—how Thomas views him; and then there is the authentic John, who is known only to his Creator. The same applies to Thomas, as there are three versions of him participating in the exchange as well. John’s ideal self bears no resemblance to Thomas’s ideal version, nor do either of them reflect the true John. This disconnect leads John and Thomas—or, in essence, you and I—to misunderstand one another.
