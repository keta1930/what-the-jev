There comes a distinct, urgent knock at my front door; a knock that carries a hint of nervous hesitation, as if the person outside feels slightly apprehensive about their intrusion and might bolt at any moment. I glance at the clock, which reads four-forty. One of the peculiar habits acquired from my long tenure as a senior assistant at sea is the tendency to wake up around 4 A.M. This oddity grants me several hours, when I’m on land, to reflect on my shortcomings, vices, and, less frequently, my successes and virtues. A man who rises at four-thirty is often met with disdain on shore. Family members express their opinions with exaggerated fervor, insisting he remain still and contemplate or face the embarrassment of inquiries about his next departure.

Yet this morning, in these venerable chambers of a historic inn nestled in the heart of London, I have committed to rising and venturing out. The catalyst for this significant deviation from a life of temporary but conscious leisure is a woman. "Cherchez la femme," as the French express with their trademark logical wit. Fortunately, she is not hard to find, as she stands just outside my solid oak door, tapping with an insistence that is both sharp and delicate. To this romantic call, I respond with a gruff acknowledgment before preparing myself. To ease any concerns from readers imagining an imminent elopement, it should be clearly stated that we are not embarking on any such wild escapade. Instead, we are headed just a few blocks up the Strand to Covent Garden Market to witness the arrival of fresh produce for the city.

After hastily dressing, a routine almost as basic as that of a sailor getting ready for watch, and soothing the occasional repetition of the tapping with brief assurances of my progress, I take my hat and cane. Drawing back the heavy, ornate bolts of my door, I find a young woman looking out the window at the inn's courtyard. She is a decisive character, constantly brainstorming what she terms "stunts" for her articles. This is her profession, or one of them—writing for the press. The other involves selling manuscripts, which creates a special connection between us. For the customary agent's fee, she is representing one of my works. Being an unattached and somewhat unguarded male, she organizes little outings around London to keep me entertained and informed. Today, she is dressed in the vibrant attire of a London flower girl, preparing to gather material for a feature in a morning newspaper. Apart from the eager glint in her bright black Irish eyes, she exudes a sense of professionalism. As we comment on the beauty of an early summer morning in the city, we descend and, passing beneath the imposing ancient archway, we stroll leisurely westward along the Strand.
