My acquaintance, Mr. G.W.E. Russell, proposes a theory suggesting that the perceived advantage of the blind over the deaf and mute regarding cheerfulness may be more illusory than substantial. He observes that blind individuals are least aware of their disadvantage when in the presence of others, whereas the deaf and mute are acutely aware of their plight. This observation holds true; in social interactions, the blind experience a sense of equality with those who can see, while the deaf and mute endure a profound sense of isolation. The ability to see, in this context, becomes a double-edged sword for the blind. They are able to observe the movements of lips and the expressions of joy around them, yet this only deepens their sense of confinement within a world devoid of sound. Consequently, they may appear more cheerful outwardly. Conversely, when it comes to solitude, the deaf and mute hold an advantage: they can immerse themselves in the vibrant colors and dynamic movements of life, while the blind lack this visual access and often face limitations in mobility that their counterparts do not experience. Therefore, Mr. Russell concludes that the blind find their greatest joy when they are observed by others, whereas the deaf and mute find happiness in moments of privacy away from the gaze of others.

While there is validity to Mr. Russell's perspective, I maintain that the prevailing belief holds true; when evaluating the cheerful acceptance of hardship, the loss of sight seems less burdensome than the loss of hearing and speech. This stems from a fundamental truth: the core of human existence lies in our ability to engage in meaningful interactions with one another. I cherish watching the sun set in brilliant hues, strolling through beech forests in the spring—indeed, in every season—and admiring the ripening apples and blackberry-laden hedgerows. Yet, these experiences serve as the backdrop for my life’s narrative; they provide scenery rather than substance. It is within human relationships that life derives its richness and vibrancy. The ears and tongue are essential conduits for the joyful exchange of thoughts and feelings. In this exchange, the blind individual fully participates, often finding enhanced fulfillment in their role within the social fabric. Their affliction often heightens their engagement in the shared human experience, allowing them to take on a central role within their communities. They embody Samuel Johnson's ideal of a sociable person, recognizable as "a clubbable man."
