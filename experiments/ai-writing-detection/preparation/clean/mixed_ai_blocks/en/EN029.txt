If you opt against naming your book Phyllis, John Temple, or Mrs. Elmsley, you might wonder what alternative title could work. One possibility is "Kapak," which has already been chosen by someone. The charm of the title "Kapak" lies in its symmetry; if a reader enters the shop from the rear and examines the book from the opposite direction, it remains unchanged. This feature offers a substantial advantage for any author. Moreover, there is an intriguing enigma surrounding "Kapak" that piques curiosity. Is it a term of endearment a bride uses for her father-in-law, a secret phrase to access a mystical castle, a product for polishing brown shoes, or perhaps simply a type of camera? The only way to uncover its meaning is to purchase the book immediately.

Another title that embodies mystery is "The Man with Thicker Beard," which likely carries significance. Similar to "Kapak," this title is palindromic, maintaining its meaning when read in reverse, though it lacks the subtlety of its counterpart. Nevertheless, it likely captures our interest enough to compel us to make a purchase. In contrast, a title like "A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette" is far less enticing. The very act of asking a bookseller for "A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette" is cumbersome; if we happen to forget the title—which is quite probable—and attempt to inquire about a "would-be nightingale and a Welsh suffragette," or perhaps a "wood nightingale and a Welsh rabbit," or even "the Welsh suffragette's night in gaol," we would soon find ourselves yearning for a more straightforward title, such as "Greed," "Earth," or "Jonah."
