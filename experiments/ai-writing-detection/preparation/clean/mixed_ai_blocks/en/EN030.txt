Although we cannot predict how we will react in critical moments, we are often acutely aware of our limitations. For instance, we might not grasp how we would respond in a life-threatening situation, such as a fire in a house or on a ship. The sheer terror and surprise of such threats could paralyze our instincts or, conversely, unleash our basest instincts, turning us into desperate creatures solely focused on self-preservation. Given the rarity of such intense emotional crises and the futility of rehearsing for them, the best we can do is hope that we would act appropriately when faced with them. Personally, I am well aware of my own shortcomings; I know without a doubt that public recitation is beyond my capabilities, and no situation could compel me to attempt it. The idea of performing, even before a friendly audience, terrifies me more than any danger I could encounter. When I mention recitation, I refer not to the simple delivery of an epigram but to the full embodiment of dramatic poetry through gestures and expressions. Just thinking about such a display fills me with an unsettling dread.

Witnessing a skilled reciter, regardless of how composed they appear, only amplifies my embarrassment. In my limited encounters with reciters, I have felt more acute shame for them than they likely have for themselves. Even the talented Brandram, when he adopted a falsetto voice for Shakespeare’s female characters, made me flush with discomfort, and I had to shut my eyes during his rendition of “Boots at the Holly Tree Inn.” Yet, Brandram was not a reciter in the traditional sense; instead, he was more of a solo performer who presented an entire play without any costumes or sets. The reciters I refer to tend to focus on individual pieces and are often amateurs who, undeterred by the harsh critiques found in works like Mr. Anstey’s “Burglar Bill,” perform at gatherings or social events. During my youth, the leading figure in this realm was the adaptable Dagonet, whose legacy was carried on by the late Mr. Eaton, known for “The Fireman’s Wedding.” Their primary humorist was the author of “T Leaves.” Following this, “Kissing Cup’s Race” became notable, thanks to the efforts of Mr. Lewis Sydney in theater and “Q” in literature, who worked tirelessly to challenge its popularity. Presently, I find myself out of touch with the current trends in recitation, having not encountered any for a considerable time.
