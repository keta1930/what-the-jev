I recently perused an American journal when I stumbled upon the observation that "one would as soon think of drinking beer out of porcelain as of slapping Nietzsche on the back." The notion of consuming beer from porcelain struck me as humorous, prompting a casual reflection on why this is not the norm. Typically, one enjoys beer from a sturdy earthenware mug, a pewter tankard, or a glass vessel, but never from fine china. If someone were to present you with beer in a china cup or basin, it would feel as if the beverage had somehow lost its essence. Conversely, if tea were served from a pewter pot, it would likely be perceived as diminished and unappealing. The argument that one drink is considered coarse while the other is refined fails to clarify this peculiar distinction. People relish beer from glass, and they prefer the finer quality of glassware. Yet, there is an intrinsic dissonance between beer and porcelain that cannot be overlooked.

I doubt that porcelain genuinely alters the flavor or quality of the drink. Instead, there seems to be an unspoken sense of propriety that the combination disrupts. The alignment of expectations is thrown off-kilter. Sensory experiences of touch and taste become misaligned, leading us to feel a discord that resonates within some deeper, unexplainable part of ourselves. In the domain of taste, we encounter the fascinating dynamics of these affinities and aversions in their most fundamental forms. Who first realized that two seemingly disparate items like mutton and red-currant jelly could create a harmonious culinary experience? What stroke of genius allowed an unknown chef to recognize that apple sauce elevates roast pork to new heights? Who was the visionary that revealed the secret love affair between cloves and apple pudding, a pairing that had long awaited its discovery throughout the ages?
