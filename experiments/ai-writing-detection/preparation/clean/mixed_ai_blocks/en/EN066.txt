A railway station is a remarkable venue, even if Ruskin had a different perspective. His views stemmed from his own modernity, which surpassed that of the railway station itself. His disposition was restless and irritable, akin to the clattering of a locomotive, rendering him incapable of appreciating the tranquil ambiance that such a station can offer. He claimed, “In a railway station, you are in a hurry, and therefore, miserable,” yet this sentiment doesn’t have to resonate with everyone—only those who mirror his modern sensibilities. A genuine philosopher regards the notion of arriving just in time for a train as a mere jest.

The most effective strategy I’ve found for catching a train is to arrive late for the one preceding it. By adopting this approach, one can discover within a railway station a sense of serenity and solace akin to that found in a cathedral. The station shares numerous attributes with grand ecclesiastical structures: it boasts soaring arches, expansive voids, vibrant lighting, and, crucially, a sense of rhythm or ritual. It serves as a homage to the essential elements of water and fire—two fundamental components of human rites. Furthermore, a train station draws parallels to ancient religions rather than their modern counterparts, primarily because it is a place where people congregate. Notably, popular venues, frequented by the masses, preserve the best traditions of the past far more effectively than locations or devices reserved for the elite. The transformative impact of the public is often slower and less radical than that of the fashionable elite. Ruskin might have unearthed more echoes of the Middle Ages within the Underground Railway than in the opulent hotels that line the streets outside.

The grand establishments of indulgence erected by the affluent in London often bear ostentatious and unrefined names. These titles can be pretentious, like the Hotel Cecil, or, even worse, cosmopolitan, such as the Hotel Metropole. However, when I travel in a third-class carriage from a nearby circle station to Battersea and then to the circle station for the DAILY NEWS, the names of the stations evoke a resonant litany of solemn and sacred memories. Departing from Victoria, I pass through a park dedicated to St. James the Apostle; next is Westminster Bridge, a name that pays homage to the venerable Abbey; Charing Cross stands as a testament to Christendom; the following station is simply called Temple; and Blackfriars recalls the medieval vision of brotherhood.
