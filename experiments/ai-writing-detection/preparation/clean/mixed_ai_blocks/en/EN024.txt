It is quite ludicrous that we (I use "we" intentionally, as I now count you among us) should act in this manner, for there exists no literary work that should cause us embarrassment, whether we have engaged with it or not. Hence, let us speak candidly. To dispel the unfortunate image I have inadvertently created, I must admit that I have only delved into three of Scott's novels and attempted, yet failed, to finish two of Henry James' works. I must further confess—this may indeed reinforce that unfortunate image—that I manage rather well in discussions about Scottish and Jacobean literature with those five books under my belt. If someone poses the question of which work by Scott stands as his crowning achievement, I can effortlessly recommend one of the three I’ve read, presenting it as if I’ve selected it from a pool of twenty options. Perhaps my choice is the widely recognized masterpiece; I cannot say for certain. If it is indeed regarded as such, then all is well. However, if it is not, I might appear rather astute for having bypassed the obvious choice. My standing with Henry James is less assured; nonetheless, I find comfort in the belief that the two novels I could not complete are unlikely to be his finest, and with a bit of finesse, I can convincingly defend this stance against a fictive critic who champions them.

At times, I feel as if I have consumed the entirety of their works, along with Ommany's Approximations, and various other texts that you would readily assume I am familiar with. In projecting an image of being well-versed in these literary treasures, I have merely conveyed a perception that has gradually taken shape in my own mind. Thus, I do not exploit these works to my advantage; and if it later turns out that we have collectively been misled, I assure you, I will feel just as astonished and outraged as they will.
