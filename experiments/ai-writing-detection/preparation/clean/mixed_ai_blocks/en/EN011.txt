The notion that lawyers are inherently more self-serving than individuals in other professions—like brewers, soap makers, or bankers—is one I find questionable. They represent a microcosm of society, encompassing both commendable and reprehensible individuals, just like any other group. Take Judge Jeffreys, for instance; he was undoubtedly a villain. Conversely, it was the legal professionals of the seventeenth century who played a significant role in safeguarding the freedoms of this nation. Few figures in history can rival the wisdom, selflessness, and heroism of Lincoln, who was also a lawyer. Likewise, in contemporary politics, few have made financial sacrifices comparable to those made by Mr. Asquith. His legal practice at the Bar reportedly generated an income of £10,000 annually, and had he chosen to focus solely on it rather than enter politics, he likely would have earned even more. Instead, he opted for a far more demanding role that never afforded him more than £5,000. Asquith could have risen to the position of Lord Chancellor, enjoying a prestigious lifestyle with a £10,000 salary, yet he decided to endure the trials of the House of Commons, becoming a target for every disappointed aspirant. One might argue, "But think of the glory!" To which I respond, as Danton famously remarked, I would prefer tending sheep in the hills to engaging in the governance of people. Politics is arguably the most thankless profession one can pursue. While Mr. Asquith may have his flaws, an obsession with the superficial allure of fame is certainly not among them. In just five minutes, you could observe more ambition for fame in Mr. Churchill than you would find in Mr. Asquith over five years, and interestingly, Churchill is not a lawyer.
