There is no narrative of the war that has captivated me as much as the account shared by Mr. Alfred Noyes in his enthralling coverage of his journey to the Fleet. This account centers on the Battle of Jutland. "In the very hottest moment of this most stupendous battle in all history," he recounts, "two grimy stokers' heads arose for a breath of fresh air." The specifics of the domestic dispute they were engaged in may remain a mystery, yet amidst the booming shells overhead, their exchange was strikingly mundane: "What I says is, 'e ought to have married 'er.'"

If this tale fails to resonate with you, then the essence of the English spirit may remain elusive. Yet, there are those among us who may never grasp it fully. During the war's early days, a good friend expressed deep despair over the song "Tipperary." How could a nation prevail when its soldiers marched into battle singing "Tipperary," while their adversaries approached with "Ein' feste Burg"? Framed this way, I had to admit the situation appeared dire. It felt as though "all Lombard Street to a China orange," as an old saying suggests. Indeed, "Tipperary" stood out for a music-hall tune, showcasing an unusual freshness and originality. When compared to the sentimental "Keep the home fires burning," which dominates today, it indeed reaches a level of artistic merit. Even now, hearing it played on the street organ brings a bittersweet joy—a joy intertwined with the profound emotions of those unforgettable times. It evokes the image of a butterfly ensnared in a tempest, a melody struggling for breath amid the chaos of death.

Nevertheless, it remains a music-hall tune, and to juxtapose it with Luther's powerful hymn feels akin to pitting a pop-gun against a formidable artillery piece. The reverberations of Luther's hymn have resonated through four centuries and will continue to echo through eternity. It embodies the fierce march of nature into battle, the upheaval of mountains, and the tumult of ocean waves. Few other compositions encapsulate such a sense of Power within the rhythm of song. The lyrics are as robust as the melody itself, with Carlyle masterfully conveying their formidable, rugged strength in his renowned translation:

A safe stronghold our God is still, A trusty shield and weapon; He'll help us clear from all the ill That hath us now o'ertaken….
