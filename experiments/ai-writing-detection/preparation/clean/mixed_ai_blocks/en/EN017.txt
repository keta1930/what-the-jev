"Being stationed behind a bookstall truly offers a unique perspective on life. This realization struck me abruptly last Monday while I stood before a bookstall at a train station, pondering the identity of those who purchased the tie-clips displayed there. Just as I boarded my train, the thought occurred to me: the man operating the bookstall would undoubtedly possess the answers. He would be aware of who bought his newspapers, books, and pamphlets, and understanding this provides insight into the nature of the people navigating the world. While one cannot judge an individual solely by the lobster they consume, the literature they choose to read reveals something significant about their character.

Take, for example, an experience I had while sharing a carriage on an eastern train line with a middle-aged woman. Shortly after departing Liverpool Street, she pulled out a bag filled with shrimps, gripping each one firmly by both the head and tail before devouring it. After she finished her meal, she discarded the shells out the window, cleaned her hands, and settled in with her newspaper. Which newspaper, you ask? You might be surprised—it was The Morning Post. This combination speaks volumes about her character. The shrimps alone wouldn’t tell you much, nor would the paper on its own; however, the pairing of the two offers a fascinating glimpse. Imagine the delight of the bookstall clerk as she approached with her bag of shrimps—he would have instantly recognized them, no doubt.

The life of a bookstall clerk is never mundane. I can envision him mentally matching the right publication to each customer. This man is likely to ask for Golfing—no, wait, he’s actually interested in Cage Birds; and that fellow over there probably wants The Motor—though The Auto-Car is close enough. Over time, he would distinguish between the various clientele, recognizing the readers of The Dancing Times versus those of The Vote, The Era compared to The Athenaeum. Occasionally, he would encounter delightful surprises, such as when—a memorable day at the station—a gentleman in a checkered waistcoat makes an unexpected purchase of Homer's Penny Stories along with The Spectator. During such rare moments, he might feel his faith in humanity begin to wane, only for the excitement of the situation to lead him to conclude that the man was, without a doubt, an escaped criminal in disguise, perhaps trying too hard to appear inconspicuous. Subsequently, he would hand over The Winning Post and The Animals' Friend to a pursuing detective with a sense of reverence. What a remarkable existence!"
