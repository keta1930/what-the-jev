Aldenham's pond is conveniently located not far from the house, easily accessed via a well-maintained gravel path, allowing for dry passage regardless of the weather. This enables visitors to stroll to its edge, whether alone or with company, and gauge roughly the amount of rainfall that occurred overnight. Some humorously refer to it as the “hippopotamus pond,” likening it to the animals’ enclosure at the Zoo, where the hippopotamus tends to remain submerged and hidden from view. However, for most people, it is simply called “the pond,” a name that overlooks the existence of other nearby natural ponds and attributes the entire creation to human effort. Indeed, “the pond” is a modest artificial construct made of cement, lacking any pretentious features.

The pond features seven steps leading down to its bottom, each measuring 10 inches in height. These steps serve as a practical rain gauge; when three steps remain visible, as was the case last Monday, it indicates that 40 inches of rainfall have accumulated since last month when the pond began to fill. This might surprise newcomers, but it’s essential to reveal a little-known fact: much of the adjacent land drains directly into the pond as well. This characteristic provides a more accurate representation of rainfall than the figures published in the newspaper. After a day of cricket ruined by rain, it feels trivial to be informed that only .026 of an inch fell; one longs for a more dramatic account. The pond's recorded increase of another 5 inches offers a sense of comfort that standard rain gauges would fail to provide, often leaving us frustrated. It reflects Aldenham's insightful perspective that he designed the pond with this in mind.
