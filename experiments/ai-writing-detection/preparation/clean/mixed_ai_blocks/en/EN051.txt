Anonymity might seem appealing if one could genuinely believe it stemmed from noble intentions. Imagine, for instance, if we were convinced that the writers at the Thunderer newspaper were valiant young idealists, fervently intent on dismantling both Municipal and National Socialism, not concerned about who would bask in the glory of their efforts. Sadly, this is not the case. What we actually perceive, or more accurately, what we understand, is that the Thunderer's critiques of Socialism emerge from a tangled web of conflicting and predominantly malevolent motives—each one would falter if exposed. An unscrupulous builder whose constructions have faced condemnation may write anonymously and claim a voice in the Thunderer. A disillusioned Socialist, at odds with fellow party members, could also take on the Thunderer persona. Likewise, a monopolist who has lost his grip on the market or a demagogue bereft of followers may both write under the veil of anonymity and assume the identity of the same publication. While it is true that youthful zeal can lead individuals to shun personal recognition, there exists a more prevalent, age-old fervor that compels individuals to conceal their identities.

Moreover, I wish to underscore another crucial principle for promoting integrity in journalism. It should be a standard practice for each publication to prominently display not only the editor's name but also that of the owner. In cases where a paper is owned by shareholders, a list of those shareholders should be made available. In scenarios that are far more frequent in this notably undemocratic era, where a single individual holds ownership, I advocate for that person’s name to be printed in bold, red letters on the paper. This way, if any particular interests are being promoted, the public will be aware of them. My acquaintances in Manchester are deeply concerned about the influence of brewers and the risks associated with their involvement in public office. Yet, when someone has swayed politics through the means of beer, the public typically becomes aware of those connections, as the topic tends to draw attention. Conversely, an individual might manipulate political narratives through journalism, and the average citizen might remain entirely oblivious to such influence. Time and again, we see individuals like Mr. Robinson suddenly honored with a peerage in the Birthday Honours list, seemingly without justification. Even the society columns, which we eagerly consume, offer scant information about such individuals, revealing only that they are either enthusiastic sportsmen, kind landlords, or have an interest in badger breeding. It is my hope that Mr. Robinson's name would be well-known among the British populace. They should be aware of the public contributions that warrant such recognition. It would be beneficial for them to have encountered his name previously in the pages of a publication like Tootsie’s Tips, The Boy Blackmailer, or Nosey Knows—those lively financial journals that have made significant impacts on the Empire while narrowly avoiding legal repercussions. Had readers come across his name in those contexts, they would likely appreciate more accurately and affectionately the Society column’s assertion that he is a true gentleman and a reputable Churchman.
