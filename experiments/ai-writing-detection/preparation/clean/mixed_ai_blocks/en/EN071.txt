During a recent stay in a quaint village, I found that the quickest route to the inn took me through the churchyard. As I walked this path repeatedly, I became familiar with the deceased residents of the area, perhaps even more so than with the living. My understanding was not as deep as that of the author of "Spoon River Anthology," who possesses an exceptional and insightful grasp of the dead, but rather a light-hearted acquaintance. It struck me as amusing that, given these individuals were complete strangers to me and could only be known through the efforts of those brave seekers of the supernatural, like Sir Oliver and Sir Conan, they occupied my thoughts quite frequently. This was largely due to the peculiar names I encountered. Many names had faded beyond recognition, eroded by the relentless passage of time, their inscriptions blurred by layers of lichen and moss or worn away by the tears of ages. However, some names remained intact and were truly remarkable. Have we ever pondered why the deceased often bear such extraordinary names compared to the living? Who among us has ever met a Lavender Wiseways in real life? Yet beneath one of those stones lay a Lavender Wiseways. Her sister, Lavinia Wiseways, rested nearby. Neither of them had wed, but it seems they could hardly give up such unique identities. And who now greets a gentleman in gaiters named Paradine Ebb? Once, not far from London, there was a farmer named Paradine Ebb, with whom one could exchange handshakes, converse, and perhaps enjoy a drink. Yet, I must be cautious not to reveal the village's name, as it would be impolite to disclose too many real names in today's inquisitive society.
