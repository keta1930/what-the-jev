The driver and I found ourselves seated outside the inn in silence, as if the atmosphere of that place dictated a shared understanding. I requested bread and a bottle of red wine for us both; it was pleasant enough, though it bore no label. Across the road stood a modest church, topped with a cross and a weathercock. To me, this scene epitomized a fitting conclusion to the world; should the narrative of existence wrap up here, it would do so beautifully. Yet, I questioned whether I could genuinely find satisfaction in concluding my journey here, amidst the finest elements of Christendom—a church, the laughter of children, fertile land, and a tavern where men could engage in conversation. As I contemplated this, a peculiar mix of doubt and yearning began to stir within me, prompting me to rise abruptly.

“Are you not content?” my companion inquired. “No,” I replied, “I cannot claim satisfaction even at what appears to be the end of the world.”

After a brief pause, I continued, “You see, there are two ends to the world. This, unfortunately, is not the right one for me. This is the French end of the world. I seek the opposite end. Drive me there.”

“The other end of the world?” he echoed, curious. “Where exactly is that?”

“It’s in Walham Green,” I murmured, my voice low. “You encounter it on the London buses. ‘World’s End and Walham Green.’ I appreciate this place; your vineyards and your independent farmers are wonderful, but my heart longs for the English end of the world. I care for you like a brother, yet I yearn for an English cab driver, who would add a touch of humor and inquire about his fare. Your horns awaken my spirit, but I crave the sight of a London constable. Please, take me to see a London policeman.”

He remained shadowy and silent against the fading light of the sunset, leaving me uncertain if he grasped my feelings. I climbed back into his carriage.

“You will come to understand,” I remarked, “if you ever find yourself an exile, even in the name of pleasure. The child to his mother, the man to his homeland, as one of your countrymen once expressed. But since it may be quite a trek to the English end of the world, we might as well return to Besançon.”

Only when the stars began to twinkle among those ageless peaks did I feel the tears for Walham Green escape me.
