Even those endowed with exceptional talent are not exempt from this creative drought. When Goldsmith composed what is arguably the finest English comedy since Shakespeare, he found himself at a loss for a title and ultimately relied on Johnson to provide one. It amuses me to consider that Shakespeare might have experienced a similar lack of inspiration—perhaps he too found himself gnawing on his quill in frustration, a sentiment all too familiar to lesser writers. This notion is supported by the very titles he chose. For instance, isn't the title As You Like It an admission of his own fatigue from the futile quest for a fitting name? Moreover, Twelfth Night: or What You Will stands as clear evidence that he struggled to find a title worthy of his most exuberant creation.

What parent has not faced a comparable struggle? Naming a child is a significant endeavor, one that bestows upon them a label that will accompany them through life and into death, representing the enigma of their early years, which only time and experience can unveil— hoc opus, hic labor est. Many falter by basing their choices on misguided influences—trends, aspirations, or fleeting whims. A child might arrive during a momentous occasion, like the announcement of a battle, or when a bestselling novel is making waves, or perhaps at a time when you are tempted to choose a name steeped in gravitas or heroism. In doing so, parents often forget that the child will carry the weight of their impulsive decision, resulting in names like Inkerman Jones, Kitchener Smith, or Milton Spinks.

Thus, the child is set on a path, forever linked to a historical reference or a bold comparison to legendary figures. Consider Milton Spinks, who may grow up to be unremarkable and ordinary—more Spinks than Milton. As plain John, he could navigate life content and unnoticed, yet the grand name of Milton clings to him like a comedic burden he cannot shed—not even in death, as it will linger, overshadowing him until time itself, with its gentle decay, obscures the name on his tombstone.
