A small assembly of men, each of whom had notably thrived in their respective fields, recently gathered after dinner around the fire in a London club's smoking room. This group included a distinguished lawyer, a politician well-known to the public, a renowned clergyman, and a journalist. Their conversation meandered through various subjects before settling on a familiar query: If given the chance, would you choose to relive your life? With one notable exception, the collective response was a resounding "No." Interestingly, the exception was not the clergyman; like the rest, he had found one experience at the theater sufficient and had no desire to repeat it.

This inquiry, I suspect, is as ancient as humanity itself. The responses are equally longstanding and, I believe, mirror the sentiments expressed by our small gathering around the fire. Such a question typically arises only in midlife, as youthful concerns are characterized by expansive dreams and an outlook that stretches into an endless horizon, rendering thoughts of repetition unnecessary. It is only when one has reached the pinnacle of life's journey and begins to descend, as evening shadows cast their lengths across the landscape, that this question springs forth uninvited. The response does not imply that the journey has been unworthy; rather, it acknowledges the arduous path traveled, the weariness of our souls, and the comforting notion of rest that beckons. This is nature's method of easing us into our shared fate. She reveals the vibrant spectacle of existence to her child, and then gently prepares him for his eventual return to the earth—

Thou hast made his mouth Avid of all dominion and all mightiness, All sorrow, all delight, all topless grandeurs, All beauty, and all starry majesties, And dim transtellar things;—even that it may, Filled in the ending with a puff of dust, Confess—"It is enough."

Indeed, it is enough. We accept the reality of mortality without complaint; in fact, we would not desire a reversal of this truth, even if it were feasible.

It is crucial to differentiate this line of inquiry from the rather misguided question, "Is life worth living?" The gentlemen in the smoking room would have responded to that query—if they had even considered it—with an immediate and disdainful "Yes." Each had discovered life to be a magnificent adventure; they had utilized their time meaningfully and would not trade a single moment or fragment of its diverse experiences. This sentiment holds true for all those with a healthy mindset. In times of despair, we may lament our birth like Job; however, such curses quickly fade. Swift, it is noted, marked his birthday as a day of mourning; yet, no individual who despises humanity can expect to find life bearable, for the extent of our compassion directly correlates with the joy we derive from living.
