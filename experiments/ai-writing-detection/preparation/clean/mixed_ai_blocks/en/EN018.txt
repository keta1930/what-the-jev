One intriguing aspect of what we term memory loss is its contagious nature. Frequently, when an individual fails to recall a name familiar to him, his companion—who also knows the name—often experiences a similar lapse. This phenomenon raises the question: why does this occur? Recently, I encountered a striking illustration of this peculiar occurrence. I needed to remember the name of a specific actor—not a household name, but a distinguished and adaptable repertory performer—so that I could promptly send him a letter. Though I was unable to recall his name, I described his appearance and performance style in enough detail that everyone present (about six of us) recognized him. Some even reminisced about the roles in which they had seen him, praising his talent, yet none of us could summon his name. Why was this collective amnesia happening despite our shared knowledge?

We left with the goal of retrieving this essential information, and my final notable contribution was that I believed his first name was Joseph, and that his last began with a P. When we reconvened the following day, each of us had suddenly remembered the name, which had come to us, more or less unexpectedly, during the night. The actor’s name turned out to be Michael Sherbrooke, and I must admit, the sudden recollection was particularly satisfying for me. If I am not recognized by my companions as Mrs. Nickleby today, it is only due to their kind nature.

The perplexing question remains: where do forgotten memories reside while they are eluding us, only to resurface later? What is their hiding place? This dilemma regarding their elusive nature troubles me far more than the difficulty of retrieving them from the depths of our minds. Could it be that these memories are not stored in our brains at all? Perhaps they enjoy excursions or breaks for lunch, leading us to occasionally miss them. Alternatively, might there exist a vast, external region of the brain from which we need to fetch these facts—similar to how one must wait for old newspapers to be retrieved from the British Museum’s archives? The fact that memories tend to return, almost invariably, eventually lends some credence to these theories.
