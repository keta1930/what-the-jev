The more one contemplates the narrative, the more plausible it appears; as Mr. Pemberton might have elaborated, additional evidence can be found linking Jonah and Jones through the species of fish that consumed the prophet yet could not hold him. For a skilled dialectician, the connection between whales and Wales would be a trivial task. I later discovered that Dr. Brewer, in "The Dictionary of Phrase and Fable," fervently supports the Jonah hypothesis. However, he unnecessarily traces "Davy" back to "duffy," a spirit from the West Indies. Consequently, he claims that Davy Jones's locker essentially refers to Duffy Jonah's locker—meaning the ocean floor or the intended resting place for Jonah as decided by sailors. This mix-up is quite amusing. First, a servant of God is cast overboard by the crew. Next, a divine fish arrives to rescue this servant. Lastly, the name of this servant of God transforms into a term for the sailors' demon, with him reigning over an element he had every reason to despise. Thus, the origins of myths unfold.

Turning to Davy Jones, J. Willock, another referenced authority in "The Oxford Dictionary," introduces us to an intriguing enigma. In one of his accounts, he remarks: "The great bugbear of the ocean is Davie Jones. At the crossing of the line, they call out that Davie Jones and his wife are coming on board...."

"And his wife"! 

However, I choose not to delve into the identity of Mrs. Davy Jones, regardless of any pressure from the entire Board of Admiralty.
