"But," I remarked, "what about this damaged tree? It was in the service of that Dryad, bound to the earth, that you rushed forth as if you were knights in shining armor. You, the enlightened advocates of humanity, cannot be fooled by the deceptive calmness of nature, which resembles the serene yet violent quiet of a waterfall. You understand that a tree is essentially a living entity, anchored to the ground by a solitary limb. You refuse to allow those who wield their Swedish daggers to spill the green lifeblood of such a being. Yet, if that is the case, why am I not detained? Where are my shackles? Bring forth from somewhere on your persons my decaying straw and my barred window. The facts I have just laid before you—my identity as Chesterton, my role as a journalist, my stay with the esteemed and charitable Mr. Blank of Ilkley—should not influence the matter at hand regarding any alleged cruelty towards plants. The tree suffers damage regardless of whether it can take pride in being harmed by an individual associated with the Liberal press. The healing of its bark does not accelerate simply because the injuries were inflicted by someone who is a guest of Mr. Blank of Ilkley. That tree, now a shadow of its former grandeur, shattered and brought low by the brute force of a Swedish knife, is a tragedy that cannot be erased merely by spending additional months in the company of wealthy individuals. It is astonishing that you lack the legal authority to apprehend even the most esteemed and fashionable individuals on such charges. If that is not so, then why did you intervene with me in the first place?"

I delivered the latter part of my speech to the stillness of the woods, for the two officers had vanished almost as rapidly as they had appeared. It is plausible, of course, that they were fairies. If so, their somewhat irrational understanding of crime, law, and accountability would provide a whimsical rationale; perhaps had I lingered in that glade until moonrise, I might have witnessed tiny policemen dancing upon the grass, or scurrying about with glow-worm belts, apprehending grasshoppers for endangering the blades of grass. However, considering the more pragmatic possibility that they were indeed policemen, I find myself in a quandary. I was clearly accused of an act that was either a crime or not. I was released because I demonstrated that I was a guest at a prominent residence. The implication seems painfully evident: either it is not deemed immoral to throw knives in a secluded woodland, or familiarity with a wealthy individual grants one a presumption of innocence. Imagine, then, a person in dire poverty, even poorer than a journalist—perhaps a laborer wandering in search of work, frequently shifting his accommodations, and often failing to pay his rent. Picture him intoxicated by the lush allure of the ancient forest. If he had hurled knives at trees and could only recount that he had recently been evicted, I pondered as I made my way home through a dusky and violet twilight, how would he have fared?
