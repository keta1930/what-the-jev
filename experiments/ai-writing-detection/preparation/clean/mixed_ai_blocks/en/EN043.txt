The fate of the Bastille serves as a clear example of a significant historical moment. Its demolition transcended mere reform; it embodied a profound act of iconoclasm, akin to shattering a revered statue. To the citizens, the Bastille loomed as a formidable giant, its many facades watching over them, and they responded by attacking it as though it were an immutable truth. Among the various manifestations of materialism that can oppress the human spirit, grand structures rank among the most intimidating. Individuals often feel like insignificant creatures caught within a colossal creation of their own making. It requires a considerable mental effort to recall that these imposing edifices are human creations, and, consequently, they can also be dismantled by human hands. Thus, the act of the impoverished masses tearing down a monumental public structure bears a symbolic and ritualistic significance that far exceeds its immediate political implications. This act resembles a sacred rite; for instance, if a group of Socialists were to bravely seize and demolish the Bank of England, one could endlessly debate the effectiveness of such an action, questioning its relevance to the core economic challenges at hand. However, the impact of such an event would be indelible; it would leave a mark on the collective consciousness and alter the course of history.

Architecture stands as a powerful reflection of a society's true resilience, especially regarding elements that are difficult to reverse, such as marriage. Among the various art forms, architecture comes closest to being irrevocable, primarily due to the challenges of its removal. While it is possible to position a painting with its back facing the viewer, it is far more complex to turn a grand cathedral away from sight. One might rip apart a poem, but it requires a deep emotional impulse to dismantle a town hall. Buildings, much like dogmas, assert their permanence with an audacity that can be unsettling. Questions arise regarding the absence of a distinctive architectural style in the modern era, akin to impressionism in art. The answer may lie in our lack of enduring principles; we struggle to accept anything in our skyline that is solid and unchanging, preferring instead the ephemeral nature of clouds. However, the decision to construct a building is inextricably linked to the equally important choice to destroy one. These two actions are inherently connected.

Paris is a prime example where a remarkable number of splendid public buildings have been erected, yet it has also witnessed substantial destruction. The tendency to preserve structures has paradoxically led to a decline in the impulse to create new ones. In London, one finds oneself lamenting the fact that so few buildings are demolished, which reflects a struggle to balance the preservation of history with the need for new expressions of societal values.
