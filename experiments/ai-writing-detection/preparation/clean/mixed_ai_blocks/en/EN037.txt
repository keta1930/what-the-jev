What insights does Vardon possess regarding this matter? When he completes a hole in five strokes, he chastises himself for not achieving it in four; should he finish in five, he feels despondent. Unlike us, he will never relish the delightful surprise that accompanies our finest shots. Instead, only his poor shots catch him off guard, which likely contributes to his unhappiness. His precision and distance are the product of a consistent technique, not the result of sudden realizations or innovative approaches that often aid us. The sole aspect of his game that may fluctuate is his putting, yet that element is more akin to croquet than to golf.

Yet, we, too, aspire to reach Vardon's level of play one day. We merely delay that goal, as there is a certain joy in our current shortcomings. The allure of being an amateur golfer lies in the possibility that overnight, we might transform into proficient players. If a struggling cricketer were to ask a skilled one, "What am I doing wrong?" the response would likely be, "Nothing specific, except that you can't play cricket." However, if we were to pose the same question to our more accomplished golfing friends, they might quickly point out, "You're moving your head," "Your right knee is dropping," or "You're not engaging your wrists early enough." With just a few adjustments, we could find ourselves playing at a different level by the next day. It appears that, for an eighteen-handicapper, golfing excellence hinges on such subtleties.

Thus, content in our current state of mediocrity and optimistic about our potential for improvement, we remain as long-handicap players. While it may be more reassuring to confidently strike the ball off the first tee, or perhaps to feel we have mastered topping by the fourteenth hole where public spectators abound, we might— 

Well, we might aim to reduce our handicap to fifteen this summer. But no further; certainly not beyond that.
