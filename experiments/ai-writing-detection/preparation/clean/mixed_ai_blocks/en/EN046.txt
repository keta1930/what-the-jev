Salmon, after exploring the human body as a source of restorative substances, shifts his focus to Alces, the Elk; Antilopus, the Antelope; and Asinus, the Ass. While many animals provide therapeutic benefits to humans, few rival the utility of the Ass. Although a living donkey has its merits, it pales in comparison to the range of medicinal properties derived from a deceased one, transformed into various drugs. Other animals such as Equus, the Horse; Capra, the Goat; and Cercopithecus, the Monkey also serve as rich sources for pharmaceutical concoctions. In essence, every creature—whether it walks on four legs or flits through the air—contributes its share of valuable remedies; however, the notion of humans extracting healing substances from themselves is unsettling. It seems fitting for the apex of creation to source treatments from lesser beings, but not from their own kind, which is a troubling notion.

Birds, too, play a significant role in this medicinal landscape. For instance, the flesh of Alauda, the Lark, is known to alleviate colicky pains—something to keep in mind at Ye Old Cheshire Cheese. Alcedo, the Kingfisher, when ground into powder and combined with powdered human skull and a dash of amber salt, proves effective against epilepsy. Disturbingly, a combination of several swallows crushed in a mortar yields a mixture that can prevent seizures. If one seeks to recover lost memories, the heart of Hirundo, the Swallow, augmented with filings from a human skull (Mr. Pelman's would be ideal) and dried peony roots, is highly regarded. Additionally, even a Swallow’s nest, when prepared as a poultice, can not only relieve quinsy but also treat snake bites. The delicate bodies of Rubecula, the Robin Red-breast, and Regulus, the Wren, also possess medicinal properties. The flesh of Lucinia, the Nightingale, is said to help with consumption, and its gall, when mixed with honey, serves as an effective eye wash. However, one might argue that songbirds deserve immunity from such utilitarian purposes. "Yet," one might interject, "if the nightingale could cure consumption, it might have saved Keats." While that's a valid point, had Keats sought out that remedy, he would have lost the essence that made him unique.

While discussing Lucinia, the Nightingale, Salmon makes an unexpectedly mundane observation that distinguishes him in literary circles. He presents a rather unremarkable assertion: “A Mr. Wilkinson, a clergyman,” stands out as a notably unpoetic line within the realm of poetry. Yet, his comment that Lucinia, the Nightingale, “grows fat in autumn” is an outright deflation of its enchanting allure.
