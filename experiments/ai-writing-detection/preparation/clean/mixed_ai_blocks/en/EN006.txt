There is a unique magic to the late February days, perfect for clearing out streams. The rushing yellow waters carry with them a dismal assortment of withered leaves, branches, acorn caps, remnants of bark, and sycamore buttonballs, all destined never to sprout. Whether standing by one bank or both, as the ambitious flood divides the land, the figure with a staff and pointing finger liberates the water spirit. She then dances and sparkles across the gleaming gravel, emerging from beneath the icy layer that blankets the higher grounds, flowing down into the gentler valleys, where her lively presence will be noted by the frogs nestled in their mud homes, and the fish, hidden in lightless pools, will rise to greet the illumination she brings.

From the frozen heights, during the warmer months, it is the birds and winds that carry the seeds of mountain flowers—lilies that lean against the eternal snows, vibrant poppies, colorful herbs, and those softly shimmering, fringed varieties that change their names with each land. It seems only fair that flowers bordering the ice in July should be willing to bloom in milder lowlands as early as February! The dazzling spectacle of blue, magenta, and scarlet adorning the stark upper Rocky slopes, where summer nights can be harsh for wanderers—why shouldn’t they thrive just beyond a valley barn during the season when icicles dangle from the eaves in this gentler locale? Yet, this is not the case. The fierce mountain storms can be endured by the delicate blooms, but the deceptive winter of the lowlands, with its enticing sunlight followed by fierce storms, is meant for flowers nurtured in a different lineage.
