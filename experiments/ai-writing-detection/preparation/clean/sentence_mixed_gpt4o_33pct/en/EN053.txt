There was a time when I stumbled upon Samuel Butler—not the other two, mind you, but the one who crafted The Way of All Flesh, a novel I deem the second-best in the English literary canon. I use the term "second-best" to grant myself the opportunity to concede that should you mention Tom Jones, The Mayor of Casterbridge, or any of your favored works, I could easily agree that those might hold the top spot. Well, I discovered him, just as Voltaire discovered Habakkuk, or your little boy discovered Shakespeare the other day, and I committed my discovery to the world in two glowing articles. Not unnaturally the world remained unmoved. It was already well acquainted with Samuel Butler.

Recently, I came across a French author named Claude Tillier, who penned a book titled Mon Oncle Benjamin in the early part of the last century. (I read it in the translation.) Eager as I am to be lyrical about it, I shall refrain. I think I can navigate my admiration for Tillier without the same risk I took with Butler, but I hesitate. The thought of your scorn at my previous ignorance of the world-famous Tillier, your amused contempt because I have only just succeeded in borrowing the classic upon which you were brought up, this is too much for me. Let us say no more about it. Claude Tillier—who has not heard of Claude Tillier? Mon oncle Benjamin—who has not read it, in French or (as I did) in American? Let us pass on to another book.

I am eager to share another discovery, a book that should hold the status of a classic but unfortunately does not. It is a work that remains largely unknown, except perhaps through my own recommendations. It was published some twelve years ago, the last-published book of a well-known writer. When I tell you his name you will say, "Oh yes! I LOVE his books!" and you will mention SO-AND-SO, and its equally famous sequel SUCH-AND-SUCH. But when I ask you if you have read MY book, you will profess surprise, and say that you have never heard of it. "Is it as good as SO-AND-SO and SUCH-AND-SUCH?" you will ask, hardly believing that this could be possible. I would respond, "Much better," and if the circumstances were just right, that might add a little something to my pocket. But, believe me, I shall be quite content with your gratitude. Well, the writer of my book is Kenneth Grahame. You’ve heard of him? Good, I thought so. The books you have read are The Golden Age. and Dream Days. Am I not right? Thank you. But the title you haven’t explored—my book—is The Wind in the Willows. Am I not right again? Ah, I was afraid so.

The reason why I knew you had not read it is the reason why I call it "my" book. For the past ten to twelve years, I have been promoting it. Usually I speak about it at my first meeting with a stranger. It is my opening remark, just as yours is something futile about the weather. If I fail to mention it at the outset, I find a way to introduce it by the conversation's end. The stranger must hear about it at some point. Should I ever find myself in legal trouble, and one can never be certain, my response to the query of whether I have anything to say would surely be, "If I may, I’d like to recommend a book to the jury before I conclude." Mr. Justice Darling would probably pretend that he had read it, but he wouldn't deceive me.

For one cannot recommend a book to all the hundreds of people whom one has met in ten years without discovering whether it is well known or not. It is the amazing truth that none of those hundreds had heard of The Wind in the Willows until I told them about it. Some were unaware of Kenneth Grahame entirely; well, one does not need to see such people again. But the majority were in your position—a great admiration for the author and his two earlier works, yet oblivious to this later masterpiece. I had their promise before they left me, and waited confidently for their gratitude. No doubt they also spread the good news in their turn, and it is just possible that it reached you in this way, but it was to me, none the less, that your thanks were due. For instance, you might have caught a couple of casual mentions of it, as if it were a universally acknowledged classic, in a well-known novel published last year. It was I who introduced that novelist to it six months before. Indeed, I feel sometimes that it was I who wrote The Wind in the Willows, and recommended it to Kenneth Grahame … but perhaps I am wrong here, for I have not the pleasure of his acquaintance. Nor, as I have already lamented, am I financially interested in its sale, an explanation which suspicious strangers require from me sometimes.

I won’t attempt to describe the book, as no words can do it justice. But I shall just say this; that it is what I call a Household Book. A Household Book is one that everyone in the home cherishes and frequently quotes long after reading. It is the kind of book that is read aloud to every new visitor, becoming a benchmark for their worthiness. But it is a book which makes you feel that, though everybody in the house loves it, it is only you who really appreciate it at its true value, and that the others are scarcely worthy of it. It is obvious, you persuade yourself, that the author was thinking of you when he wrote it. "I hope this will please Jones," were his final words, as he laid down his pen.

Well, of course, you will order the book at once. But I must give you one word of warning. When you finally sit down with it, do not be absurd enough to think that you are judging my taste or even Kenneth Grahame’s genius. You are merely sitting in judgment on yourself. … You may be worthy; I do not know. But it is you who are on trial.
