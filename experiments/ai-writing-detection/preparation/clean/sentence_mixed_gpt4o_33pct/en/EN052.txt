Please do not assume that this title suggests an intriguing personal story waiting to be shared. "Story! God bless you, I have none to tell, sir." I have not fallen in love for quite a long time, and, looking in the glass and observing what Holmes calls "Time's visiting cards" on my face and hair, I come to the conclusion that I shall never enjoy the experience again. I may say with Mr. Kipling's soldier that

That's all shuv be'ind me Long ago and fur away.

Yet, as Wordsworth suggested, poetry is emotion recollected in tranquility, and it is only when you have moved past the experience of love that you can genuinely articulate it or discuss it with the necessary philosophical objectivity.

Now of course there is no difficulty about falling in love. Any one can do that. The difficulty is to know when the symptoms are true or false. So many people mistake the symptoms, and only discover when it is too late that they have never really had the true experience. Hence the overtime in the Divorce Court. It also highlights the significance of "calf love," which serves as a formative experience, helping one to differentiate between the essence of love and its mere semblance.

When I refer to "calf love," I am not including the vague inklings of early childhood, reminiscent of those immortalized in "Annabel Lee":

I was a child and she was a child In that kingdom by the sea.

* * * * *

But we loved with a love that was more than love, I and my Annabel Lee.

I recall that love vividly; it blossomed when I was merely eight years old. "She" was also eight, and she had just come from India. She was frightfully plain, but then—well, she had come from India. The very essence of that distant land's coral beaches captivated me, and it was that allure that drew me to her. Additionally, she was the daughter of a soldier, and in my young mind, being a soldier's daughter was second only to being a soldier himself. For that was about the time when, under the inspiration of The Story of the Hundred Days , I had set out with a bag containing a nightshirt and a toothbrush to enlist in the Black Watch. Alas, my quest ended rather abruptly at the train station. Ultimately, she bestowed upon me, as a symbol of her affection, "Poor Little Gaspard's Drum," a book that introduced me to Napoleon and the Egyptian desert, and most thrilling of all, the Mamelukes. How that word thrilled me! "The Mamelukes!" What could one do but fall for a girl who invoked such enchanting phrases?

But this is not the true calf love. That comes with the down upon the lip. People laugh at "calf love," but one might as well laugh at the wonder of dawn or the coming of spring. When David Copperfield found himself enamored with the eldest Miss Larkins, he was genuinely in love with the vast, unfolding universe, and she merely served as the available conduit for his burgeoning emotions.

It is crucial to experience "calf love" while still young. It is like the measles, which is harmless enough in childhood, but apt to be dangerous when you are grown up. The "calf love" of an elderly man is always a disaster. Hence the adage, "There's no fool like an old fool." An elderly man should not fall in love. He should walk into it. He should survey the ground carefully as Mr. Barkis did. That admirable man took the business of falling in love seriously:

"'So she makes,' said Mr. Barkis, after a long interval of reflection, 'all the apple parsties, and does all the cooking, do she?'

"I confirmed that this was indeed the case.

"'Well, I'll tell you what,' said Mr. Barkis. 'P'raps you might be writin' to her?'

"'I shall certainly write to her,' I responded.

"'Ah!' he said, slowly turning his eyes towards me. 'Well! If you was writin' to her, p'raps you'd recollect to say that Barkis was willin', would you?'"

This is a model of caution in the art of middle-aged love-making. The error made by the "Northern Farmer" was that he applied the same cautiousness of age to the youthful heart. "Doänt thou marry for munny; but goä wheer munny is," he said to his son Sammy, who wanted to marry the poor parson's daughter. He presented his own matrimonial experience as a model for Sammy:

And I went wheer munny wor, and thy moother coom to and Wi' lots o' munny laäid by, and a nicetish bit o' land. Maybe she worn'd a beauty: I nivver giv' it a thowt; But worn'd she as good to cuddle and kiss as a lass as an't nowt?

I have always hoped that Sammy rejected his father's counsel and stuck to the poor parson's daughter.

There is no harm of course in marrying money. George Borrow said that there were worse ways of making a fortune than marrying one. And perhaps it is true, though I don't think Borrow's experience was very convincing. I have known people who "have gone where money was" and have fallen honestly and rapturously into love, but you have got to be very sure that money in such a case is not the motive. If it is the penalty never fails to follow. Mr. Bumble married Mrs. Corney for "six teaspoons, a pair of sugar tongs, and a milk-pot, with a small quantity of secondhand furniture and twenty pounds in money." Just two months later, he lamented his decision, admitting he had gone "dirt cheap." "Only two months to-morrow," he said. "It seems a age."

Those who subscribe to the notion of "love at first sight" believe that marriages are divinely orchestrated and that our earthly existence is merely about fulfilling that destiny. Johnson, who was an excellent husband to the elderly Mrs. Porter, scoffed at that view and held that love is only the accident of circumstance. But though that is the sensible view, there are cases like those of Dante and Beatrice and Abelard and Heloise, in which the passion does seem to touch the skies. However, such relationships rarely conclude in happiness. A more hum-drum way of falling in love seems better fitted to earthly conditions. Sir Thomas More's methods of courting were perhaps the most unusual on record. He had developed a preference for the second of three sisters and was poised to marry her when he experienced a moment of realization—But let me share the words of Roper, his son-in-law, who documented the event:

"And all beit his mynde most served him to the seconde daughter, for that he thoughte her the fayrest and best favoured, yet when he considered that it woulde be bothe great griefe and some shame alsoe to the eldest to see her yonger sister in mariage preferred before her, he then of a certeyn pittye framed his fancye towardes her, and soon after maryed her."

This illustrates a calculated form of love, yet it resulted in one of the most beautiful family lives imaginable, centered around this remarkable figure of English heritage.

In conclusion, there exists no universal formula for falling in love. Each one does it as the spirit moves.
