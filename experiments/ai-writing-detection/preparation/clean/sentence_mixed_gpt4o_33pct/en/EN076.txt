The happy man would have happy faces round him; a sad face is a reproach to him for his happiness. So, as I board the 2:10 train on Saturday, I generously share my good fortune. The cabman, feeling that an effort is required of him, mentions that I am the first gentleman he has met that day; he penetrates my mufti and calls me captain, leaving it open whether he regards me as a Salvation Army captain or the captain of a barge. The porters rush to my cab, jostling slightly over who will have the privilege of attending to me.

Inside the station things go on as happily. The booking-office clerk gives me a pleasant smile; he seems to approve of the station I am taking. He implies that while others may opt for Brighton, a gentleman like me would find this choice more fitting. He takes a moment to highlight that my ticket allows for a return on Tuesday, which, between us, I hope to utilize. In exchange for his courtesies I push him my paper through the pigeon hole. A scruffy little boy thrusts it into my cab; I didn’t ask for it, but today, in the spirit of happiness, I give him a penny.

I follow my porter to the platform. "On the left," says the ticket collector. He has said it mechanically to a hundred persons, but he becomes human and kindly as he says it to me. I feel that he really wishes me to get into the right train, to have a pleasant journey down, to be welcomed heartily by my friends when I arrive. It feels less like a transaction among a crowd and more like an interaction between individuals.

The porter has found me an empty carriage. He informs me about the train's departure, the stops we’ll make, and the anticipated arrival time. Am I sure I wouldn't like my bag in the van? Can he get me any papers? I decline; I don’t wish to occupy my mind with reading. I give him sixpence, and there is another one of us happy.

Presently the guard. He also seems pleased that I have selected this one particular station from among so many. His satisfaction is not surprising; he expected me to select this route. It is a very good run down in his train, and he shouldn't be surprised if we had a fine week- end. …

I stand at the door of ray carriage feeling very happy. It is good to get out of London. Come to think of it, we are all getting out of London, and none of us is going to do any work to- morrow. How jolly! Yet I can’t help but think of my porter again. I wish I had offered him more than sixpence. Still, he may have a sweetheart and be happy that way.

We are off. I have nothing to read, but then I want to think. It is the ideal place in which to think, a railway carriage; the ideal place in which to be happy. I ponder whether I’ll perform well at cricket and tennis, croquet, billiards, and all the other delightful games I plan to enjoy. My gaze drifts outside, landing on children attempting to play cricket in a grimy backyard. Poor little beggars! Fancy living in one of those horrible squalid houses. But you cannot spoil to- day for me, little backyards. On Tuesday perhaps, when I am coming again to the ugly town, your misery will make me miserable; I shall ask myself hopelessly what it all means; but just now I am too happy for pity. I remind myself that they might not envy me; they could very well be happy too. Of course; we are all happy to-day. See, I am waving back to you.

As my eyes scan the carriage, they settle on my bag. Have I put everything in? Of course I have. Still, an unsettling feeling creeps in, suggesting I’ve forgotten something crucial. Well, I can soon settle the question. Let's start with to-night. Evening attire—yes, that’s packed. Shirts, collars …

I go through the whole programme for the week-end, allotting myself in my mind suitable clothes for each occasion. Yes, it appears I have accounted for everything I might need. But what a very jolly programme I am drawing up for myself! Will it really be as delightful as that? Well, it was last time, and the time before; that is why I am so happy.

The train draws up at its only halt in the glow of a September mid-afternoon. There is a little pleasant bustle; nice people get out and nice people meet them; everybody seems very cheery and contented. Then we’re off again, and I know my stop is next.

We are there. A porter greets me with a friendly smile and a casual “Nice day.” I see Brant outside with the wagonette, not the trap; then I am not the only guest coming by this train. Who are the others, I wonder. Is there anyone I know? To my delight, I see Bob and Mrs. Bob, and—oh!—Cynthia! And is that old Anderby? How splendid! I must get that shilling back from Bob that I lost to him at billiards last time. And if Cynthia really thinks that she can play croquet …

We greet each other happily and climb into the wagonette. The countryside appears more beautiful than ever. "No rain today," Brant assures us, "and the barometer is rising." The porter loads our luggage into the cart and smiles at us. It is a rotten life being a porter, and I do so want everybody to enjoy this afternoon. Besides, I haven't any coppers.

I slip half a crown into his palm. Now we are all very, very happy.
