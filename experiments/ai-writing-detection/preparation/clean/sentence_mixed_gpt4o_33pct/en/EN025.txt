On this hillside, two distinct voices resonate with me. One is the voice of the day, the other of the night. Throughout the day the robin sings his song with unflagging spirit. It is not a very brilliant song, but it is indomitably cheerful. Regardless of the weather—be it rainy or sunny, chilly or warm—his song fills the November air from dawn until dusk. This little creature, adorned in his bright red vest, flits from tree to tree, often landing on the fence before hopping to the garden path, and finally to the door that leads into the kitchen. If you will give him decent encouragement he will come on to your hand and take his meal with absolute confidence in your good faith. Then he will trip away and resume his song on the fence.

Some critics have harsh words for the robin, labeling him selfish and claiming he can be difficult to live with. Yet, in my eyes, he stands as one of nature's most steadfast and uplifting companions. He is a bringer of good tidings—a philosopher who insists that we are masters of our fate and that winter is just the time when there is some sense in being an optimist. He seems to convey that anyone can adopt a hopeful outlook when days are long, the air is warm, and food is abundant; however, it is in those bleak moments when others begin to grumble that he dons his brightest waistcoat, tunes his best whistle, and comes forth to remind us that the indomitable spirit surpasses any circumstance.

As night falls and the valley below is shrouded in darkness, another voice emerges—the sorrowful lament of the owl from the thicket beyond the orchard. The day is over, he says, and all is lost. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." He insists that he alone remains to recount the conclusion of all matters. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." I've told it all before a thousand times, but you wouldn't believe me. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." The darkness envelops us, the cold wind bites, and the earth feels like a grave. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." Where are the songs of spring and the leaves of summer? "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." Where the red-cheeked apple that hung on the bough and the butterfly that fluttered in the sunshine? All, all are gone. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo … Tu-whit, tu-whoo … Tu-whit, tu-whoo…."

A cheerless fellow. Some people find him an intolerable companion. I was talking at dinner in London a few nights ago to a woman who has a house in Sussex, and I found that she had not been there for some time.

"I used to find the owl endurable," said she, "but since the war I have found him unbearable. He hoots all night and makes me so depressed that I feel that I shall go mad."

"And so you come and listen to the owl in London?" I said.

"The owl in London?" she replied, puzzled.

"Yes," I said, "the owl that hoots in Carmelite Street and Printing House Square."

"Oh," she said, "but that owl is utterly ridiculous. The country owl, on the other hand, carries such a grave demeanor."

"He says a very foolish thing In such a solemn way,"

I murmured.

"Yes, but in the silence and the darkness there doesn't seem any answer to him."

"Madame," I said, "if you will look up at the stars you will find a very complete answer."

I confess that I find the owl not only tolerable but stimulating. I like to hear the pessimist really let himself go. It is the vague, undefined fears that can immobilize us, yet when this owl articulates the situation in its most dismal light, I begin to feel a sense of defiance and determination. If this is the worst he can conjure, then let us strive to uncover the best and work towards achieving it. "This task is impossible," the pessimist might tell Cobden. "Indeed," said that great man. "Then the sooner we set about doing it the better." Oh, dear owl, are you claiming all is lost? You wait till the dawn comes, and hear what that little chap in the red waistcoat has to say about it. He's got quite another tale to tell, and it's a much more likely tale than yours. I shall go to bed and leave you to Gummidge in the trees until the sun comes up and tells you what a dismal fraud you are.

"Tu-whit, tu-whoo," the owl responds to me.

Indeed, dear sir, but you have uttered that lament every night I have known you, and yet the sun consistently rises, bringing spring once more, along with blooming flowers and fields bursting with harvest.

"Tu-whit, tu-whoo."

Oh, bother you. You could easily serve as a sensational headline in the Daily Mail.

No doubt the owl is quite happy in his way. Louis XV once articulated the owl’s philosophy when he remarked, "Let us entertain ourselves by making ourselves miserable." I suspect the poor creature finds some twisted joy in his own despair. I have always thought that, secretly, Mrs. Gummidge had a roaring time. She really enjoyed being miserable and making everybody about her miserable. I have known such people, and I daresay you have known them, too—people who nurse unhappiness with the passion of a miser. They are having the time of their lives now. They go about saying, "Tu-whit, tu-whoo! The Russians are beaten again, or if they are not beaten they will be. Tu-whit, tu-whoo! We're slackers and laggards, and the Germans are overwhelming us. Tu-whit, tu-whoo. They're on the way to India and Egypt, and nothing will stop them. All hope is lost." But I notice that they enjoy a beef-steak as much as anybody, and do not refuse their soup though they salt it with their tears.

I find the anecdote about Stonewall Jackson and the owl particularly telling. The owl was a general, and he rushed up to Jackson in the crisis of the first battle of Bull's Run, crying "All is lost! We're beaten!" "Oh," said Jackson, "if that's so I'd advise you to keep it to yourself." A half-hour later, Jackson's brigade made a charge that turned the tide of the battle. I can only wonder what became of the owl, but I suspect he continued to lament, "Tu-whit-ing" and "Tu-whoo-ing" until the very end. The owl can't help being an owl.

Ah, there is little red waistcoat singing on the fence. Let’s find him a worm, shall we?
