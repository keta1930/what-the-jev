In the town of Belfort, I find a seat on the street and observe my surroundings. We talk in a cant phrase of the Man in the Street, but the Frenchman is the man in the street. Things quite central for him are connected with these lamp-posts and pavements; everything from his meals to his martyrdoms. When first an Englishman looks at a French town or village his first feeling is simply that it is uglier than an English town or village; when he looks again he sees that this comparative absence of the picturesque is chiefly expressed in the plain, precipitous frontage of the houses standing up hard and flat out of the street like the cardboard houses in a pantomime—a hard angularity allied perhaps to the harshness of French logic. Upon a third glance, it becomes apparent that this is primarily because French homes do not feature front gardens. The vague English spirit loves to have the entrance to its house softened by bushes and broken by steps. It likes to have a little anteroom of hedges half in the house and half out of it; a green room in a double sense. The Frenchman desires no such little pathetic ramparts or halting places, for the street itself is a thing natural and familiar to him.

While the French may lack front gardens, their streets serve as a collective front garden for everyone. These thoroughfares are adorned with trees and occasionally fountains. The street is the Frenchman’s tavern, for he drinks in the street. It is his dining-room, for he dines in the street. It is his British Museum, for the statues and monuments in French streets are not, as with us, of the worst, but of the best, art of the country, and they are often actually as historical as the Pyramids. The street again is the Frenchman’s Parliament, for France has never taken its Chamber of Deputies so seriously as we take our House of Commons, and the quibbles of mere elected nonentities in an official room seem feeble to a people whose fathers have heard the voice of Desmoulins like a trumpet under open heaven, or Victor Hugo shouting from his carriage amid the wreck of the second Republic. The street, therefore, is not merely a place for dining and drinking; it is also a battleground where the Frenchman fights and sometimes perishes, imbibing the streets with a significance that transcends the ordinary.

Consider, for instance, the unassuming lamp-post. In London a lamp-post is a comic thing. We think of the intoxicated gentleman embracing it, and recalling ancient friendship. But in Paris a lamp-post is a tragic thing. For we think of tyrants hanged on it, and of an end of the world. There was a satirical Republican newspaper in Paris called LA LANTERNE, and one might find amusement at the thought of a British publication titled THE LAMP POST. We have said, then, that the Frenchman is the man in the street; that he can dine in the street, and die in the street. And if I ever pass through Paris and find him going to bed in the street, I shall say that he is still true to the genius of his civilisation. All that is good and all that is evil in France is alike connected with this open-air element. French democracy and French indecency are alike part of the desire to have everything out of doors. Compared to a café, a public-house is a private house.

Two particular reasons prompted these reflections while I wandered the streets of Belfort.

First of all, it lies close upon the boundary of France and Germany, and boundaries are the most beautiful things in the world. To love anything is to love its boundaries; thus children will always play on the edge of anything. They build castles on the edge of the sea, and can only be restrained by public proclamation and private violence from walking on the edge of the grass. For when we have come to the end of a thing we have come to the beginning of it.

Consequently, Belfort appears even more quintessentially French precisely because it lies on the very edge of Germany. Despite the German influences present in the area—such as German names, larger beer mugs, and theatrical barmaids dressed in exaggerated Alsatian costumes—the dominant French character seems to be intensified by these foreign elements. All day and night, groups of weary, sun-kissed soldiers trudge through the streets, emanating an air of stubborn disdain. German soldiers may exude contempt, but French soldiers possess a dual disdain, directed both at themselves and at others. This realism, perhaps, is an integral part of a nation skilled in war, science, and other undertakings that necessitate a blend of the essential and the unpleasant. And the soldiers and the civilians alike had most of them cropped hair, and that curious kind of head which to an Englishman looks almost brutal, the kind that we call a bullet-head. Indeed, we are speaking very appropriately when we call it a bullet-head, for in intellectual history the heads of Frenchmen have been bullets—yes, and explosive bullets.

But there was a second reason why in this place one should think particularly of the open-air politics and the open-air art of the French. For this town of Belfort is famous for one of the most typical and powerful of the public monuments of France. From my café seat, I can gaze upon the hill that looms over the town, crowned by a substantial, flat-faced citadel, its many windows glowing in the evening sun. At the base of this steep hill rests a colossal stone lion, as imposing as the hill itself. It is hacked out of the rock with a sort of gigantic impression. No trivial attempt has been made to make it like a common statue; no attempt to carve the mane into curls, or to distinguish the monster minutely from the earth out of which he rises, shaking the world. The face of the lion has something of the bold conventionality of Assyrian art. The mane of the lion is left like a shapeless cloud of tempest, as if it might literally be said of him that God had clothed his neck with thunder. Even at this distance the thing looks vast, and in some sense prehistoric. Yet it was carved only a little while ago. It commemorates the town's unwavering resistance against the Germans throughout a harrowing year, surrendering only at the behest of its own government. Yet, the essence of defiance and resilience has pervaded this land since time immemorial.

As I depart this town and board a train into Germany, the news becomes increasingly urgent, filtering through the streets that Southern France is ablaze, hinting at the potential culmination of a grim modern struggle between wealth and poverty. And as I pass into quieter places for the last sign of France on the sky-line, I see the Lion of Belfort stand at bay, the last sight of that great people which has never been at peace.
