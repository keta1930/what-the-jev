"I have spent a large part of my life in advising business men how to get out of their difficulties," said Mr. Asquith the other day. It was a statement wrung from him by a deputation which was inflicting on him the familiar talk about lawyers and the need of "business men" to run our affairs. I suppose there has been no more banal cackle in this war than the cackle about a "business Government" and the pestilence of lawyers.

Personally, I am not a lawyer and do not hold any particular fondness for the profession. I tend to steer clear of their services whenever possible. But it is as foolish to ban them as a class as it would be to assume that a grocer or a tailor is a great statesman because he is a successful grocer or tailor. Running an empire is quite a different job from running a grocery establishment, and it is folly to suppose that because a man has been successful in buying and selling bacon and butter for his own profit he can ipso facto govern a nation with wisdom and prudence. Take, for example, the most notable grocers of our time—Lord Devonport and Sir Thomas Lipton. Both excellent men, I've no doubt. But would you like to hand over the Premiership to either of them? Now, would you?

A great statesman must demonstrate his capabilities through the application of statesmanship, especially under the intense scrutiny of the public eye. If the grocer makes a howler in his trade the world knows nothing about it. If the statesman makes a howler all the world knows about it. He has to emerge to the front in the most public of all battles, and you may be sure that no one comes to eminence without great powers which have passed the test of the fiercest trials. He does not evade that test because he is a lawyer. This scrutiny does not spare lawyers; Mr. Asquith faced the same trials as Mr. Chamberlain, who was a nail maker, or Mr. Balfour, a landowner. No one derided Mr. Chamberlain as merely a "nailmaker" or Mr. Balfour as simply a "landlord" without adequately addressing their qualifications. Why should you suppose that when you have said "Yah! lawyer" to Mr. Asquith or Mr. Lloyd George you have disposed of them?

Is there a belief that lawyers possess a greater self-interest than people in other professions, such as brewers, soap manufacturers, or bankers? I doubt it. Lawyers are representative of the general populace, comprising both virtuous and unscrupulous individuals. Judge Jeffreys was indeed a notorious figure, yet it was the lawyers of the seventeenth century who played a crucial role in preserving the liberties we enjoy today. I doubt whether the world has ever produced a wiser, more unselfish, more heroic figure than Lincoln. And he was a lawyer. I doubt whether any man in politics to-day has made such financial sacrifices as Mr. Asquith has made. He relinquished a legal practice that earned him approximately £10,000 annually, a sum that would have increased had he committed fully to it. Instead, he chose a role that has consistently been more burdensome and never provided him more than £5,000. He might have been Lord Chancellor, with a comfortable seat on the Woolsack and £10,000 a year, and he chose instead to sit in the House of Commons every day to be the target of every disappointed placeman. Ah, you say, but look at the glory. Well, look at it. I would, as Danton said, rather keep sheep on the hillside than meddle with the government of men. It is the most ungrateful calling on earth. And, whatever other defects may be attributed to Mr. Asquith, a passion for such an empty thing as glory is not one of them. You will discover more passion for glory in Mr. Churchill in five minutes than you will discover in Mr. Asquith in five years. And Mr. Churchill is not a lawyer.

But this dislike of lawyers in the abstract has a certain basis. This is not a new phenomenon. Recall Samuel Johnson’s quip when asked about a departed individual: "I dislike making disparaging remarks about someone who isn’t present; however, I believe he is an attorney." And Carlyle was not much more civil when he described a barrister as "a loaded blunderbuss "—if you bought him he blew your opponent's brains out; if your opponent bought him he blew yours out. A lawyer’s weapon may be the law, yet their objective is not necessarily justice. As often as not he aims at defeating justice, and the more skilful a lawyer he is the more injustice he succeeds in doing. This detachment from the substance of a case and the conscious rejection of moral principles in their professional dealings creates a lingering suspicion around lawyers. Of course he has a very sound reply. They might argue that it is their duty to advocate for their client, while their opponent is tasked with doing the same for theirs, leaving the judge and jury to ensure justice prevails. That is true, but it does not get rid of the suspicion that attaches to a man who fights for the guilty or the innocent with equal fervour.

And then he deals in such a tricky article. Consider Sancho Panza, who administered justice as Governor of the Island of Barataria; had he been Governor of Britain, he would have enforced the law, and his judgments would have varied significantly. Law has about the same relation to justice that grammar has to Shakespeare. If Shakespeare were put in the dock and tried by the grammarians he would be condemned as a rogue and vagabond, and, similarly, justice is not infrequently hanged by the lawyers. We must have law just as we must have grammar, but we have no love for either of them. They are dry, bloodless sciences, and we look askance at those who practice them. One could be the most unscrupulous individual of their era, yet if one adheres strictly to the law, the lance of justice may never touch them. Law, which ought to serve justice, can often betray its intended purpose.

But do not let us be unjust. If law to-day is more nearly the instrument of justice than it has ever been, it is the great lawyers to whom we chiefly owe the fact. There are Dodsons and Foggs in the law, but there are also Pyms and Pratts who have upheld the liberties of this country in the teeth of tyrant kings and servile Parliaments.
