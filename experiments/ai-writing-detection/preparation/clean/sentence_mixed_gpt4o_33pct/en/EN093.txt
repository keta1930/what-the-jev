It is quite astonishing to me that there are individuals who genuinely believe that fairy tales are detrimental to children. I do not include in this group a certain man in a green tie, as I cannot truly regard him as human. But a lady has written me an earnest letter saying that fairy tales ought not to be taught to children even if they are true. She says that it is cruel to tell children fairy tales, because it frightens them. You might just as well say that it is cruel to give girls sentimental novels because it makes them cry. All this kind of talk is based on that complete forgetting of what a child is like which has been the firm foundation of so many educational schemes. If you keep bogies and goblins away from children they would make them up for themselves. One small child in the dark can invent more hells than Swedenborg. A single child can envision monsters so vast and dark that they cannot fit into any illustration and assign them names that no sane person would think to utter. The child, to begin with, commonly likes horrors, and he continues to indulge in them even when he does not like them. There is just as much difficulty in saying exactly where pure pain begins in his case, as there is in ours when we walk of our own free will into the torture-chamber of a great tragedy. The fear does not come from fairy tales; the fear comes from the universe of the soul.

The timidity of the child or the savage is entirely reasonable; they are alarmed at this world, because this world is a very alarming place. Their discomfort with solitude is rooted in the genuine terror of being alone. Barbarians fear the unknown for the same reason that Agnostics worship it—because it is a fact. Fairy tales, then, are not responsible for producing in children fear, or any of the shapes of fear; fairy tales do not give the child the idea of the evil or the ugly; that is in the child already, because it is in the world already. Fairy tales do not give the child his first idea of bogey. Instead, what these stories offer is a clear understanding of the possibility of vanquishing such fears. The baby has known the dragon intimately ever since he had an imagination. Fairy tales introduce them to a St. George, who can slay the dragon.

The essence of fairy tales lies in their ability to present a series of vivid images that teach children that these boundless terrors indeed have limits, that for every shapeless foe, there exists a valiant knight of God ready to confront it. There is something in the universe that transcends darkness and is more potent than the most intense fear. When I was a child I have stared at the darkness until the whole black bulk of it turned into one negro giant taller than heaven. If a single star shone above, it transformed that figure into a Cyclops. But fairy tales restored my mental health, for next day I read an authentic account of how a negro giant with one eye, of quite equal dimensions, had been baffled by a little boy like myself (of similar inexperience and even lower social status) by means of a sword, some bad riddles, and a brave heart. Sometimes the sea at night seemed as dreadful as any dragon. But then I was acquainted with many youngest sons and little sailors to whom a dragon or two was as simple as the sea.

Consider, for instance, one of the most disturbing tales from the Brothers Grimm, “The Boy Who Could Not Shudder.” This story is filled with unsettling imagery and incidents. There are some living shocks in that tale. I remember specially a man’s legs which fell down the chimney by themselves and walked about the room, until they were rejoined by the severed head and body which fell down the chimney after them. That is very good. But the point of the story and the point of the reader’s feelings is not that these things are frightening, but the far more striking fact that the hero was not frightened at them. The most fearful of all these fearful wonders was his own absence of fear. He slapped the bogies on the back and asked the devils to drink wine with him; many a time in my youth, when stifled with some modern morbidity, I have prayed for a double portion of his spirit. If you have not read the end of his story, go and read it; it is the wisest thing in the world. Ultimately, the hero learns to experience fear through an encounter with a wife who douses him with cold water. In that one sentence there is more of the real meaning of marriage than in all the books about sex that cover Europe and America.

Guarding a child's bedside are the figures of Perseus, Roland, Sigurd, and St. George. If we remove these heroic protectors, we are not helping the child grow rationally; instead, we are leaving them to combat the devils on their own. For the devils, alas, we have always believed in. While the elements of hope in our universe have been continuously challenged and reaffirmed in modern times, the existence of hopelessness has never been questioned. As I told “H. N. B.” (whom I pause to wish a Happy Christmas in its most superstitious sense), the one thing modern people really do believe in is damnation. The greatest of purely modern poets summed up the really modern attitude in that fine Agnostic line—

“There may be Heaven; there must be Hell.”

The pessimistic view of existence has been a longstanding tradition, and new forms of spiritual exploration tend to begin with a sense of gloom. A little while ago men believed in no spirits. Now, there is a slow resurgence in the belief in spirits, albeit at a cautious pace.

Some people objected to spiritualism, table rappings, and such things, because they were undignified, because the ghosts cracked jokes or waltzed with dinner-tables. I do not share this objection in the least. I wish the spirits were more farcical than they are. That they should make more jokes and better ones, would be my suggestion. For almost all the spiritualism of our time, in so far as it is new, is solemn and sad. Some Pagan gods were lawless, and some Christian saints were a little too serious; but the spirits of modern spiritualism are both lawless and serious—a disgusting combination. The specially contemporary spirits are not only devils, they are blue devils. This encapsulates the true essence of Christmas; as long as the mythology endures, it serves as a form of joyful mythology. Personally, I maintain my belief in Santa Claus, but I am willing to extend forgiveness to those who do not share this belief during this season of grace. But if there is anyone who does not comprehend the defect in our world which I am civilising, I should recommend him, for instance, to read a story by Mr. Henry James, called “The Turn of the Screw.” It stands as one of the most impactful works ever penned, though I often question whether it should have ever been published. It describes two innocent children gradually growing at once omniscient and half-witted under the influence of the foul ghosts of a groom and a governess. As I say, I doubt whether Mr. Henry James ought to have published it (no, it is not indecent, do not buy it; it is a spiritual matter), but I think the question so doubtful that I will give that truly great man a chance. I will approve the thing as well as admire it if he will write another tale just as powerful about two children and Santa Claus. If he finds this impossible, then it becomes evident that we have the capacity to tackle dark mysteries, but we struggle with those filled with joy.

In this way, we are not rationalists; rather, we are diabolists.

As I pondered these thoughts while gazing into a large, fiery red blaze that stood before me like a great red angel, I realized that you may not be familiar with the concept of a red angel. But you have heard of a blue devil. That is exactly what I mean.
