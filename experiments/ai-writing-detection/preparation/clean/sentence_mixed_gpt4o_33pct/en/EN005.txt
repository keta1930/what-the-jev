For three days and three nights the sea had charged England as Napoleon charged her at Waterloo. The phrase is instinctive, because away to the last grey line of the sea there was only the look of galloping squadrons, impetuous, but with a common purpose. The ocean surged forth like a wave of armored horses, and upon reaching the land, it unleashed a cacophony akin to cannon fire. I saw the worst assault at night on a seaside parade where the sea smote on the doors of England with the hammers of earthquake, and a white smoke went up into the black heavens. There one could thoroughly realise what an awful thing a wave really is. I talk like other people about the rushing swiftness of a wave. But the horrible thing about a wave is its hideous slowness. It hoists its massive weight of water with painstaking effort, reminiscent of a Titan laboring to lift a boulder before letting it slip, shattering it into dust. In front of me that night the waves were not like water: they were like falling city walls. The breaker rose first as if it did not wish to attack the earth; it wished only to attack the stars. For a brief moment, they stood tall in the air, resembling a tower; then, their shape faltered, like a structure destined to collapse. When it fell it was as if a powder magazine blew up.

I have never seen such a sea. A fierce and suffocating wind swept across the land, one that felt solid enough to lean against like a wall. One expected anything to be blown out of shape at any instant; the lamp-post to be snapped like a green stalk, the tree to be whirled away like a straw. Had I possessed any form, I would surely have been swept away, for I walked along the stone embankment above the turbulent sea, grappling with the notion that it was an invasion of England. However, as I approached a certain point, I was taken aback by the emergence of another sound mingling with the relentless roar of the ocean.

Somewhere at the back, in some pleasure ground or casino or place of entertainment, an undaunted brass band was playing against the cosmic uproar. I couldn't identify the band, but judging by the bombastic British imperial airs it performed, it seemed likely to be a German ensemble. But there was no doubt about its energy, and when I came quite close under it it really drowned the storm. The band played rousing tunes like “Tommy Atkins” and “You Can Depend on Young Australia,” along with many others whose lyrics I didn't know but could imagine to be about “John, Pat, and Mac, With the Union Jack,” or that unwritten piece, “Wait till the Bull Dog gets a bite of you.” Now, I for one detest Imperialism, but I have a great deal of sympathy with Jingoism. And there seemed something so touching about this unbroken and innocent bragging under the brutal menace of Nature that it made, if I may so put it, two tunes in my mind. It is so obvious and so jolly to be optimistic about England, especially when you are an optimist—and an Englishman. However, beneath that triumphant brass, the voice of the invasion, the ominous undertone of the sea, persisted. In a moment of folly, unable to articulate my feelings in prose, I attempted to express them through a poem—though it was a poor one. You can call it what you like. It might be called “Doubt,” or “Brighton.” It might be called “The Patriot,” or yet again “The German Band.” I would prefer to call it “The Two Voices,” but that title belongs to a far inferior poem. This is how it began—

“They say the sun is on your knees A lamp to light your lands from harm, They say you turn the seven seas To little brooks about your farm. I hear the sea and the new song that calls you empress all day long. “(O fallen and fouled! O you that lie Dying in swamps—you shall not die, Your rich have secrets, and strong lust, Your poor are chased about like dust, Emptied of anger and surprise— And God has gone out of their eyes, Your cohorts break—your captains lie, I say to you, you shall not die.)”

Then I revived a little, remembering that after all there is an English country that the Imperialists have never found. The British Empire may annex what it likes, it will never annex England. The island itself remains undiscovered, let alone conquered. I took up the two tunes again with a greater sympathy for the first—

“I know the bright baptismal rains, I love your tender troubled skies, I know your little climbing lanes, Are peering into Paradise, From open hearth to orchard cool, How bountiful and beautiful. “(O throttled and without a cry, O strangled and stabbed, you shall not die, The frightful word is on your walls, The east sea to the west sea calls, The stars are dying in the sky, You shall not die; you shall not die.)”

The two resounding noises swirled around me, the perilous call of England intermingling with the louder melody of its tranquility. I acknowledge that the final verse may have taken a somewhat haphazard and abrupt form:

“I see you how you smile in state Straight from the Peak to Plymouth Bar, You need not tell me you are great, I know how more than great you are. I know what William Shakespeare was, I have seen Gainsborough and the grass. “(O given to believe a lie, O my mad mother, do not die, Whose eyes turn all ways but within, Whose sin is innocence of sin, Whose eyes, blinded with beams at noon, Can see the motes upon the moon, You shall your lover still pursue. To what last madhouse shelters you I will uphold you, even I. You that are dead. You shall not die.)”

Yet, much like King Canute's futile attempt, the sea paid no heed to my words, nor did the German band cease its performance for anyone.
