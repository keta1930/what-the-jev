Not long ago, I had the peculiar fortune of encountering once more the renowned prophet known as Old Moore, whose cautious predictions can be found in the most unexpected places, including the streets. To my surprise, he did not recognize me. While it is not uncommon for people to forget others, it struck me as particularly odd that someone with such extraordinary foresight could fail to recall me, especially since I recognized him immediately. Long habits of fixing his penetrating gaze on the murky future have no doubt rendered the backward look less simple to him. Anyway, there we stood, I challenging him to remember me and he failing to do so. This momentary superiority of my own poor wits over those of a man who (undismayed by the refusal of events always to fall into line) foretells so much, uplifted me; but the untrustworthiness of memory is so constant and lands one in such embarrassments that it is foolish for anyone to boast.

Among the marvels of the human machine, memory is, indeed, strangest. The great bewildering fact of memory at all—of the miracle of the brain—is, of course, as far beyond our finite apprehension as the starry heavens. Of this? I never dare to think. But the minor caprices of memory may, fittingly enough, engage our wonder. The lawlessness of our prehensile apparatus, for example—the absurdly unreasoning system of selection of such things as are to be permanent—how explain these? And why should memory be subject also to that downward tendency in life which forces us always to fight if we would save the best? It seems that at the inception of our memories, it would have been equally feasible to instill an upward momentum. That was not done, but the memory, at any rate, being all spirit, might have been exempted from the general law. But no; as we grow older, not only do we remember with less and less accuracy, but of what we retain much is inferior to that which once we had but now have lost.

Once, I could effortlessly recite lengthy excerpts from esteemed poets, as well as those less celebrated but deeply cherished, like Matthew Arnold and William Cory. Now, I find solace in reciting a Bab Ballad to lull myself to sleep. That piece of nonsense never fails me, but I cannot at this moment give the right sequence of any two of the quatrains of the "Rubáiyát" of Omar Khayyám, although once, and for years, I had the whole poem complete too. I would much prefer to have retained the wistful verses of the Persian poet over Gilbert's light-hearted "Etiquette," but alas, the whims of memory had different plans.

Any prose that I might once have learned naturally faded first, because there was no rhyme or metre to assist retention; but why is it that there is one sentence which, never wholly mine, flits so often before the inward eye? It is in that story of Mr. Kipling's of the mutinous elephant who refused to work because his master was too long absent. This master, Dheesa, having taken leave for a trip, overstays his welcome. The phrase that persistently echoes in my mind is: "Dheesa had vagabonded along the roads till he met a marriage procession of his own caste, and drinking, dancing, and tippling, had drifted past all knowledge of the lapse of time." Now, surely, out of all the thousands of books which I have read and more or less dimly remember, it is very strange that this should be almost the only sentence that is photographed on the mind.

In my past, I was well-versed in numerous psalms; however, those have faded. Curiously, I have held onto a rather ludicrous exchange from a book titled "The World of Wit and Humour," which I studied many years ago:

"Father, I have spilt the butter. What shall I do?"

"Rub it briskly with a woollen fabric."

"Why?"

"Because friction generates caloric, which volatises the oleaginous particles of the stearine matter."

—And once I knew many psalms.

One of the odd things about what we call loss of memory is that it is catching. How often does one person forget a name that is well-known to him, only to have his companion, equally familiar with it, also draw a blank? Why is that? The other day I had an excellent example of this curious epidemic. This odd phenomenon was highlighted recently when I needed to remember the name of a not-so-famous but talented repertory actor in order to send him a letter. I had forgotten his name, but I described him and his methods with sufficient accuracy for every one (there were about six of us) to recognise him. We could even discuss the roles we had seen him play and praise his skills, yet none of us could retrieve his name in that moment. What was at play here? We all knew it; why did we unanimously fail to know it then?

We left, determined to uncover this elusive information, and my last wise contribution was that I believed his first name was Joseph and his last name started with a P. When we reconvened the following day, each of us had suddenly remembered the name. It seemed to have dawned on us during the night. Since the name was Michael Sherbrooke, you will understand why, in my case, its arrival was peculiarly gratifying. If I am not now known to those others as Mrs. Nickleby, it is only because they are so kind-hearted.

The great mystery is, Where, while one is forgetting them, are the things one forgets, but suddenly will remember again? Where are they lurking? This concern about their elusive presence weighs on me far more than the inability to summon them from the depths of my brain. Or are they, perhaps, not there at all? Do they not, perhaps, have evenings out, times off for lunch and so forth, and thus we sometimes miss them? Or can there perhaps be some vast extra-mural territory of the brain from which facts have to be fetched—as, if one would consult old newspapers at the British Museum, one must wait until the volumes can be brought from Hendon? The fact that they always, or nearly always, return, sooner or later, rather supports these theories.
