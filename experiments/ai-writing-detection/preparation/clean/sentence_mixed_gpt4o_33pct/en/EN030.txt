The future is uncertain, and while we cannot predict how we will react in dire situations, we are often acutely aware of our limitations. We may not know, for example, what kind of figure we should cut in a burning house, and even less in a burning ship: to what extent the suddenness and dreadfulness of the danger would paralyse our best impulses, or even so bring out our worst as to make us wild beasts for self-protection. Although true emotional crises are infrequent, and no amount of preparation can truly equip us for them, we can only hope that we would act appropriately in those moments. But most of us know with certainty what our limitations are. I, for instance, know that I cannot recite in public and that no circumstances could make me. Facing a crowd, even one made up of friends, fills me with a dread that surpasses any physical danger I might encounter. And by recite I do not mean the placid repetition of an epigram, but the downright translation of dramatic verse into gesture and grimace. The bare idea of such a performance fills me with creeping terror.

The spectacle of any real reciter, however self-possessed and decent, at his work, suffuses me with shame. I myself have in my brief experience of them blushed more for reciters than the whole army of them could ever have blushed for themselves. Even the great humane Brandram when he adopted the falsetto which he deemed appropriate to Shakespeare's women sent the hot tide of misery to my face, while over his squeaking in "Boots at the Holly Tree Inn" I had to close my eyes. Brandram, however, was not strictly a reciter in the way that I mean: rather was he an actor who chose to do a whole play by himself without costumes or scenery. The reciters I refer to typically focus on individual pieces and often consist of enthusiastic amateurs who remain undeterred by the scathing critiques in Mr. Anstey's "Burglar Bill," entertaining at various gatherings or smoking concerts. Their leading poet when I was younger was the versatile Dagonet, who had a humble but terribly effective derivative in the late Mr. Eaton, the author of "The Fireman's Wedding," and their leading humourist was the writer of a book called "T Leaves." Their era also introduced "Kissing Cup's Race," a piece that Mr. Lewis Sydney and "Q" worked tirelessly to render obsolete. Today, I find myself at a loss regarding the current favorites in recitation, as I have not encountered any in quite some time.

But from those old days when escape was more difficult comes the memory of the worst and the best that I ever heard. The worst was "Papa's Letter," a popular poem of sickly and irresistible sentimentality, which used to call out the handkerchiefs in battalions. The nominal narrator is a young widow whose golden-haired boy wishes to join her in writing a letter to his father. This was at a time before Sir Oliver Lodge had established wireless telephony between heaven and earth. Since the child cannot write she turns him into a letter himself by fancifully sticking a stamp on his forehead. He then dashes outside to play but is tragically struck by a runaway horse, and—

Who wrote this saccharine tragedy I cannot say, but I once found the name of W. S. Gilbert against it on a programme. Could he possibly have been the author? The psychology of humour is so curious....

So much for the worst recitations. The best that I can recall I heard twenty-five years ago, and have only just succeeded in tracking to print. It was recited at a Bohemian gathering of which I made one in a Fleet Street tavern, the reciter being a huge Scottish painter with a Falstaffian head. His jovial, ruddy face and exuberantly white hair and beard made quite an impression. I had never crossed paths with him before or after that evening, yet the memory of his presence amidst the swirling tobacco smoke remains vivid. When called upon to share something, the towering figure claimed he would recite James Boswell's authentic opinion of Dr. Johnson. A thrill of expectation ran through the room, for it appeared that the artist was famous for this effort. For someone like me, unfamiliar with the work, the title alone was enticing enough. With a depth of humor that seemed to require crossing borders, he presented the piece while perched tipsily on the edge of an imaginary bed. Every word told, and at the end the greatness of the Great Cham was a myth. For years I tried to find this poem; but no one could tell me anything about it. Here and there was a man who had heard it, but as to authorship he knew nothing. The Scotsman was no more, I discovered. It was only last year that I learned the name of the author: Godfrey Turner, a prominent figure in Fleet Street during the 'sixties and 'seventies. A chance meeting with his son led me to various humorous anthologies, one of which, H. S. Leigh's "Jeux d'Esprit," contained the poem. Like many of the best recitations, it does not read famously in cold blood, but as delivered by my Scottish painter it carried big guns. While the piece was splendid in the hands of that Scottish artist, I find no desire to hear it again. Nor, indeed, do I want to hear any recitation again, unless it is given in mimicry of some one else. Under those conditions I could listen to anything, so powerful is the attraction of the mimic's art. Possibly part of this fascination may be due to one's own inability to imitate too; be that as it may, no mimic who is at all capable ever bores me, and all fill me with wonder. Of course I am conscious that many of the imitators who throng the stage are nothing but pickpockets: too lazy and too mean to acquire novelties of their own, they annex snatches of the best songs of the moment under the plea of burlesquing the original singers. But even so, I often find myself immorally glad that they figure in the programme.

What stands out about talented mimics is their ability to not only replicate vocal tones but also to capture the essence of a person's conversational style. I recall an instance where a particularly skilled performer gave a spontaneous impression of a renowned scholar he had just met. Interestingly, during that impersonation, the mimic, despite being somewhat uneducated, seemed to adopt vocabulary and phrasing that I am certain he did not possess in his everyday speech. It was almost as if, for a brief interval, the mimic was the scholar, though always with the drop of ridicule or mischief added. It would be interesting to know if, when anyone is being impersonated as intensely as this, any virtue departs from him—whether he is, for the moment, by so much the less himself.
