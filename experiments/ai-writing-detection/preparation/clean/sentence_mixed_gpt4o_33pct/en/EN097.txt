Two soldiers, clearly siblings, were positioned at the entrance of a railway carriage—one seated within the compartment, the other standing on the platform.

"Now, you won't forget to write, Bill," said the latter.

"No," said Bill. "I shall be back at—tonight, and I'll write all round to-morrow. But, goodness, what a task! There's mother and the missus and Bob and Sarah and Aunt Jane and Uncle Jim, and—well, you know the lot. You’ve had to do it, Sam."

"Yes," said Sam, ruefully; "it's a fair teaser."

"And if you write to one and overlook another, they all get upset," Bill continued. "But I always mention all of 'em. I say 'love to Sarah,' and 'hope Aunt Jane's cold's better,' and that sort of thing, and that fills out a page. But I'm blowed if I can find anything else to say. I just begin 'hoping this finds you well, as it leaves me at present,' and then I'm done. What else can I say?"

"Nothing," said Sam, mournfully. "I just sit and scratch my head over the blessed paper, but nothing'll come. Seems as though my head's as empty as a drum."

"Same here. 'Tisn't like writing love-letters. When I was up to that game 'twas easy enough. When I got stuck I just put in half a page of crosses, and that filled up fine. But writing to mother and the missus and Sarah and Jim and the rest is different. You can’t just fill it with doodles; that would look ridiculous."

"Quite right," Sam agreed.

As the train began to pull away, the soldier inside settled back into his seat, pulled out a cigarette, and lit it. I found he had been twice out at the front, and was now home on sick leave. He had participated in the battle of Mons, experienced the retreat to the Marne, advanced to the Aisne, fought in the first battle of Ypres, and engaged in the fighting at Festubert. In a word, he had seen some of the greatest events in the world's history, face to face, and yet he confessed that when he came to writing a letter, even to his wife, he could find nothing to say. He was much like a woman mentioned by Horace Walpole, whose correspondence began and ended with the statement: "I write to you because I have nothing to do: I finish because I have nothing to say."

I suppose there has never been so much letter-writing in the world as is going on to-day, and much of it is good writing, as the papers show. But the case of my companion in the train is the case of thousands and tens of thousands of young fellows who for the first time in their lives want to write and discover that they have no gift of self-expression. It is not that they are stupid. It is that somehow the act of writing paralyses them. They cannot condense the atmosphere in which they live to the concrete word. You have to draw them out. They require encouragement to articulate their feelings. Once prompted, they can articulate their thoughts perfectly well, but without that nudge, they remain silent.

In the great sense letter-writing is no doubt a lost art. The introduction of the penny post and the fast pace of modern life have contributed to its decline. When Madame de Sévigny, Cowper, Horace Walpole, Byron, Lamb, and the Carlyles wrote their immortal letters the world was a leisurely place where there was time to indulge in the luxury of writing to your friends. And the cost of franking a letter made that letter a serious affair. If you could only send a letter once in a month or six months, and then at heavy expense, it became a matter of first-rate consequence. The less fortunate, of course, could hardly partake in the luxury of letter-writing. De Quincey tells us how the dalesmen of Lakeland a century ago used to dodge the postal charges. A letter arriving by stagecoach would be received by a mother who, upon glancing at the address and recognizing a certain mark indicating her son was well, would return it unopened to the carrier. In those days a letter was an event.

Today, however, with the ability to send a letter across the globe for a mere penny and postal services delivering multiple times a day, many of us do not take letter-writing seriously. Carlyle saw that the advent of the penny post would kill the letter by making it cheap. "I shall send a penny letter next time," he wrote to his mother when the cheap postage was about to come in, and he foretold that people would not bother to write good letters when they could send them for next to nothing. He was correct; the telegraph, the telephone, and the postcard have further contributed to the decline of this once revered form of communication. The rarity or difficulty of obtaining something often elevates its value. If diamonds were as plentiful as pebbles we shouldn't stoop to pick them up.

Yet, the situation for Bill, Sam, and countless others today diverges from this trend. They don't want to write literary letters, but they do want to tell the folks at home something about their life and the great things of which they are a part. But the great things are too great for them. They cannot put them into words. And they ought not to try, for the secret of letter-writing is intimate triviality. Bill might not be able to narrate the retreat from Mons, but he could share, as he did with me, the story of the blister on his heel, his yearning for a cigarette, the endless marches that left him exhausted, losing his friend at Le Cateau, or how his boot sole fell off at Meaux. And through such trivialities he would have given a living picture of the great retreat.

In short, to write a good letter you must approach the job in the lightest and most casual way. You must be personal, not abstract. One should not dismiss the significance of everyday occurrences as too trivial to mention. You must say, "This is just the sort of small thing we talk about at home. If I tell them this they will see me, as it were, they'll hear my voice, they'll know what I'm about." That is the purpose of a letter. Keats encapsulated this concept beautifully in one of his extensive letters addressed to his brother George and his wife in America, where he expressed the depth of familial affection that characterized his nature. He has described how he had been to see his mother, how she had laughed at his bad jokes, how they went out to tea at Mrs. Millar's, and how in going they were struck with the light and shade through the gateway at the Horse Guards. And he goes on: "I intend to write you such volumes that it will be impossible for me to keep any order or method in what I write; that will come first which is uppermost in my mind, not that which is uppermost in my heart—besides I should wish to give you a picture of our lives here whenever by a touch I can do it; even as you must see by the last sentence our walk past Whitehall all in good health and spirits—this I am certain of because I felt so much pleasure from the simple idea of your playing a game of cricket."

There is the recipe by one of the masters of the craft. A letter written in this vein annihilates distance; it continues the personal gossip, the intimate communion, that has been interrupted by separation; it preserves one's presence in absence. It should never be too simple, too ordinary, or too conversational. Its familiarity is not its weakness, but its supreme virtue. Should it strive for order, grandeur, or elaboration, it may turn into a fine essay yet will undoubtedly fail as a letter.
