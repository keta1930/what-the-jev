A friend of mine calling to see me the other day and observing my faithful Airedale—"Quilp" by name—whose tail was in a state of violent emotion at the prospect of a walk, remarked that when the new taxes came in I should have to pay a guinea for the privilege of keeping that dog. I expressed my hope that Mr. McKenna would not act so foolishly. In fact, I said, I am sure he will do nothing so foolish. I know him well and have always viewed him as a sensible individual. I suggested that if he must impose taxes, they should be based on our incomes, but questioned why he should interfere with how we choose to spend our earnings. Why should he deny the friendship of that most friendly animal the dog to a poor man and make it the exclusive possession of the well-to-do?

The emotion of Quilp's tail kept pace with the fervour of my remarks. He knew that he was the subject of the conversation, and his large brown eyes gleamed with intelligence, and his expressive eyebrows were eloquent of self-pity and appeal. He was satisfied that whatever the issue I was on his side, and at half a hint he would have given my friend a taste of the rough side of his tongue. But he is a well-mannered brute, and knows how to restrain his feelings in company.

What would be the consequence of such a high tax? I continued, my voice rising with fervor. It would be a blow at the democracy of dogs. It would reduce the whole of dogdom to a pampered class of degenerates. Is there anything more odious than the spectacle of a fat woman in furs nursing a lap dog in furs, too? This scenario degrades the noble lineage of dogs just as much as a footman adorned in gold buttons and braid diminishes the dignity of humanity. But it is just these degenerates whom a high tax would protect. Honest fellows like Quilp here (more triumphant tail flourishes), dogs that love you like a brother, that will run for you, carry for you, bark for you, whose candour is so transparent and whose faithfulness has been the theme of countless poets—dogs like these would be taxed out of existence.

Now cats, I continued—(at the thrilling word Quilp became tense with excitement), cats are another affair. Personally I don't care two pence if Mr. McKenna taxes them a guinea a whisker. The only moment in a cat's life that I find tolerable is when it is still a kitten. Who was that French thinker who suggested that women should be born at seventeen and perish at thirty? Cats ought to die when they cease to be kittens and become cats.

Cats, said my friend coldly, are the spiritual superiors of dogs. The dog is a flunkey, a serf, an underling, a creature that is eternally watching its master. Look at Quilp now, he said, what a display of servility. You don't see cats making themselves the slaves of men. They may enjoy being petted, but they do not form attachments to the hand that strokes them. They are not parasites, but independent souls, going their own way, living their own lives, indifferent to applause, calling no man master. This is precisely why the French regard them as superior to dogs.

I do not care what the French think, I said with warmth.

But they are our Allies, said my friend severely. The Germans, on the other hand, prefer dogs. I hope you are not a pro-German.

When it comes to the cat-and-dog debate, I unabashedly align with dogs, I replied boldly. And I hate these attempts to drag in prejudice. Moreover, I would beg you to observe that it was a great Frenchman, none other than Pascal, who paid the highest of all tributes to the dog. "The more I see of men," he said, "the better I like dogs." I challenge you to produce from any French source such an encomium on the cat.

No, I continued, the dog is a generous, warmhearted, chivalrous fellow, who will play with you, mourn for you, or die for you. Why, literature is full of his heroism. Who has climbed Helvellyn without being haunted by that shepherd's dog that inspired Scott and Byron? Or traversed the Pass of St. Bernard without recalling the devoted hounds of the renowned monastery? In contrast, the cat is a secretive and aloof being, selfish and enigmatic, reminiscent of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde. See her purring on the hearth-rug in front of the fire, and she seems the picture of innocence and guileless content. But do not be deceived, my dear friend—wait until night falls. Then where is demure Mistress Puss? Is she at home, keeping watch alongside the loyal dog Tray? No, the house may be in blazes or ransacked by burglars for all she cares. She is out on the prowl, indulging in clandestine escapades that seem so Oriental and ominous, steeped in a sense of devilish intent. I can understand why cats have long been associated with witchcraft; their very presence almost compels me to believe in such things, despite my rationality. I can entertain any notion about a cat. She is heartless and mercenary. Her name has become the synonym of everything that is mean, spiteful, and vicious. "An old cat" is the unkindest thing you can say about a woman.

But the dog wears his heart on his sleeve. His life is as open as the day. He has his indecorums, but he has no secrets. You can glimpse his flaws instantly, but his virtues are boundless. A cat is as distant from your life as a lizard, while a dog is as familiar as your own thoughts or shadow, and his steadfastness is one of the few comforts in a world often devoid of loyalty. You remember that remark of Charles Reade's: "He was only a man, but he was as faithful as a dog." It was the highest tribute he could pay to his hero—that he was as faithful as a dog. And consider the contributions of dogs—they draw carts in Belgium, gather sheep in the Yorkshire fells, tend to cattle by the roadside, and guard the lonely homesteads from prowlers, always vigilant, always obedient, perpetually the friend of humanity, regardless of how friendless one may feel… Shall we take a walk?

At the joyous word Quilp leapt on me with a frenzied demonstration. "Good dog," I said. "If Mr. McKenna puts a guinea tax on you I'll never say a good word for him again."
