Reading the terms of the agreement which Charlie Chaplin refused in New York early in 1916 I had a kind of nervous collapse. For we English are not so accustomed to great sums of money as the Americans are. I wrapped a damp cloth around my head and attempted to analyze the staggering figures with a detached perspective, though it was challenging to do so when they resembled fortunes fit for kings. Chaplin was presented with a staggering ten thousand dollars a week for a year, amounting to £104,000 at the time, and significantly more today (in 1920). Additionally, he was offered a signing bonus of one hundred thousand dollars and fifty percent of any profits generated from his films after the payment of his salary. But it did not satisfy him. He refused it.

Now here is a most remarkable state of affairs—that the popular demand for laughter is such that a little acrobatic man with splay feet and a funny way with a cigarette, a hat, and a cane could be offered and could repudiate such colossal wealth as that, and for no other services than to clown it for the cinematoscope. The peculiarity of this scenario is heightened when one considers that these astounding figures emerged during wartime. Chaplin's ascent to wealth and influence coincided with one of history's most brutal conflicts.

If it is needful for so many people to hold their sides, Charlie's career is justified. He is also the first droll to conquer the whole world. I suppose that it is no exaggeration to say that at any moment of the day and night—allowing for divergences of time—it would be safe to maintain that ten million people are laughing at the Chaplin antics somewhere or other on this planet of ours. Wherever there is a community of more than two thousand people, it is reasonable to assume there exists a cinema; where there is a cinema, Chaplin's presence is often felt, even if not always in the latest release, as managers tend to be resourceful, showcasing older films as well. Does the Funniest Man on Earth, as he is called, I should like to know, realise what a rôle he fills? Does he stand before the glass and search the recesses of his countenance—which is now far more familiar to the world than any other—and marvel?

Charlie, by the way, has his private uses too. During a recent visit from a young friend, I found that the ordinary gulf that is fixed between a boy in the neighbourhood of ten and a man in the neighbourhood of five times that number was for once easily bridgeable. We found common ground, and very wisely stuck to it, in the circumstance that each of us had seen Charlie, and, by great good fortune, we had each seen him in his latest sketch. Whenever, therefore, a longueur threatened, I had but to mention another aspect of Charlie's genius, and in the discussion that followed all was well.

That Charlie is funny is beyond question. I will swear to that. His humor is so universal that it could resonate with a Tierra del Fuegan or a Bushman from Central Australia just as well as with ourselves, who might consider ourselves more cultured. One needs no civilised culture to appreciate the fun of the harlequinade, and to that has Charlie, with true instinct, returned. The essence of his comedy draws upon the traditional harlequinade, intensified and adapted for the discerning tastes of a growing audience in the film industry. It is also farce at its busiest, most furious. Charlie brought back that admirable form of humour which does not disdain the co-operation of fisticuffs, and in which, by way of variety, one man is aimed at, and another, too intrusive, is hit. However long the world may last, it is safe to say that the spectacle of one man receiving a blow meant for another will be popular.

What quickly becomes apparent is the extraordinary effort Chaplin invests compared to many of his more celebrated contemporaries in film. He is seldom absent from the screen, constantly in motion, and delivering a full performance. There is no physical humiliation he does not endure and inflict upon others. Such impartiality is rare in drama, where usually men are either on top or underneath. In the ordinary way our pet comedians must be on top and untouched. Even the clown, though he receives punishment en route , eventually triumphs. He seldom emerges victorious; instead, he embodies the quintessential victim of fate, always resilient in the face of adversity. His very essence is resiliency under difficulties, an unabashed and undefeatable front. His especial fascination to me is that life finds him always ready for it—not because he is armed by sagacity, but because he is even better armed by folly. He is first cousin to the village idiot, a natural child of nonsense, licensed up to the hilt, and (like Antæus) every time he rises from a knockdown blow he is the stronger.

It is a proof of the charter which the world has handed to this irresistible humourist that he has been permitted to introduce such an innovation in stage manners as the hitting of women. We only laugh the more when, having had his ears boxed by the fair, he retaliates with double strength. In one scene, he humorously extracts a lady in evening attire from a fountain, only to push her back in with glee. It takes someone of Chaplin's immense charm and innocence to make such a scenario seem acceptable, and his unpretentious nature allows audiences to embrace even the most absurd antics.

One significant reason for the widespread appeal of Chaplin's films—beyond the allure of the central character—is that they depict scenarios that could not occur in reality without legal consequences, and which often remain absent from traditional theater. I mean that men so freely assault each other; physical violence has the fullest and most abundant play. Every one longs to see kicks and blows administered, but is usually defrauded, and Charlie is a spendthrift with both. Charlie, however, is generous with his slapstick, delivering humor through kicks and punches with such cheerful spontaneity that one might wonder why such behavior hasn’t sparked a global trend. I know this—that a fat policeman with his back towards the exit of a cinema at the time a Chaplin film had ended would be in great danger from my foot were I then leaving. While I hope to maintain self-discipline, I cannot guarantee it; Chaplin's influence might just sanctify such an impulse. Film life and real life would merge into each other so naturally that if the policeman repaid me—or attempted to—in any other way but kind, I should feel outraged. To be arrested for it would be like a stab in the back from a friend.

How long Charlie will remain the darling of two hemispheres we must wait to see. But of one thing I am certain, and that is that if at any time the "The Funniest Man on Earth" ceases to compel laughter, he might by slightly changing his methods draw tears. He has the ability to entertain the most relentless seekers of joy, but he is equally equipped with the tools to evoke sadness. One of his melancholy smiles is really beautiful.
