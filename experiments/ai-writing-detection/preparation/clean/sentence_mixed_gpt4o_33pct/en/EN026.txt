The conclusion that we had to relinquish the governess-cart signified that a new home for Polly needed to be secured.

Polly is a roan pony, notably plump, and particularly so in recent weeks due to the limited diet of meadow grass. She had been with us (this is my neighbour's story, as told to me during the War: a very charming neighbour who keeps her temper at croquet)—Polly had been with us so long as to become, as ponies peculiarly can, a member of the family, so that to part with her savoured of treachery. Necessity, however, knows no law and sanctifies no memory, and the distasteful preparations were therefore begun. The first was the framing of the advertisement; which is not the simple matter that it might appear to be, because so much depends upon the choice of adjective: the selected word must both allure and (in our case) keep within the bounds of truth. What are the qualities most valued in a pony, we had to ask ourselves. Celerity? Polly was fixed in her determination not to exceed the speed limit, at any rate on outward journeys. Willingness? Polly could be desperately stubborn. Strength? Yes, she was robust. Youth? Well, she had joined our family a decade ago and was no longer a young foal. After much serious contemplation, which could be likened to the deliberations at Versailles, we settled on describing Polly as either a "strong useful pony" or a "useful strong pony." Eventually, we agreed on the phrasing "Pony, strong, useful" and sent the advertisement off to the local paper, often humorously referred to as the county chronicle.

The next pressing matter was determining an appropriate price. Here expert opinion was resorted to, in the shape of Mr. Edmead, the butcher. No one knows more about ponies than butchers do, and Mr. Edmead is exceptionally wise.

"Taking everything into consideration," he said, "I think that twenty-five pounds would be a fair price."

We clung to each other for support. Twenty-five pounds! And we had given only nine pounds all those years ago. Why had we not taken up pony breeding as a hobby? Mr. Edmead elaborated that the War had inflated the value of ponies. Yes, taking everything into consideration, twenty-five pounds was a fair price. We ought to get that. In fact, if he had been in need of a pony he would have given that himself; but just then he was well supplied, and Polly was, he feared, not quite fast enough for him. Good morning.

Those seeking to buy a pony share a striking similarity. They are clean-shaven and wear hard round hats, and the collars of their overcoats are carelessly treated so that they are half up and half down. They carry sticks. Also, although they want a pony, they don't want one at quite such a figure. Each man who came to inspect Polly echoed a similar sentiment: while they recognized her as a strong useful pony, she ultimately did not meet their needs. Day after day Polly was examined. They opened her mouth and shook their heads, they felt her knees and her hocks, they looked at her with narrow eyes from near by and from far, they rattled their sticks in their hard hats, they gave her sudden cuts and prods. As time wore on, desperation began to set in.

While we were fond of Polly, the urgency of selling her grew stronger. Things should be done quickly. Then, a market gardener named Fox, a jovial and portly man, arrived. And Polly was again led forth and again subjected to every test known to pony-buyers. All was going well, and would have gone well, but for Vivian.

Who, you ask, is Vivian? We should be better prepared for the irruption of new characters. True, but this is not my story, but my nice neighbour's.

Vivian is a small boy who had known Polly all his life, and who by some mischance wandered out from his lessons in the morning-room at the precise moment when Mr. Fox, who obviously was attracted by Polly, was making up his mind to pay the full money. I should clarify that Vivian possesses a charm that leads him to view the world as a playground designed for making friends, drawing him to laborers who work outdoors. The moment he spotted Mr. Fox's kind face, he eagerly added him to his list of acquaintances.

"Run away, Vivian," I said. "It's not play-time yet, and we're busy."

"Are you going to buy Polly?" Vivian asked Mr. Fox by way of a suitable rejoinder to my command.

"I was considering it," Mr. Fox replied, turning to me. "How old do you estimate she is? She looks to me about twelve."

The figure was so low that I nodded assent, but Vivian spoilt it by exclaiming, "Oh, mother, and Mr. Brooks says she's seventeen if she's a day, and I'm sure she's a day."

Mr. Fox became thoughtful. "Mr. Brooks said that, did he?" he inquired.

I felt that I couldn't tell Vivian again to go in, because it would look as though I feared his frankness; which, to be candid, I did. All I could do was to hope for the best.

"She’s quite docile; accustomed to traffic and so forth?" Mr. Fox asked.

Vivian then began to chuckle. This nostalgic laughter, reflecting on past events—he often revisits old stories, and his giggles are quite endearing; however, in this moment, I felt a knot tighten in my stomach.

"Don't you remember, mother," he began, "that day when she was frightened by the traction engine and ran into the grocer's shop?"

Mr. Fox, whose large hand now held my son's tiny one, adopted a serious expression.

"That's against her in my business," he said.

I quickly interjected, "Oh, that was ages ago! She’s quite steady now. Don't you remember, Vivian, it was on your fifth birthday?"

"No," said Vivian, "that was on my seventh birthday—something funny always happens on my birthdays," he explained to Mr. Fox—"it was on my fifth birthday that Polly fell down."

"She's been down, has she?" said Mr. Fox ominously.

The ensuing exchanges grew increasingly disheartening. I had no intention of concealing anything; Mr. Edmead knew the pony's whole history when he valued her; but Vivian's presence made me nervous, painfully self-conscious; I felt my face burning and knew that I must suggest duplicity.

Mr. Fox, to his credit, navigated the situation skillfully. He asked Vivian no questions; indeed he talked of other things than defective ponies; but I could see his mind working; I could see pound after pound dropping away from the grand total.

Well, that's the story. Mr. Fox led Polly away some ten minutes later, leaving in her stead a cheque. But it was not for twenty-five pounds—Vivian saw to that.

The moral? The moral is: when your husband is in Mesopotamia and the time comes to sell the pony, lock your cherubic son in the nursery.
