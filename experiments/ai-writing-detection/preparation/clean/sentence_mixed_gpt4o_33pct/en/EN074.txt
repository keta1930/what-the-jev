We have all met the man who says that some odd things have happened to him, but that he does not really believe that they were supernatural. In stark contrast, I find myself firmly grounded in the belief of the supernatural, rooted in intellect and reason rather than personal encounters. I do not see ghosts; I only see their inherent probability. But it is entirely a matter of the mere intelligence, not even of the motions; my nerves and body are altogether of this earth, very earthy. Yet, it is often the most peculiar experiences that leave a lasting impression on those of my mindset. And the weirdest circumstance that ever occurred to me occurred a little while ago. It consisted in nothing less than my playing a game, and playing it quite well for some seventeen consecutive minutes. I daresay, even the ghost of my grandfather would have evoked less astonishment.

On one of these blue and burning afternoons I found myself, to my inexpressible astonishment, playing a game called croquet. I had imagined that it belonged to the epoch of Leach and Anthony Trollope, and I had neglected to provide myself with those very long and luxuriant side whiskers which are really essential to such a scene. I played it with a man whom we will call Parkinson, and with whom I had a semi-philosophical argument which lasted through the entire contest. It is deeply implanted in my mind that I had the best of the argument; but it is certain and beyond dispute that I had the worst of the game.

“Oh, Parkinson, Parkinson!” I exclaimed, affectionately tapping him on the head with my mallet. “You are so far removed from the pure love of the sport! You, who can actually play! It is only we who play badly who love the Game itself. You love glory; you love applause; you love the earthquake voice of victory; you do not love croquet. You do not love croquet until you love being beaten at croquet. It is we, the bunglers, who worship the sport for its own sake. It is we to whom it is art for art’s sake. If we could glimpse the face of Croquet herself—if I may put it that way—we would be content to see her expression of displeasure. Our play is called amateurish; and we wear proudly the name of amateur, for amateurs is but the French for Lovers. We accept all adventures from our Lady, the most disastrous or the most dreary. We wait outside her iron gates (I allude to the hoops), vainly essaying to enter. Our devoted balls, eager and chivalrous, refuse to be confined within the rigid confines of the croquet lawn. They seek honor in the far reaches of the earth; they end up in flower beds, conservatories, front gardens, and even in neighboring streets. No, Parkinson! The good painter has skill. It is the bad painter who loves his art. The good musician loves being a musician, the bad musician loves music. With a pure and unrelenting passion, I revere croquet. I love the game itself. I love the parallelogram of grass marked out with chalk or tape, as if its limits were the frontiers of my sacred Fatherland, the four seas of Britain. I love the mere swing of the mallets, and the click of the balls is music. The four colours are to me sacramental and symbolic, like the red of martyrdom, or the white of Easter Day. You miss all of this, dear Parkinson. To comfort yourself for the absence of this vision, you resort to the trivial consolation of successfully navigating hoops and striking the stick.”

With that, I flourished my mallet with a lighthearted elegance.

“Don’t be too sorry for me,” said Parkinson, with his simple sarcasm. “I shall get over it in time. But it seems to me that the more a man likes a game the better he would want to play it. While the pleasure derived from the activity itself is paramount, doesn’t the satisfaction of success naturally follow? Or, take your own simile of the Knight and his Lady-love. I admit the gentleman does first and foremost want to be in the lady’s presence. Yet, I’ve never heard of a gentleman who would want to appear foolish in her company.”

“Perhaps not; though he generally looks it,” I replied. “However, therein lies a flaw in the analogy, despite it being my own creation. The happiness at which the lover is aiming is an infinite happiness, which can be extended without limit. The more he is loved, normally speaking, the jollier he will be. It is definitely true that the stronger the love of both lovers, the stronger will be the happiness. But it is not true that the stronger the play of both croquet players the stronger will be the game. It is logically possible—(follow me closely here, Parkinson!)—it is logically possible, to play croquet too well to enjoy it at all. If you could send that blue ball through that distant hoop as easily as picking it up with your hand, you would not bother to do it; it would lose its significance. If you could play flawlessly, you would cease to play altogether. The moment the game is perfect the game disappears.”

“I don’t believe, however,” Parkinson interjected, “that you are in any immediate danger of achieving that level of perfection. I do not think your croquet will vanish through its own faultless excellence. You are safe for the present.”

I again caressed him with the mallet, knocked a ball about, wired myself, and resumed the thread of my discourse.

The long, warm evening had been gradually closing in, and by this time it was almost twilight. By the time I had delivered four more fundamental principles, and my companion had gone through five more hoops, the dusk was verging upon dark.

“We’ll have to call it a night,” Parkinson said, missing a ball for what seemed like the first time. “I can’t see a thing.”

“Nor can I,” I answered, “and it is a comfort to reflect that I could not hit anything if I saw it.”

With that, I struck the ball decisively, sending it off into the darkness toward the hazy shape of Parkinson. He let out a loud and dramatic exclamation. The situation indeed warranted such a response, as I had struck the correct ball.

Astonished, I traversed the dimly lit ground and hit my ball again. It went through a hoop. I could not see the hoop; but it was the right hoop. I shuddered from head to foot.

Words were wholly inadequate, so I slouched heavily after that impossible ball. Again I hit it away into the night, in what I supposed was the vague direction of the quite invisible stick. In the oppressive silence, I heard the dull thud as the ball made contact with the stick.

I dropped my mallet, exclaiming, “I can’t endure this! “My ball has gone right three times. These things are not of this world.”

“Pick your mallet up,” said Parkinson, “have another go.”

“I tell you I daren’t. If I made another hoop like that I should see all the devils dancing there on the blessed grass.”

“Why devils?” asked Parkinson; “they may be only fairies making fun of you. They are sending you the ‘Perfect Game,’ which is no game.”

I looked about me. The garden was full of a burning darkness, in which the faint glimmers had the look of fire. I stepped gingerly across the grass, as though it were on fire, retrieved the mallet, and struck the ball somewhere—anywhere another ball might be. I heard the dull click of the balls touching, and ran into the house like one pursued.
