This morning, the postwoman delivered a small publication titled The Superman, which piqued my interest with its focus on celestial matters, palmistry, and similar enigmas. I gather from it that "Althea," a normal clairvoyant, and other seers, have visited the planets—in their astral bodies, of course—to make inquiries on various aspects of the war. Althea and her fellow seers appear to have been quite busy exploring the cosmos, engaging with its inhabitants about the conflicts plaguing our world. They seem to believe they have uncovered profound truths. One of the more striking claims is that there is discord between Lucifer and Arniel. Lucifer, described as a fallen planetary deity, has been exiled from his position of power on Jupiter due to his insatiable thirst for dominion. He is the evil genius overshadowing the Kaiser and is striving to possess this world so that he may pass it on to Jupiter and eventually blot out the Solar Logos," etc., etc.

I do not know who sent me this paper or for what purpose; but let me say that it is sheer waste of postage stamps and material. I hope I am not intolerant of the opinions of others, but I confess that when people talk to me about reading the stars and the lines of the hand and things of that sort I shut up like an oyster. I do not speak of the humbugs who deliberately exploit the credulity of fools. I speak of the sincere believers—people like my dear old friend W.T. Stead, who was the most extraordinary combination of wisdom and moonshine I have ever known. He would startle you at one moment by his penetrating handling of the facts of a great situation, and the next moment would make you speechless with some staggering story of spirit visitors or starry conspiracies that seemed to him just as actual as the pavement on which he walked.

I am not at home in this atmosphere of mysteries. It is not that I do not share the feeling out of which it is born. I do. Thoreau said he would give all he possessed for "one true vision," and so long as we are spiritually alive we must all have some sense of expectancy that the curtain will lift, and that we shall look out with eyes of wonder on the hidden meaning of this strange adventure upon which we are embarked. For thousands of years we have been wandering in this wilderness of the world and speculating about why we are here, where we are going, and what it is all about. The puzzle seems particularly intricate now, as we are embroiled in the act of slaughtering one another. And at every stage there have been those who have cried, "Lo, here!" and "Lo, there!" and have called men to witness that they have read the riddle and have torn the secret from the heart of the great mystery.

As long as humanity has the capacity to think and feel, this quest for understanding will persist. We could not cease that quest if we would, and we would not if we could, for without it all the meaning would have gone out of life and we should be no more than the cattle in the fields. Nor is the quest in vain. We pursue various leads, grasp at fleeting glimpses of meaning, and though many paths may lead us to dead ends, we continually learn about the mysteries of our existence. Perhaps one day—suddenly, as the majestic white peaks of the Oberland reveal themselves to the weary traveler—we will discern the destination of this protracted journey. “Do not say the struggle is in vain,” a poet, not prone to delusions, once noted. And he went on:—

For while the tired waves, vainly breaking. Seem here no painful inch to gain, Far back, through creeks and inlets making. Comes silent, flooding in, the main.

Though I yearn for a revelation as much as anyone else, I find myself disconnected from the community of the credulous. I align myself with the skepticism of Doubting Thomas. I lack the ability to believe in the implausible and harbor a profound distrust of dimly lit rooms and the allure of magic. People with bees in their bonnets leave me wondering, but cold. I know a man—a most excellent man—whose life is a perfect debauch of visions and revelations. He seems to discover the philosopher's stone every other day. Sometimes it is brown bread that is the way to salvation. If you eat brown bread you will never die, or at any rate you will live until everybody is tired of you. Sometimes it is a new tax or a new sort of bath that is the secret key to the whole contraption. For a while, he could not stop talking about dried milk; during another phase, it was acetic acid. Rub yourself with acetic acid and you would be as invulnerable to the ills of the body as Achilles was after he had been dipped by Thetis in the waters of Styx. He interprets the stars to validate whatever belief he chooses to hold and summons spirits as effortlessly as one might hail a cab. It is not that he is a fool. In practical affairs he is astonishingly astute. It is that he has an illimitable capacity for belief. He is always on the road to Damascus.

As for me, I prefer to exercise patience. I am for Wordsworth's creed of "wise passiveness." I would find as much insight in interpreting my fate from the sole of my boot as from the lines on my palm. The one would be just as illuminating as the other. It would tell me what I chose to make it tell me. That and no more. And so with the stars. Those who claim to decipher the riddles of our experiences through the celestial bodies are either deluding themselves or attempting to deceive others. They are merely bestowing their personal whims with the authority of the cosmos. The butterfly I observe dancing in the sunshine has just as much claim to interpret the European conflict as a reflection on its aimless existence. The stars do not chatter about us, but they have a balm for us if we will be silent. The "huge and thoughtful night" speaks a language simple, august, universal.

It is one of the smaller consolations of the war that it has given us in London a chance of hearing that language. The lamps of the street are blotted out, and the lamps above are visible. Five nights of the week all the year round I take the last bus that goes northward from the City, and from the back seat on the top I watch the great procession of the stars. It is the most astonishing spectacle offered to men. Emerson said that if we only saw it once in a hundred years we should spend years in preparing for the vision. It is hung out for us every night, and we hardly give it a glance. Despite its availability, it is undeniably worth our attention. It is the best corrective for this agitated little mad-house in which we dwell and quarrel and fight and die. The celestial display offers us a fresh perspective and a reordering of our thoughts. Even the war seems only a local affair of some ill-governed asylum in the presence of this ordered march of illimitable worlds. I do not feel compelled to seek revelations from the stars; I do not pressure them for insights regarding the war. It is enough to see and feel and be silent.

Thus, I sincerely hope that Althea will refrain from squandering any more postage on her irreverent nonsense.
