As I navigated the winding lane that ascends the hillside towards the cottage nestled beneath the towering beech trees, a sense of gentle anticipation enveloped me, though I couldn’t quite pinpoint its source. It was late evening. Venus, who looks down with such calm splendour upon this troubled earth in these summer nights, had disappeared, but the moon had not yet risen. The air was heavy with those rich odours which seem so much more pungent by night than by day—those odours of summer eves that Keats has fixed for ever in the imagination:—

I cannot see what flowers are at my feet. Nor what soft incense hangs upon the boughs; But, in embalmed darkness, guess each sweet Wherewith the seasonable month endows The grass, the thicket, and the fruit-tree wild; White hawthorn….

Ah, that was it. I remembered now. Suddenly, I was reminded of my last nighttime journey up this lane, two weeks prior, when the dazzling sight of white hawthorn blooms illuminated by starlight had struck me with unexpected beauty. I knew the spot. It was just beyond here, where the tall hedgerow leans over the grass side-track and makes a green arbour by the wayside. I anticipated reaching that familiar spot soon, eager to once again experience the thrill of spring’s arrival.

However, upon arriving at the designated place, I found the white hawthorn had disappeared. The arbour was there, but its glory had faded. My two weeks spent in the hustle and bustle of Fleet Street had stripped it of its vibrant charm, and I realized I would have to wait for the full cycle of the seasons before I could once again witness the hedgerows erupting in a frothy display. I do not mind confessing that I continued my way up the lane with something less than my former exhilaration. Partly no doubt this was due to the fact that the hill at this point begins its job of climbing in earnest, and is a stiff pull at the end of a long day's work and a tiresome journey—especially if you are carrying a bag.

Yet, the deeper reason for the cloud over my spirits was the absence of the hawthorn. Poor sentimentalist, you say, to cherish these idle fancies in this stern world of blood and tears. Perhaps it is precisely this unforgiving reality that lends such depth to these seemingly trivial thoughts. It is through these reflections that we become acutely aware of the fleeting nature of all things. The coming and the parting in the round of nature are so wonderfully mingled that we can never be quite sure whether the joy of the one triumphs over the regret for the other. It is always "Hail" and "Farewell" in one breath. Today, I heard the cuckoo calling across the meadows, and I could already detect a wavering in its second note. Soon, that second note would fade entirely, leaving the solitary call to resonate over the valley like a bell tolling the end of spring.

Who, I thought, would not fix these fleeting moments of beauty if he could? Who wouldn’t wish to preserve the cuckoo’s twin calls, echoing eternally across summer fields, or to see the blackbird perpetually singing its joyous song after summer showers? Who can see the daffodils nodding their heads in sprightly dance without sharing the mood of Herrick's immortal lament that that dance should be so brief:—

Fair daffodils, we weep to see You haste away so soon; As yet the early-rising sun Has not attain'd its noon. Stay, stay. Until the hasting day Has run But to the evensong; And, having prayed together, we Will go with you along.

Indeed, I believe Herrick would have understood my momentary sadness regarding the white hawthorn. He would have understood. You will see that he understood if you will recall the second stanza, which, if you are the person I take you for, you will do without needing to turn to a book.

It is the same sense of the transience of beauty that inspired the "Ode to a Grecian Urn" on which pastoral beauty was fixed in eternal rapture:—

Ah, happy, happy boughs I that cannot shed Your leaves, nor ever bid the spring adieu.

And there we touch the paradox of this strange life. We yearn to hold on to the fleeting beauty of nature, yet we also recognize the importance of its transience. The thought of those trees whose leaves are never shed, and of that eternal spring to which we never bid adieu, is pleasant to toy with, but after all we would not have it so. It is no more seriously tenable than the thought that little Johnny there should remain for ever at the age of ten. You may feel that you would like him to remain at the age of ten. Indeed you are a strange parent if you do not look back a little wistfully to the childhood of your children, and wish you could see them as you once saw them. But you would not really have Johnny stick at ten. After five years of the experience you would wish little Johnny dead. For life and its beauty are a living thing, and not a pretty fancy sculptured on a Grecian urn.

The same holds true for the spectacle of nature. If the seasons were to halt, so too would the wonder surrounding them. I should have no sudden shock of delight at hearing the first call of the cuckoo in spring or seeing my hawthorn hedge burst into snowy blossoms. I should no longer remark the jolly clatter of the rooks in the February trees which forms the prologue of spring, nor look out for the coming of the first primrose or the arrival of the first swallow. While I would lose the pangs of “Farewell,” I would also forfeit the ecstasy of “Hail.” I would possess my Grecian urn, but at the cost of losing the magic of the vibrant, living world.

By the time I had reached the gate I had buried my regrets for the vanished hawthorn. I knew that to-morrow I should find new miracles in the hedgerows—the wild rose and the honeysuckle, and after them the blackberries, and after these again the bright-hued hips and haws. Even if the cuckoo’s call should fade, the thrush would take its place, and after the thrush, that steadfast little creature in the red waistcoat would continue the melody through the dark days of winter.
