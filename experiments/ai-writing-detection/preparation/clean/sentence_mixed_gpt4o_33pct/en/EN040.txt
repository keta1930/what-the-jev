As I sat in the garden just now, with a writing-pad on my knee and my mind ranging the heavens above and the earth beneath in search of a subject, my eye fell on a tragedy in progress at my elbow. A small greenfly had become ensnared in a spider's web, desperately flapping its delicate wings in attempts to break free. The filaments of the web were so delicate as to be hardly visible, but they were not too delicate to bear the spider whom I saw advancing upon his prey with dreadful menace. My initial disdain for the greenfly faded away, replaced by a fierce animosity towards the plump spider, who seemed to hold all the cards. Here, said I, is the Hun encompassing the ruin of poor little Belgium. What chance did the vulnerable little insect have against the deviousness of this creature, which set its gossamer traps in the breeze and waited patiently until its victim was ensnared and powerless? What kind of justice exists in nature that permits such an uneven struggle?

At this point, the spider had reached the hapless fly and wrapped another filament around it. Then at frightful speed he raced to the top of his web and disappeared in the woodwork of the arbour, drawing the new filament tight round the victim, which continued its flutterings for a little time and then gave up the ghost. At this moment I was called in to lunch, and at the table I told the story of the spider and the fly with undisguised hostility to the spider. "That," said Robert, home from the front—"that is simply a sentimental point of view. My sympathies as a practical person are all with the spider. He is the friend of man, the devourer of insects, the scavenger of the gardens. He helps in the great task of keeping the equilibrium of nature. Furthermore,” he continued, “I have seen you squash greenflies yourself. You did so because they were a nuisance. Why should you object to the spider doing the same useful work for a living?"

"Ah," said I weakly, "I suppose it is because he does it for a living. Now I …" "Now, you," interrupted the other, "do it for a living, too, because you want your fruit trees to bear fruit, and your roses to thrive, and your cabbages to prosper. Who is more ruthless than you when it comes to slugs and other pests that crawl or fly? No, no, we are all out for a living, you as much as the spider, the spider as much as the fly." "We are all Huns," said I. "What a detestable world it is." "Not at all," said he. "It's a very jolly world. I drink to the health of the spider."

"And you have no pity for the fly?" I said. "Not a little bit." he replied. “I am on the side of righteousness.” "Whose side is that?" I asked. "Mine," said he. "We must all act according to our point of view. That’s what the greenfly does. That’s what the spider does. We will never align all points of view in this world. We shall go on scrambling for a living to the end. Sometimes the greenfly prevails, sometimes the spider does. Look at that cherry tree in the orchard. A month ago its branches were laden with fruit. Now, not a single cherry remains. The blackbirds and the starlings have stripped the tree as clean as a bone. Their point of view is that the cherries are provided for them, and they are right. They know nothing of the laws of property which man makes for his own protection. There’s no sense in approaching them to show your title deeds or remind them of the police and anti-theft laws. Our moral code is for us, not for them.

"We are all creatures of our own point of view," he went on. “Before Jones next door acquired a car, he felt very strongly against motorists—he often called them road-hogs and insisted he would tax these ‘land-torpedoes’ out of existence. He was filled with sympathy for the children coming home from school. Now he drives a car as hard as anybody; blows the hoggiest of horns; and says it's disgraceful the way parents allow their children to play about in the streets. Nothing has changed except his point of view. He has shifted round to another position, and sees things from a new angle of vision. Samuel Butler hit the comedy of the thing off long ago:—

What makes all doctrine plain and clear? About two hundred pounds a year. And that which was proved true before Prove false again? Two hundred more."

“Are our viewpoints then solely dictated by our selfish desires, like those of your spider friend, who has likely consumed my greenfly by now?” “No, I don’t mean to imply that. I think that, comprehending all our private points of view, there is an absolute motive running through human society, call it the world spirit, the mind of the race, or what you will, that is something greater and better than we. The collective movement of humanity is, barring rare exceptions, loftier than its individual expressions. I respond and you respond to an abstract justice, an abstract righteousness, which is purer and better than anything we are capable of. At our core, I believe we are superior to what our actions depict, more noble than our limited perspectives enable us to be, and in our moments of clarity, we catch a glimpse of that Jacob's ladder that Francis Thompson described, with its ascending angels, at Charing Cross. Some one called Shelley 'an ineffectual angel.' I think most of us are ineffectual angels. Consider the current tragedy engulfing the world in horror. We are fighting like tigers for our own points of view, but in our hearts we are ashamed of the spectacle, and know that humanity is better than its deeds. One day, perhaps, the ineffectual angel will find his wings and outsoar the spider point of view…. And, by the way, suppose we go and see how the spider is getting on."

We ventured out to the garden and approached the web. But the little green corpse had gone, and the spider was digesting his meal somewhere out of sight.
