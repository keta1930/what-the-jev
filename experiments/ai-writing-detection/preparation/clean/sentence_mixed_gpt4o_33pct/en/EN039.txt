I find myself seated beneath towering trees, where a fierce wind whips through their crowns, creating a tumultuous symphony of leaves that resonates with a blend of joy and turmoil. I feel, in fact, as if I were actually sitting at the bottom of the sea among mere anchors and ropes, while over my head and over the green twilight of water sounded the everlasting rush of waves and the toil and crash and shipwreck of tremendous ships. The wind pulls at the trees with such vigor that it seems capable of uprooting them as though they were mere tufts of grass. To capture this indescribable force in another way, I envision the trees as a band of dragons, each tethered by its tail, straining against an unseen force.

Observing these immense beings writhing under the influence of an invisible and violent energy brings to mind a memory of a young boy I once knew. During a stroll through Battersea Park on a day much like this, with dark skies and swaying trees, he exhibited a profound dislike for the wind. He did not like the wind at all; it blew in his face too much; it made him shut his eyes; and it blew off his hat, of which he was very proud. He was, as far as I remember, about four. After many complaints about the chaotic weather, he finally turned to his mother and exclaimed, “Well, why don’t you take away the trees, and then it wouldn’t wind.”

Nothing could be more intelligent or natural than this mistake. Any one looking for the first time at the trees might fancy that they were indeed vast and titanic fans, which by their mere waving agitated the air around them for miles. Nothing, I say, could be more human and excusable than the belief that it is the trees which make the wind. Indeed, the belief is so human and excusable that it is, as a matter of fact, the belief of about ninety-nine out of a hundred of the philosophers, reformers, sociologists, and politicians of the great age in which we live. My small friend was, in fact, very like the principal modern thinkers; only much nicer.

In the little parable he inadvertently crafted, the trees symbolize all tangible entities, while the wind represents the intangible. The wind is the spirit which bloweth where it listeth; the trees are the material things of the world which are blown where the spirit lists. The wind is philosophy, religion, revolution; the trees are cities and civilisations. We only know that there is a wind because the trees on some distant hill suddenly go mad. We only know that there is a real revolution because all the chimney-pots go mad on the whole skyline of the city.

Just as the ragged outline of a tree grows suddenly more ragged and rises into fantastic crests or tattered tails, so the human city rises under the wind of the spirit into toppling temples or sudden spires. No man has ever seen a revolution. Crowds surging through palaces, blood flowing through the streets, the guillotine raised above the throne, a prison in tatters, a populace revolting—these scenes depict not the revolution itself but rather its aftermath.

The elusive nature of wind parallels that of a revolution; while you cannot see wind itself, you can observe its effects. So, also, you cannot see a revolution; you can only see that there is a revolution. And there never has been in the history of the world a real revolution, brutally active and decisive, which was not preceded by unrest and new dogma in the reign of invisible things. All revolutions began by being abstract. Most revolutions began by being quite pedantically abstract.

The wind is up above the world before a twig on the tree has moved. So there must always be a battle in the sky before there is a battle on the earth. Since it is lawful to pray for the coming of the kingdom, it is lawful also to pray for the coming of the revolution that shall restore the kingdom. We are justified in yearning to hear the whispers of Heaven among the trees, praying for “Thine anger come on earth as it is in Heaven.”

The fundamental human doctrine asserts that the wind is what stirs the trees.

In contrast, the profound fallacy lies in the belief that the trees generate the wind. When people begin to say that the material circumstances have alone created the moral circumstances, then they have prevented all possibility of serious change. If my circumstances have rendered me entirely foolish, how can I trust that I am even correct in wishing to change those circumstances?

The man who represents all thought as an accident of environment is simply smashing and discrediting all his own thoughts—including that one. To treat the human mind as having an ultimate authority is necessary to any kind of thinking, even free thinking. And nothing will ever be reformed in this age or country unless we realise that the moral fact comes first.

For example, most of us, I suppose, have seen in print and heard in debating clubs an endless discussion that goes on between Socialists and total abstainers. The abstainers argue that alcohol leads to poverty, while Socialists maintain that poverty drives individuals to drink. I find it perplexing that either side accepts such simplistic, physical explanations. Surely it is obvious that the thing which among the English proletariat leads to poverty is the same as the thing which leads to drink; the absence of strong civic dignity, the absence of an instinct that resists degradation.

When you have discovered why enormous English estates were not long ago cut up into small holdings like the land of France, you will have discovered why the Englishman is more drunken than the Frenchman. The Englishman, despite possessing numerous admirable traits, is often characterized by a tendency to live “hand to mouth,” where his instinct is to seek self-gratification rather than challenge his oppressor. And a man who says that the English inequality in land is due only to economic causes, or that the drunkenness of England is due only to economic causes, is saying something so absurd that he cannot really have thought what he was saying.

Yet things quite as preposterous as this are said and written under the influence of that great spectacle of babyish helplessness, the economic theory of history. We have people who represent that all great historic motives were economic, and then have to howl at the top of their voices in order to induce the modern democracy to act on economic motives. The most extreme Marxists in England position themselves as a small, valiant minority, desperately trying to convince the world to act according to a theory they claim governs all behavior. The truth is, of course, that there will be a social revolution the moment the thing has ceased to be purely economic. You can never have a revolution in order to establish a democracy. You must have a democracy in order to have a revolution.

I rise from my spot beneath the trees, as the wind and gentle rain have subsided. The trees now stand like golden columns bathed in bright sunlight. The tossing of the trees and the blowing of the wind have ceased simultaneously. So I suppose there are still modern philosophers who will maintain that the trees make the wind.
