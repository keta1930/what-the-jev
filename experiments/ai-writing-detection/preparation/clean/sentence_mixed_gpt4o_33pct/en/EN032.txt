The rain fell steadily as Victor Crummles stepped out onto the pavement. But he did not notice the fact. True, he put his umbrella up, but that was mere force of habit. He was not aware that he had put it up. His mind was far too engaged with the ordeal before him to permit any consciousness of external things to creep into it. He was "up against it and no mistake," he observed to himself. “I’m really in a bind,” he reminded himself, glancing at the piece of paper in his pocket that indicated where and when he was due for a medical examination. Reassuring himself, he confirmed its presence: Kilburn. Twelve o'clock.

Yes, he was fairly up against it. Not, as he hastened to assure himself, that he objected…. Not at all…. His patriotism ran deep, and he felt a strong desire to contribute to the war effort. He longed to take on the Germans and show them what he was made of. If only he had been younger, he thought; he would have enlisted without hesitation. That's what he'd have done—he'd have gone like a shot. No fetching him—if he'd been a bit younger. But at thirty-eight, he felt the weight of his age.

Here was the "Golden Crown." Yes, he thought he'd better have "just one." A drink would bolster his spirits and prepare him for the doctors. After all, he believed he ought to give them a fair chance, regardless of the potential ramifications for himself. A whisky-and-soda would just put him "in the pink."

There, that was better. Now he could face anything. Now for Kilburn. He contemplated his travel options. It was at least a two-mile journey—plenty of distance to cover. There was No. 16—he could take that. And there was the Tube—he could take that.

Or he could walk. There was time to spare. Yes, on the whole he thought he ought to walk. There was that varicose vein. The doctors ought to know about that. It wouldn't be fair to them or to the country that they shouldn't know about it. Varicose veins were serious conditions, and he had done his research. He could recall the definition clearly: It had made such a deep impression on him-that he could repeat what it said:—

“The dilation and thickening of veins, accompanied by lengthening and tortuosity, leading to the formation of knots or knobs, where blood coagulates, fibrin deposits, and sometimes even bone matter develops; additionally, the vein walls are diseased.”

There was more about it than that. It looked a very black case indeed. Many a man had been turned down for varicose veins, and—and—well, the doctors ought to know about it. That was all…. They ought to know about it…. He oughtn't to go there and pass himself off under false pretences…. Mind you, he wanted to fight the Germans all right. He wanted to do his bit—nobody more so. But was it fair not to let the doctors see what was the matter with him? He certainly had those knots and knobs when he walked very hard. Who knew? Perhaps there was "fibrin" and "osseous matter" there. At any rate, the doctors ought to see his leg under fair conditions….

He didn't hold with allowing your patriotism to make you deceive your country. He felt a moral duty to avoid deceiving his country, especially considering the financial resources that would be wasted on someone who might falter early in service. It wasn't fair to the fellow either. Not that he was thinking about himself…. Not at all. It was the country he was thinking of. A fellow must think about the country sometimes. It was his duty to put his own feelings, as it were, under the tap. He wanted to go to the war as much as any man, but he didn't want the country to lose by him….

Yes, he decided, it was his obligation to walk. It was crucial that he disclose the details of his varicose vein. He hoped it wouldn’t be a disqualifying factor, but he was committed to being forthright no matter the outcome. He was not the man to palm himself for what he wasn't. He would show the doctor quite plainly what his varicose vein was like.

When Victor finally entered the examination room, fatigue washed over him, but he steeled himself. He had taken another "stiffener" at the "Spread Eagle" and felt equal to any fate. The room was occupied by two doctors—one seated at a desk, the other standing near a window.

“Anything bothering you?” the seated doctor inquired.

"Not that I know," said Victor with the air of a man who meant business. However, he begrudgingly added, “I do have a bit of a varicose vein, but it’s hardly worth mentioning.”

“Oh, don’t worry about that,” the doctor assured him. “We’ve moved past that point. Now strip."

Don't worry about that! Moved past that point?” He had done his duty by mentioning the condition. If there was indeed fibrin or osseous matter in his veins, he had provided ample warning. It was the country that would suffer. Doctors could be so dismissive sometimes.

"Stripped? Now, let's have a look at you."

The doctor examined him carefully. Perhaps that varicose vein would surprise him after all. He had walked two miles, and it should be noticeable—though he didn’t necessarily wish for it to be. If his condition was significant, it was only fair that the doctors were informed.

“What did you say your age was?” the doctor asked.

"Thirty-eight, sir."

"Thirty-eight! Thirty-eight… um… Jeffkins!” the doctor called to his colleague, who joined him in assessing Victor.

They appeared to disregard his leg entirely. Well, it was their look out. He wouldn't be to blame if he broke down.

"You can dress." And the two doctors went to the window and consulted in low tones.

After a moment, the first doctor returned.

"Well, my man, it won't do," he said. “We admire your spirit… very commendable, indeed. But (laughing) thirty-eight! Come, come."

A realization struck Victor. Was he really being rejected?… And because he was too old?… Oh, the scandal, the shame…. And he dying to get at those Huns….

"But upon my oath…." He was really in earnest now.

"There, there, we understand," said the doctor. “You’ve done your best, and it’s commendable. And it's very creditable to you—very. But thirty-eight! Come, come…. Now, good morning."

Outside, Victor's anguish and indignation were too bitter to be borne unaided. He turned into the "Spread Eagle."
