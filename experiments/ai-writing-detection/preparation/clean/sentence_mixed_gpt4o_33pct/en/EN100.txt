Some time ago I wrote in these columns an article called “The Extraordinary Cabman.” Today, I have the opportunity to recount an even more remarkable experience involving a cab. What made this cab so extraordinary was its distinct aversion to me; it ejected me forcefully right in the middle of the Strand. If my friends who read the DAILY NEWS are as romantic (and as rich) as I take them to be, I presume that this experience is not uncommon. It stands to reason that many of them may have also found themselves unceremoniously tossed from cabs across London. Still, as there are some people, virginal and remote from the world, who have not yet had this luxurious experience, I will give a short account of the psychology of myself when my hansom cab ran into the side of a motor omnibus, and I hope hurt it.

I do not need to dwell on the essential romance of the hansom cab—that one really noble modern thing which our age, when it is judged, will gravely put beside the Parthenon. It is really modern in that it is both secret and swift. My particular hansom cab exemplified these traits, though it met with unfortunate circumstances as well. But it is also English; it is not to be found abroad; it belongs to a beautiful, romantic country where nearly everybody is pretending to be richer than they are, and acting as if they were. It is comfortable, and yet it is reckless; and that combination is the very soul of England. But although I had always realised all these good qualities in a hansom cab, I had not experienced all the possibilities, or, as the moderns put it, all the aspects of that vehicle. My previous assertions regarding the merits of such vehicles were made while they were upright. Therefore, allow me to convey my feelings during that fateful moment when I was unceremoniously ejected from a hansom cab for what I hope is the first and last time. Polycrates threw one ring into the sea to propitiate the Fates. I have thrown one hansom cab into the sea (if you will excuse a rather violent metaphor) and the Fates are, I am quite sure, propitiated. Though I am told they do not like to be told so.

I was driving yesterday afternoon in a hansom cab down one of the sloping streets into the Strand, reading one of my own admirable articles with continual pleasure, and still more continual surprise, when the horse fell forward, scrambled a moment on the scraping stones, staggered to his feet again, and went forward. The horses in my cabs often do this, and I have learnt to enjoy my own articles at any angle of the vehicle. So I did not see anything at all odd about the way the horse went on again. However, a glance at the faces of the pedestrians revealed a stark change; they were transfixed with fear, as if struck by a bolt of lightning. And one man half ran out into the road with a movement of the elbow as if warding off a blow, and tried to stop the horse. Then I knew that the reins were lost, and the next moment the horse was like a living thunder-bolt. I try to describe things exactly as they seemed to me; many details I may have missed or mis-stated; many details may have, so to speak, gone mad in the race down the road. I remember that I once called one of my experiences narrated in this paper “A Fragment of Fact.” This is, at any rate, a fragment of fact. No fact could possibly be more fragmentary than the sort of fact that I expected to be at the bottom of that street.

I am a firm believer in addressing those who already hold certain convictions, for I have often found that they do not fully grasp the depth of their own beliefs.

Thus I have always urged in this paper that democracy has a deeper meaning than democrats understand; that is, that common and popular things, proverbs, and ordinary sayings always have something in them unrealised by most who repeat them. Here is one. We have all heard about the man who is in momentary danger, and who sees the whole of his life pass before him in a moment. In the stark, literal sense, this is an outright fabrication. No one can claim that during a crisis, they meticulously recalled every event, every ticket they ever purchased to Wimbledon, or the countless times they shared brown bread and butter.

But in those few moments, while my cab was tearing towards the traffic of the Strand, I discovered that there is a truth behind this phrase, as there is behind all popular phrases. I did really have, in that short and shrieking period, a rapid succession of a number of fundamental points of view. I went through what might be described as five distinct religions in the span of just a few seconds. My first religion was pure Paganism, which among sincere men is more shortly described as extreme fear. This state was soon succeeded by a mindset that, while real, defies proper nomenclature. The ancients might have referred to it as Stoicism, and perhaps it aligns with what some German philosophers mean when they discuss Pessimism. It was an empty acceptance of whatever fate had in store, as if I had transcended the significance of it all. And then, curiously enough, came a very strong contrary feeling—that things mattered very much indeed, and yet that they were something more than tragic. It was a feeling, not that life was unimportant, but that life was much too important ever to be anything but life. I hope this realization was a reflection of Christianity; at least, it coincided with the moment of our collision with the omnibus.

In what felt like a surreal twist, the hansom cab seemed to topple over me, enveloping me like a massive hood. I then found myself crawling out from underneath it in attitudes so undignified that they must have added enormously to that great cause to which the Anti-Puritan League and I have recently dedicated ourselves. I mean the cause of the pleasures of the people. As to my demeanour when I emerged, I have two confessions to make, and they are both made merely in the interests of mental science. First, just moments before the collision, I had been in a seemingly serene state of mind; however, upon standing up and realizing I had sustained only minor cuts, I began, much like St. Peter, to unleash a torrent of curses. A man offered me a newspaper or something that I had dropped. I can distinctly remember consigning the paper to a state of irremediable spiritual ruin. I am very sorry for this now, and I apologise both to the man and to the paper. I have not the least idea what was the meaning of this unnatural anger; I mention it as a psychological confession. It was immediately followed by extreme hilarity, and I made so many silly jokes to the policeman that he disgraced himself by continual laughter before all the little boys in the street, who had hitherto taken him seriously.

There is one other odd thing about the matter which I also mention as a curiosity of the human brain or deficiency of brain. Every three minutes or so, I found myself reminding the policeman that I had yet to pay the cabman and fervently hoped he would not suffer a financial loss. The officer reassured me that everything would be fine and that the cabman would show up. But it was not until about half an hour afterwards that it suddenly struck me with a shock intolerable that the man might conceivably have lost more than half a crown; that he had been in danger as well as I. I had instinctively regarded the cabman as something uplifted above accidents, a god. I immediately made inquiries, and I am happy to say that they seemed to have been unnecessary.

But henceforward I shall always understand with a darker and more delicate charity those who take tythe of mint, and anise, and cumin, and neglect the weightier matters of the law; I shall remember how I was once really tortured with owing half a crown to a man who might have been dead. Some dedicated gentlemen in white coats at Charing Cross Hospital attended to my minor injuries, after which I stepped back out into the bustling Strand. A sense of unnatural youth enveloped me, igniting a yearning for uncharted experiences. So to open a new chapter in my life I got into a hansom cab.
