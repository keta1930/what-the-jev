On those rare occasions when I put on my best clothes and venture into society, I am always astonished at the number of people in it whom I do not know. I have stood in a crowded ball-room, or sat in a crowded restaurant, and reflected that, of all the hundreds of souls present, there was not one of whose existence I had previously had any suspicion. Yet, each one of them leads a life of tremendous significance—not just to themselves, but also to a multitude of friends and family. Each day, they face their own challenges, and there inevitably comes a moment for each individual when the rest of the world, including—much to my chagrin—myself, seems utterly irrelevant. That I had lived all these years in contented ignorance of their existence makes me a little ashamed.

Today, dressed in my oldest attire, I have perused the index of The Times Literary Supplement, and I find myself grappling with a profound sense of shame over my lack of knowledge regarding numerous books. Of novels alone there seem to be about 900. To write even a thoroughly futile novel is, to my thinking, a work of extraordinary endurance; yet in, say, 600 houses this work has been going on, and I (and you, and all of us) have remained utterly unmoved. Well, I have been making up for my indifference this morning. I have been reading the titles of the books. That is not so good (or bad) as reading the books themselves, but it enables me to say that I have heard of such and such a novel, and in some cases it does give me a slight clue to what goes on inside.

In my estimation, the most enjoyable aspect of crafting a novel must be the selection of a title. My idea of a title is that it should be something which reflects the spirit of your work and gives the hesitating purchaser some indication of what he is asked to buy. To call your book Ethnan Frame or Esther Grant or John Temple or John Merridew (I quote from the index) is to help the reader not at all. All it tells him is that one of the characters inside will be called John or Esther—a matter, probably, of indifference to him. Conversely, a title like Phyllis does hint at the nature of the narrative. No novel with a tragic ending, no powerful realistic novel, would be called Phyllis. I would surmise that this novel is a delightful exploration of suburban life, predominantly conveyed through dialogue, featuring Phyllis as an endearing character, albeit somewhat unkind regarding that initial box of chocolates he sent her. However, she married him in the end all right.

But if you don't call your book Phyllis or John Temple or Mrs. Elmsley, what—I hear you asking—are you to call it? You might consider the title Kapak, as I have noticed someone has done. The beauty of Kapak as a title is that if you come into the shop by the back entrance, and so approach the book from the wrong end, it is still Kapak. A title that reads the same in either direction provides substantial advantage to the author. Besides, in this particular case there is a mystery about Kapak which one is burning to solve. Is it the bride's pet name for her father-in-law, the password into the magic castle, or that new stuff with which you polish brown boots? Or is it only a camera? Let us buy the book at once and find out.

Another mystery title is The Man with Thicker Beard, which probably means something. It is like Kapak in this, that it reads equally well backwards; but it is not so subtle. Still, it would likely entice readers to explore its contents. On the other hand, A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette is just the sort of book to which we would not be tempted by the title. The mere act of asking a shopkeeper, "Do you have A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette?" feels cumbersome. If we were to forget the title and instead inquire about a would-be nightingale and a Welsh suffragette, or perhaps a wood nightingale and a Welsh rabbit, or even the Welsh suffragette's night in jail, we would rapidly regret not opting for something straightforward like Greed, Earth, or Jonah.

And this is why a French title is always such a mistake. Authors must bear in mind that their readers not only need to order the book verbally but also recommend it to their acquaintances. So I think Mr. Oliver Onions made a mistake when he called his collection of short stories Pot au Feu. It is a good title, but it is the sort of title to which the person to whom you are recommending the book always answers, "What?" When faced with such confusion after attempting to pronounce the title in your best Parisian accent, the only recourse is to shift the topic entirely. But it is quite time that we came to some sort of decision as to what makes the perfect title. Kapak, as mentioned, may attract buyers, though some might find it somewhat unfair. While it excels commercially, it does not fulfill our initial criteria; the title must resonate with the book's essence. In one sense Five Gallons of Gasolene does this, but of course nobody could ask for that in a book-shop.

Well, then, here is a perfect title, Their High Adventure. That explains itself just sufficiently. When a Man's Married, For Henri and Navarre, and The King Over the Water are slightly more overt, yet still effective. The Love Story of a Mormon makes no attempt to deceive the purchaser, but it can hardly be called a beautiful title. Melody in Silver, on the other hand, is beautiful, but for this reason makes one afraid to buy it, lest there should be disappointment within. As I scan the index, I find myself relieved by the multitude of novels I have yet to read. In most of them there would be disappointment. And really one only reads books nowadays so as to be able to say to one's neighbour on one's rare appearances in society, "HAVE you read The Forged Coupon, and WHAT do you think of The Muck Rake?" And for this an index is quite enough.
