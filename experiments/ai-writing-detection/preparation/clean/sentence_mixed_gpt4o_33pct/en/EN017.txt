I have often longed to be a grocer. To be surrounded by so many interesting things— sardines, bottled raspberries, biscuits with sugar on the top, preserved ginger, hams, brawn under glass, everything in fact that makes life worth living; at one moment to walk up a ladder in search of nutmeg, at the next to dive under a counter in pursuit of cinnamon; to serve little girls with a ha'porth of pear drops and lordly people like you and me with a pint of cherry gin —is not this to follow the king of trades? Some day I shall open a grocer's shop, and you will find me in my spare evenings aproned behind the counter. Look out for the currants in the window as you come in—I have an idea for something artistic in the way of patterns there; but, as you love me, do not offer to buy any. We grocers only put the currants out for show, and so that we may run our fingers through them luxuriously when business is slack. I have a good line in shortbreads, madam, if I can find the box, but no currants this evening, I beg you.

Indeed, being a grocer seems to promise a fulfilling life; however, I realize that it doesn’t offer a true sense of engaging with the world. A grocer, in as far as it is possible to a man who sells both scented soap and pilchards, would become narrow. Our connection to the outside world is limited, often filtered through the medium of potted lobster, which tells us little about the people we serve. Potted lobster does not define a man. All customers are alike to the grocer, provided their money is good. I perceive now that I was over-hasty in deciding to become a grocer. That path may be more suited for one’s later years. While one is young, and interested in persons rather than in things, there is only one profession to follow—the profession of bookstall clerk.

Working behind a bookstall is, without a doubt, an avenue to genuinely experience life. This realization struck me rather suddenly last Monday as I stood before a station bookstall, pondering who might buy the tie-clips displayed. The answer came to me as I boarded my train—ask the person behind the bookstall; he would certainly know. Yes, and he would know who bought all his papers and books and pamphlets, and to know this is to know something about the people in the world. You cannot tell a man by the lobster he eats, but you can tell something about him by the literature he reads.

For instance, I once occupied a carriage on an eastern line with, among others, a middle-aged woman. The moment we departed from Liverpool Street, she produced a bag of shrimps, gripping each one firmly by the head and tail before consuming it. When she had finished, she emptied the ends out of the window, wiped her hands, and settled down comfortably to her paper. What paper? You may not believe it, but it was The Morning Post. Now doesn't that give you the woman? The shrimps alone wouldn’t tell you much about her, and neither would the choice of newspaper, but together they paint a vivid picture. Just imagine the delight of the bookstall clerk as she approached, bag of shrimps in hand, and requested The Morning Post.

The day can never be dull to the bookstall clerk. I imagine him assigning in his mind the right paper to each customer. This man will surely ask for Golfing—wrong! He wants Cage Birds; that gentleman over there seeks The Motor—ah, The Auto-Car will suffice. Soon he would begin to know the different types; he would learn to distinguish between the patrons of The Dancing Times and of The Vote, The Era and The Athenaeum. Delightful surprises would overwhelm him at intervals; as when—a red-letter day in all the great stations—a gentleman in a check waistcoat makes the double purchase of Homer's Penny Stories and The Spectator. On such occasions, his faith in humanity might waver until he reassures himself that the man is likely an escaped criminal in disguise, perhaps overacting the part. After which he would hand over The Winning Post and The Animals' Friend to the pursuing detective in a sort of holy awe. What a life!

But he has other things than papers to sell. He knows who buys those little sixpenny books of funny stories—a problem which has often puzzled us others; he understands by now the type of man who wants to read up a few good jokes to tell them down at old Robinson's, where he is going for the week-end. Our bookstall clerk doesn't wait to be asked. As soon as this gentleman approaches, he whips out the book, dusts it, and places it before the raconteur. He has also developed an instinct for recognizing the hapless individuals who frequently misplace their indiarubber umbrella rings. From halfway across the station, he can spot them and prepares a new card with a knowing smile. ("Or we would let you have seven for sixpence, sir.") Even when faced with those more elusive characters—those who could be in the market for either a fountain pen with case or the Life and Letters of Major-General Clement Bulger, C.B. at a discount—our clerk is always ready. If his first guess misses the mark, he swiftly recovers with a second try. "Bulger, sir. One of our greatest soldiers."

I thought of these things last Monday, and definitely renounced the idea of becoming a grocer; and as I wandered round the bookstall, thinking, I came across a little book, sixpence in cloth, a shilling in leather, called Proverbs and Maxims. It contained some thousands of the best thoughts in all languages, such as have guided men along the path of truth since the beginning of the world, from "What ho, she bumps!" to "Ich dien," and more. The thought occurred to me that an interesting article might be extracted from it, so I bought the book. Unfortunately enough I left it in the train before I had time to master it. I plan to visit the bookstall again next Monday to acquire another copy. That will be all right; you shan't miss it.

But I am wondering now what the bookstall clerk will make of me. A man who keeps on buying Proverbs and Maxims. Well, as I said earlier, they truly see life.
