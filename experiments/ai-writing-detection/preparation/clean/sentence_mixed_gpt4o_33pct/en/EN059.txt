Recently, while I found myself contemplating the nature of ethics and the figure of Mr. H. Pitt, I was unexpectedly thrust into a jury box, tasked with the responsibility of adjudicating. The snatching took some weeks, but to me it seemed something sudden and arbitrary. I was summoned for this duty simply because I resided in Battersea and my surname started with a C. As I surveyed my surroundings, I noticed that I was not alone; numerous other men, all from Battersea and sharing the initial C, were also present.

It seems that they always summon jurymen in this sweeping alphabetical way. At one official blow, so to speak, Battersea is denuded of all its C’s, and left to get on as best it can with the rest of the alphabet. A Cumberpatch was missing from one street, a Chizzolpop from another, while several Chucksterfields were absent from Chucksterfield House. The children lamented the absence of a Cadgerboy, and a woman on the corner mourned her Coffintop, inconsolable in her grief. We settle down with a rollicking ease into our seats (for we are a bold, devil-may-care race, the C’s of Battersea), and an oath is administered to us in a totally inaudible manner by an individual resembling an Army surgeon in his second childhood. Nevertheless, we understood that our role was to fairly judge the case presented by our sovereign lord the King against the accused, neither of whom had yet appeared.

Just when I was wondering whether the King and the prisoner were, perhaps, coming to an amicable understanding in some adjoining public house, the prisoner’s head appears above the barrier of the dock; he is accused of stealing bicycles, and he is the living image of a great friend of mine. We go into the matter of the stealing of the bicycles. We do well and truly try the case between the King and the prisoner in the affair of the bicycles. After a brief yet reasonable debate, we reached the conclusion that the King had no involvement in this matter. Then we pass on to a woman who neglected her children, and who looks as if somebody or something had neglected her. And I am one of those who fancy that something had.

All the time that the eye took in these light appearances and the brain passed these light criticisms, there was in the heart a barbaric pity and fear which men have never been able to utter from the beginning, but which is the power behind half the poems of the world. The mood cannot even adequately be suggested, except faintly by this statement that tragedy is the highest expression of the infinite value of human life. Never had I stood so close to pain; and never so far away from pessimism. Ordinarily, I would refrain from discussing such dark emotions, as they are challenging to convey; however, I feel compelled to mention them now for a particular reason, which I will outline shortly. I speak these feelings because out of the furnace of them there came a curious realisation of a political or social truth. It became vividly clear to me what the essence of a jury is and why it must never be allowed to perish.

The trend of our epoch up to this time has been consistently towards specialism and professionalism. We tend to have trained soldiers because they fight better, trained singers because they sing better, trained dancers because they dance better, specially instructed laughers because they laugh better, and so on and so on. This principle has been extended to the realms of law and politics, as many modern writers have pointed out. Many Fabians have insisted that a greater part of our political work should be performed by experts. Many legalists have declared that the untrained jury should be altogether supplanted by the trained Judge.

Now, if this world of ours were really what is called reasonable, I do not know that there would be any fault to find with this. But the true result of all experience and the true foundation of all religion is this. That the four or five things that it is most practically essential that a man should know, are all of them what people call paradoxes. That is, although we recognize these truths as self-evident in life, articulating them often leads to apparent contradictions. For example, it is a well-known yet paradoxical truth that those who seek pleasure the most often find it elusive. Another paradox lies in the concept of courage; the idea that fearing death too intensely can ironically lead to its greater likelihood. Whoever is careless enough of his bones to climb some hopeful cliff above the tide may save his bones by that carelessness. Whoever will lose his life, the same shall save it; an entirely practical and prosaic statement.

Now, one of these four or five paradoxes which should be taught to every infant prattling at his mother’s knee is the following: That the more a man looks at a thing, the less he can see it, and the more a man learns a thing the less he knows it. The Fabian claim, which suggests that expertise should drive trust, would be irrefutable if it were true that those who immerse themselves in a subject gradually uncover its deeper significance. But he does not. He goes on seeing less and less of its significance. This phenomenon affects us all, as we often drift away from appreciating the significance of the sky or the stones around us unless we actively cultivate a sense of gratitude and humility.

Now it is a terrible business to mark a man out for the vengeance of men. But it is a thing to which a man can grow accustomed, as he can to other terrible things; he can even grow accustomed to the sun. And the horrible thing about all legal officials, even the best, about all judges, magistrates, barristers, detectives, and policemen, is not that they are wicked (some of them are good), not that they are stupid (several of them are quite intelligent), it is simply that they have got used to it.

In truth, they often do not perceive the defendant in the dock as a unique individual; instead, they view them as just another face in a familiar setting. They do not see the awful court of judgment; they only see their own workshop. Hence, the instinct of Christian civilization has wisely determined that every judgment must be infused with fresh perspectives and insights drawn from the community. Ordinary citizens must be brought in, individuals who can truly see the court, the crowd, the rough features of the police, the faces of the accused, and the rehearsed expressions of counsel, experiencing it all as one would a new painting or an unfamiliar performance.

Our civilisation has decided, and very justly decided, that determining the guilt or innocence of men is a thing too important to be trusted to trained men. It wishes for light upon that awful matter, it asks men who know no more law than I know, but who can feel the things that I felt in the jury box. When it wants a library catalogued, or the solar system discovered, or any trifle of that kind, it uses up specialists. However, when it comes to matters of real significance, twelve ordinary men are summoned from the streets. The same thing was done, if I remember right, by the Founder of Christianity.
