Critics often claim that journalism is overly personal; however, I have consistently found it to be quite the opposite—remarkably impersonal. While journalism is meant to strip away the layers of private lives, it often seems to create translucent but blinding barriers between individuals. The sensationalist press is frequently criticized for revealing private details, yet I wish it would engage in something truly valuable—capturing the unique individual nuances that define a person. A clear illustration of this is the disorienting experience of meeting someone in real life after having encountered them countless times in newspapers; the disconnect can be striking. The sensationalist journalist often fails to grasp the singular, defining traits of a person that shape all subsequent impressions. For instance, prior to meeting George Bernard Shaw, I had heard that he expressed himself with a bold penchant for paradox or a disdain for sentimentality. What I was completely unprepared for was his Irish accent, which I later realized was far more significant than any of the prior critiques.

Journalism lacks a sufficient personal touch. Rather than uncovering the depths of private personalities, it often neglects even the most apparent characteristics that lie on the surface. There exists a vivid and tangible impression we all experience when we encounter great poets or politicians, but this sensation rarely makes its way into news articles. A common experience is the realization that these eminent figures often appear much older than we had previously imagined. We tend to associate greatness with past achievements, which typically occurred years ago; thus, many who idolized the youthful Napoleon of Marengo must have been surprised to encounter the more corpulent Napoleon of Leipzig.

I recall reading a newspaper piece detailing how an emerging politician addressed the House of Lords with a youthful exuberance. It described his “brave young voice” echoing throughout the chamber. Yet, when I met him days later, I was startled to discover that he was significantly older than my own father. I share this observation not merely as an anecdote but to highlight a specific instance: I once met a distinguished individual who was unexpectedly younger than I had anticipated.

My journey began as I traversed the wooded hills surrounding Epsom, following a winding path that led me down towards the valley of Dorking. The warm sunlight filtered through the foliage, casting a golden hue that, though lacking in sanctity, possessed an evening quality. It reminded me that the sun begins its descent almost immediately after reaching its zenith. The light seemed to fade as I ventured deeper into the woods.

As I descended, I experienced a peculiar sensation typical of such tangled paths. The treetops above felt fixed and tangible, akin to the steady level of the sea, while the ground beneath me felt increasingly unstable. Soon, the brilliant sunlight transformed into mere splashes of light, resembling fiery stars scattered in a canopy of green. In this emerald twilight, I observed tree trunks of every conceivable shape and form; it felt as though I had entered a cathedral supported by pillars of both earthly and otherworldly architecture.

Unintentionally, my thoughts began to wander into the realm of the forest's essence, contemplating the philosophy of mystery and energy. The woods embody a combination of vitality and intricacy. They are not at all crude or primitive; rather, they are characterized by a rich delicacy. Unique forms that an artist would painstakingly study or a philosopher would contemplate for years, if discovered in an open field, exist here in a harmonious cacophony. This is not darkness born of deformity but rather an enigmatic darkness suffused with life and perfection. It led me to consider how often humanity has misinterpreted the highest forms of obscurity. People often assert that theology became convoluted due to its lifelessness. However, I believe that if it had been devoid of life, it would not have evolved into something so intricate; it is the living tree that bears an abundance of branches.

As I walked, the trees gradually receded from one another, and I emerged into a field of tall grass alongside a road. I was caught off guard by how late the day had advanced; it felt as though this valley had its own unique sunset. Continuing along the road as directed, I passed through a modest gate that marked the transition from the woods to a garden. There was a certain charm and subtle elegance in the surroundings, a quality that seemed to emanate from the character I was about to meet. It was reminiscent of the ethereal essence described by the old English term “faërie,” a beauty that can never be grasped by those who view the past as merely brutal. It embodied an ancient grace akin to that found in trees. 

As I entered the garden, I spotted an elderly man seated at a table, appearing diminutive in his oversized chair. He had already been afflicted by illness, and both his hair and beard were white—not the cold, heavy white of snow, but rather a light, airy white, almost fierce, resembling thistledown. I approached him closely; he extended his frail hand and, in that moment, I was struck by the surprising youthfulness in his eyes. Here was a great man from the old world who was not merely a statue marking his own grave.

Although he was deaf, he spoke with a torrent of words. He didn’t dwell on the books he had published; he was far too vibrant for that. Instead, he discussed the unwritten works, unveiling a collection of purple bundles filled with stories he had yet to share. He casually asked me to pen one of these narratives for him, as if he were requesting a favor from a neighbor. The tale he proposed was a magnificent and frenzied narrative—a kind of astronomical farce centered around a man racing to the Royal Society with the sole means of averting a planet-destroying comet. Yet, even in the midst of this monumental task, the protagonist stumbled repeatedly over his own weaknesses and follies, losing trains to trivial matters or finding himself incarcerated for brawling. This was merely one of many ideas he had; another, which I only vaguely recalled, revolved around the fall of Parnell, exploring the notion that a genuinely honorable individual might choose secrecy out of a pure desire for self-control.

As I left that garden, I was enveloped in a blurred sense of the infinite possibilities inherent in creative literature. This feeling intensified as I reentered the woods, for a forest is akin to a grand palace with countless intersecting corridors. I truly sensed that I had encountered the essence of creativity, something almost supernatural. I had witnessed what Virgil referred to as the Old Man of the Forest; I had encountered an elf. The trees loomed behind me as I departed, and I have never seen him since; now I know I will not see him again, for he passed away last Tuesday.
