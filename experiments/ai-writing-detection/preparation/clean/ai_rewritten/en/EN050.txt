After years of battling against oppression, I find myself tethered to a different kind of bondage: I now possess a telephone.

While there are numerous benefits to having this device, it has also deprived me of one of life's rarest and most delightful experiences—the thrill of making a three-pence call from a public call box without having to pay. Over the years, this extraordinary occurrence has happened to me only twice. Just last week, I encountered a wealthy man, typically somber in demeanor, whose face lit up with a triumphant grin as he recounted that it had also occurred to him recently.

On the flip side, owning a telephone offers me the opportunity to avoid one of life's most common and tiresome frustrations: the excruciating wait outside a call office while the person inside engages in a conversation that is not only trivial but seems to stretch on indefinitely. In London, these call offices serve both men and women; however, in the suburbs, they are predominantly frequented by women, who may appear to be romantically involved but are often just reminding their husbands to pick up the fish. Nonetheless, having my own telephone does not completely eliminate the agony of waiting. If a fairy godmother were to appear before me—though, after all these years, I am not particularly optimistic about such an occurrence—and grant me a wish, I would deliberate long before settling on anything more significant than reclaiming all the time I’ve wasted with the telephone pressed to my ear, awaiting a response. The usual waits can feel lengthy, but nothing quite compares to the sensation of eternity when one sits at the device while someone is fetched from another part of the building. This experience is not just a glimpse of eternity; it feels like a descent into despair, for there is nothing to occupy the mind. When left to idle, time drags on interminably. Even if a book happens to be at hand, reading is impossible as one’s thoughts cannot wander freely; all that occupies the mind is the anxious speculation about the messenger's progress, the current activity of the person being sought, and the potential journey they are undertaking to reach the receiver. In this preoccupation, minutes, hours, days, and even weeks appear to stretch out in reluctant agony.

You can also imagine the mindset of the person being summoned. Despite the telephone's ubiquity, it remains steeped in an air of formality. I observe that men called to the phone by page boys in restaurants and hotels adopt a notably important gait suited to the occasion. 

Owning a telephone undoubtedly simplifies certain aspects of life, but it also introduces numerous complications, even if one adheres to the sound principle of being more often called than calling. One such complication arises from the confusion stemming from delays and misunderstandings, particularly concerning the nature of the Exchanges. Each of us likely holds a personal conception of what these Exchanges entail. There may have once been photographs in the more enlightening magazines, but I have missed them, and as such, I cling to a vague image of machinery and wire-haired women. A friend describes it more vividly: "A vast building," he explains, "like Olympia, with its roof shrouded in darkness and pale women milling about, spinning tops and blowing penny trumpets." To me, as I mentioned earlier, it resembles Tartarus more than Olympus. Indeed, it is a fitting hell for any life poorly spent, having to endlessly dial numbers only to be met with the most sorrowful phrase known to humanity: "Number engaged."

I yearn to comprehend the entire telephone system. I am curious about how the operators all manage to sound so remarkably similar. I know that women can be quite adept at imitation; the transition of a chorus girl from Brixton to the Savoy restaurant can occur as seamlessly as the shift from dusk to dawn, often accompanied by a change in accent. However, it is astonishing how operators from various Exchanges bear such striking resemblances to one another. They cannot possibly all be the same person. While the wonders of the telephone are miraculous—communicating quietly over wires that traverse the earth beneath the bustling streets of the world is undeniably enchanting—it would be a bit too incredible for a single operator to be omnipresent. Hence, there must be many. Is there a school dedicated to teaching the most refined form of speech along with lessons on how to trill the letter 'R'? Why do they all say "No replay" when they mean "No reply"? And how do they converse at home? It must be quite distressing for their families if they do not temper their speech in more casual settings. The sheer delight that comes when we hear a male voice on the Exchange serves as further evidence that women do not always embody the gentler nature.

But these are scarcely the only mysteries that I wish to unravel. I long to understand how those odd and jarring noises are produced. There is a peculiar tapping sound, akin to a woodpecker in the throes of delirium tremens, that both startles and captivates the ear. How do they create that sound, and are they aware of its impact? Furthermore, why is it that one sometimes overhears conversations on other lines and at other times does not? These snippets are rarely engaging, but occasionally... My pen hesitates as I recall the humiliating oversight—the tragic failure to recognize a tip—that struck me on the morning of June 4th, 1919, otherwise known as Derby Day. On that fateful day, the art—or perhaps the science—of prediction reached a nadir in England, as every prognosticator selected The Panther. At the time, I was merely annoyed to find myself listening to a lengthy exchange between what appeared to be a bookmaker and a client discussing a wager on Grand Parade. This annoyance turned to regret when, after Grand Parade triumphed at 33 to 1, I recognized my earlier interruption as a divine hint—if only I had been perceptive enough to heed it.

Clearly, telephony is not yet a precise science, as evidenced by both personal experience and letters I have read in newspapers. In real life, it falls short of perfection; however, in theatrical productions and American detective novels, it seems flawless. An actor lifts the receiver, states the number, and begins talking right away. If they are on film, their lips move in a manner reminiscent of molten rubber, creating a shifting cavern. Do the rest of us, I wonder, contort our faces into such grimaces when using the telephone? Perhaps I should set up a mirror to find out.

There are numerous amusing anecdotes related to telephones. One of the most compelling tales I know involves a journalist who harbors an exaggerated reverence for worldly success. His employer, a peer, will be referred to as Lord Forthestait, while the journalist will be named Mr. Blank. One day, several members of the newspaper staff were chatting in one of the offices when the telephone rang.

“Mr. Blank, you’re needed at the phone,” said the clerk.

Blank, who was on the verge of heading out for lunch, returned impatiently and seized the instrument. 

“Yes, what is it?” he snapped.

“Is this Blank?” came the response. “Lord Forthestait speaking.”

“Yes, my lord,” Blank replied, instantly adopting an air of utmost deference as he removed his hat.
