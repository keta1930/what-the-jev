Not long ago, I shared a piece titled "The Extraordinary Cabman" in these pages. Today, I have the opportunity to recount an even more remarkable experience involving a cab. What made this cab so extraordinary was its distinct aversion to me; it ejected me forcefully right in the middle of the Strand. If my esteemed readers of the DAILY NEWS possess the romantic spirit (and wealth) that I assume they do, then I suspect this is an experience that is not entirely rare. It stands to reason that many of them may have also found themselves unceremoniously tossed from cabs across London. Yet, for the sake of those few who remain untouched by this particular luxury, I will describe the psychological journey I underwent when my hansom cab collided with a motor omnibus, leaving me to wonder about the fate of my dignity.

The innate charm of the hansom cab is something I need not elaborate on—the quintessential modern creation that, when our era is reflected upon, will be regarded alongside the grandeur of the Parthenon. It embodies modernity in its swiftness and secrecy. My particular hansom cab exemplified these traits, though it met with unfortunate circumstances as well. It is a uniquely English invention, absent in foreign lands, rooted in a beautiful and romantic country where most inhabitants pretend to possess wealth beyond their means. The hansom is both comfortable and reckless, and this juxtaposition encapsulates the very essence of England. Despite my appreciation for the many virtues of a hansom cab, I had yet to confront its full spectrum of possibilities—or, as contemporary expressions go, its various aspects. My previous assertions regarding the merits of such vehicles were made while they were upright. Therefore, allow me to convey my feelings during that fateful moment when I was unceremoniously ejected from a hansom cab for what I hope is the first and last time. While Polycrates tossed one of his rings into the sea to appease the Fates, I have metaphorically cast a hansom cab into the depths, and I trust the Fates are satisfied, even if they do not appreciate being told so.

Yesterday afternoon, I found myself riding in a hansom cab, descending one of the gentle inclines toward the Strand, engrossed in the pleasure and surprise of reading one of my own commendable articles. Suddenly, the horse faltered, stumbled briefly on the rough stones, and then regained its footing. This was a common occurrence for the horses in my cabs, and I had grown accustomed to enjoying my articles regardless of the vehicle's angle. Thus, I thought nothing of the horse's momentary struggle. However, a glance at the faces of the pedestrians revealed a stark change; they were transfixed with fear, as if struck by a bolt of lightning. One man even rushed into the street, his arm raised as if to deflect an impending blow, attempting to halt the horse's advance. It was then I understood that the reins had slipped from my grasp, and in an instant, the horse surged forward like a bolt of lightning. I strive to recount events as they appeared to me, acknowledging that I may have overlooked or misstated numerous details, and that many aspects might have been distorted in the chaos of that wild dash down the street. I once referred to a past experience as “A Fragment of Fact”; this moment, too, qualifies as a fragment of reality, though it is one that I could never have anticipated at the street's end.

I am a firm believer in addressing those who already hold certain convictions, for I have often found that they do not fully grasp the depth of their own beliefs. This has led me to assert that democracy encompasses a meaning that often eludes those who champion it. Ordinary expressions, proverbs, and common phrases frequently harbor insights that many who repeat them fail to recognize. Take, for instance, the notion that a person in imminent danger can witness their entire life flash before their eyes in an instant. In the stark, literal sense, this is an outright fabrication. No one can claim that during a crisis, they meticulously recalled every event, every ticket they ever purchased to Wimbledon, or the countless times they shared brown bread and butter.

Yet, as my cab hurtled toward the bustling traffic of the Strand, I discovered that there is indeed a kernel of truth nestled within that phrase, as is often the case with popular expressions. In those fleeting, shrill moments, I experienced a rapid succession of fundamental perspectives. I went through what might be described as five distinct religions in the span of just a few seconds. Initially, I found myself engulfed in pure Paganism, better known among sincere individuals as sheer terror. This state was soon succeeded by a mindset that, while real, defies proper nomenclature. The ancients might have referred to it as Stoicism, and perhaps it aligns with what some German philosophers mean when they discuss Pessimism. It was an empty acceptance of whatever fate had in store, as if I had transcended the significance of it all. Strangely, this was followed by an intense contrary feeling—that life mattered immensely, and yet it was something beyond mere tragedy. I sensed that life was far too vital to ever be reduced to anything less than life itself. I hope this realization was a reflection of Christianity; at least, it coincided with the moment of our collision with the omnibus.

In what felt like a surreal twist, the hansom cab seemed to topple over me, enveloping me like a massive hood. I soon found myself crawling out from beneath it, in such undignified postures that I must have inadvertently contributed significantly to the cause championed by the Anti-Puritan League, with which I recently aligned myself—namely, the pursuit of the people’s pleasures. Regarding my demeanor upon emerging from beneath the cab, I must confess two things, both shared solely for the sake of psychological inquiry. First, just moments before the collision, I had been in a seemingly serene state of mind; however, upon standing up and realizing I had sustained only minor cuts, I began, much like St. Peter, to unleash a torrent of curses. A man approached, offering me a newspaper or some item I had dropped. I can vividly recall condemning that paper to a fate of irredeemable spiritual ruin. I now deeply regret this outburst, and I extend my apologies to both the man and the newspaper. The reason for this inexplicable anger escapes me, but I mention it as a psychological curiosity. This outburst was swiftly followed by a wave of hilarity; I made so many absurd jokes to the policeman that he succumbed to laughter, much to the chagrin of the little boys in the street who had previously taken him seriously.

There was another peculiar aspect of this incident that I mention as an intriguing facet of human psychology—or perhaps a deficiency thereof. Every three minutes or so, I found myself reminding the policeman that I had yet to pay the cabman and fervently hoped he would not suffer a financial loss. The officer reassured me that everything would be fine and that the cabman would show up. However, it wasn’t until half an hour later that it struck me with a jolt that the cabman might have been at risk of losing more than just a small fare; he had faced danger alongside me. In my mind, I had instinctively elevated the cabman to a status akin to that of a deity, impervious to mishaps. I promptly began to make inquiries and was relieved to learn that my concerns had been unfounded.

From that moment on, I gained a deeper and more nuanced understanding of those who meticulously adhere to the lesser details while neglecting the more significant matters at hand; I, too, felt the weight of guilt for having owed a mere half-crown to a person who might have been injured or worse. Some dedicated gentlemen in white coats at Charing Cross Hospital attended to my minor injuries, after which I stepped back out into the bustling Strand. A sense of unnatural youth enveloped me, igniting a yearning for uncharted experiences. Thus, to mark the start of a new chapter in my life, I climbed into yet another hansom cab.
