My friend once declared that it was his fate to acquire items from the most expensive markets and to sell them—if he managed to sell at all—at the lowest prices. More often than not, after tiring of a piece of art or a decorative item, I found myself compelled to give it away, almost pleading with others to accept it as a personal favor. Yet, an incident occurred recently that deviated so significantly from this pattern that I began to wonder if perhaps my fortunes had shifted, allowing me to become a truly enviable figure—a successful dealer.

The event unfolded during one of my meandering explorations through the quaint curiosity shops in a cathedral city. There, I stumbled across a portfolio filled with watercolor drawings, one of which caught my eye. It bore an uncanny resemblance to the work of Turner, a notion seemingly shared by a previous owner who had inscribed the esteemed artist's name—albeit with the initials jumbled—beneath the artwork.

Curiosity piqued, I inquired, “How much for this?”

The dealer responded, “Well, if it were a genuine Turner, it could command any price. But let’s settle on ten shillings for now. You can have it for that amount, though I won’t mind if you pass; I plan to take it to London next week to seek a professional opinion.”

I considered his words carefully.

“Now, I don’t guarantee its authenticity,” he added.

Without further hesitation, I handed over the ten shillings.

The details of how I ultimately found a buyer willing to pay fifty pounds for that drawing are not the focus of this tale. What truly matters is the internal negotiation I faced. The astonishing reality was that I had made a profit of forty-nine pounds ten shillings, which filled me with elation. However, this newfound success prompted deeper reflections.

I thought about the dealer from that quaint street near the cathedral's west door; it felt right for him to share in this fortune. He had treated me fairly, and I should reciprocate that fairness. It seemed only just to offer him half of the proceeds.

With that in mind, I took a moment to write a brief note. I informed him that the drawing he may remember had turned out to be genuine, and I was pleased to enclose half the earnings, as I believed this was the only honorable course of action.

However, without stamps and with the hour growing late, I decided to hold off on mailing the letter and went to bed.

Around 3:30 a.m., I awoke fully alert and, as was my custom, began to reflect on the many errors of my life, which I seemed to have in abundance. From there, I shifted my focus to my recent fortunate transaction and subjected my decision regarding the dealer to rigorous scrutiny. Why, I pondered, should I give him half? Why indulge in such chivalry? This world hardly favors those who act quixotically. It was my eye that recognized the potential value of the drawing, not his. He had, after all, failed to see its worth. Why reward incompetence? Surely, a more modest gift of ten pounds would suffice.

Sleep eluded me, so I picked up a book of short stories and read one before trying to close my eyes again. Yet, my mind wandered back to the dealer. I questioned the wisdom of sending him ten pounds. Wouldn’t that create a false expectation of fairness among his other customers? He might come to anticipate such gestures regularly, only to be disappointed when they didn’t materialize. Wouldn't that lead to bitterness and a descent into despair for someone who seemed like a kind old man? No, ten pounds was excessive. Five would be more than enough; after all, ten might elevate his expectations unduly.

As I prepared for the day ahead, the dealer occupied my thoughts once more. Why, I mused, should I tarnish my very first financial triumph by giving away a substantial portion of my earnings? Wasn’t that an affront to the Goddess of Business, whoever she may be? Was I not inviting her to overlook me, especially after we had finally established a rapport? History teaches us that the Rockefellers and Vanderbilts achieved their wealth precisely because they refrained from such impulsive acts. If I hoped to thrive in my new role as a fine-art speculator, I needed to rein in my emotions. Five pounds felt ludicrous. Surely, a token of one pound would suffice. It wouldn’t significantly impact me, yet it would be a windfall for the dealer, albeit without causing him harm. A pound would be ample, accompanied by a simple note.

The note conveyed that I had sold the drawing at a profit, allowing me to share a portion of my good fortune, as I held an old and perhaps peculiar belief that such luck should be distributed.

When I arrived at the club for lunch, the envelope containing my note and check was still in my pocket. However, that afternoon, I played bridge so poorly that I was relieved I hadn’t sent it.

Ultimately, I concluded as I tore up the envelope and its contents, that such transactions were simply part of the game. The relationship between dealer and customer is straightforward: the dealer sets his price based on what he believes he can extract, and once the customer pays, they are under no obligation to him. The matter is settled, and the incident should be considered closed.
