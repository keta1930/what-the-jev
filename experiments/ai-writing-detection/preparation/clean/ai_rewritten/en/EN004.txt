There exists a certain enchantment in the woods on Midsummer Day—at least, that is what people say. Titania is said to host her festivities, but I must politely decline any invitation to join her court. On this particular day, I lack the spirit for merriment. I can be as cheerful as anyone else on other celebrations, yet on the longest day of the year, I feel heavy-hearted, burdened by the realization that the evenings are beginning to grow shorter from this very moment onward. We are inevitably heading toward winter.

It is around Midsummer Day that the cuckoo alters his song, fully aware that the finest days are behind him and that soon he will need to depart. I would like to compose an academic piece titled "The Habits of the Cuckoo," but I suspect that by the time I finished, my fondness for him would have waned. Perhaps it is more prudent to understand just one aspect of him: his habit of laying eggs in the nests of other birds—a rather endearing trait—and then to accept him as he is. Ultimately, the only noteworthy aspect of the cuckoo is his charming call of "Cuckoo."

The nightingale embodies sorrow, the thrush sings an unsettling tune about the joys yet to come, the blackbird offers a crisp, cool whistle that resonates best on a February morning, and the skylark soars toward a sky that remains unattainable for humankind. As for the lesser white-throat, his song remains a mystery to me. Yet, the cuckoo represents the joys of the present; he accompanies us on sunlit lawns during summer, serenading us under a brilliant sun in a vibrant world painted in shades of blue and green. I believe only those who are truly happy can hear him. He is always present when we are engaged in delightful activities, and he refrains from singing when the sun is obscured by clouds, only venturing a soft note to himself so as to maintain his melody. Then, with a hearty "Cuckoo!" he announces that warmth and brightness have returned.

But now he is preparing to leave us. Where he goes is a mystery to me, but I imagine him drifting toward a place like Mozambique, a paradise for birds that relish long days. If geography were taught properly in schools, I would know precisely where Mozambique lies and what sort of inhabitants it has. However, one might speculate that the influx of cuckoos and swallows from July to April has made the land so crowded that a stable population cannot exist. It could also be that Mozambique is not the correct place I have in mind, though its name carries a certain avian quality.

The calendar year is poorly arranged. If Mr. Willett were still living, he would surely address this issue. Why must the days start to shorten just as summer reaches its peak? Why can the vicar declare that evenings are getting shorter while we are still enjoying strawberries at tea? At times, I ponder whether it would be more bearable if we swapped the months—if June were labeled August and April were called June. This change would allow us to discuss how unseasonably warm it is for October in what we now refer to as August, making the gradual approach of winter easier to endure. If Midsummer Day fell within such a restructured calendar, we would revel with joy, and the madness of midsummer would not weigh upon us.

Already, the oak trees are beginning to exhibit an autumnal appearance. I have been informed that this is due to a local invasion of caterpillars rather than the inevitable decline of summer, yet it seems rather suspect. Perhaps the caterpillars are aware of the changing season. It strikes me as odd to recall a time when I found joy in caterpillars; I would chase them down suburban streets, bring them home to care for, learn their various names, and assist them in becoming chrysalises, nurturing them in that vulnerable stage as if they were my children. How dear those little creatures were to me back then! Now, however, I regard them as pests, akin to mosquitoes and blight, nuisances of the countryside. I once allowed them to crawl up my arm in those blissful days, yet now I cannot tolerate even the thought of them landing softly in my hair. I would struggle to find the right words to say to a chrysalis now.

There are knowledgeable individuals who can explain all about solstices and zeniths; they can articulate precisely why June 24 is significantly hotter and longer than December 24 in England. I believe (and they would correct me if I am mistaken) that at the equator, days and nights maintain equal lengths. This must complicate social interactions, as one cannot remark, "How quickly the days are lengthening (or drawing in)" when visiting someone there—one might as well stay home. "How stationary the days are remaining" might suffice for a first visit, but I suspect the locals would not appreciate such a reminder. They likely feel that, despite the melancholic nature of Midsummer Day, an unchanging year would be far more unbearable. One can envision the air of superiority from a resident living just a couple of miles from the equator, proudly leading her guests to the furthest reaches of her garden, where the seasons are most variable. It would be a trial to endure her company.

Faced with these thoughts, I refuse to succumb to gloom. I find solace in the notion that if June 25 marks the onset of winter, at least there will be another summer to anticipate. Anything could happen next summer. I imagine a scientist would be taken aback if the sun failed to rise one morning or, after rising, chose not to set. That would not surprise me at all. The truly astonishing aspect is how Nature persists in her routines year after year; any sudden deviation from this pattern would be entirely understandable. When experts assure us with certainty that there will be a solar eclipse in 1921, invisible from Greenwich, do they not harbor any doubts as the date approaches? Do they glance up from their meals at the designated hour, just in case the eclipse is visible after all? Or if they have traveled to Pernambuco or wherever the best view is to be had, do they perhaps wonder… maybe… and tell each other the night before that they were undoubtedly going to Pernambuco for other reasons, such as visiting an aunt?

Perhaps they do not. However, I remain uncertain, and I hold onto the hope that, come next year—or perhaps even this year—the days will continue to lengthen beyond midsummer.
