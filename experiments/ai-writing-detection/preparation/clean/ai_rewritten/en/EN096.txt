Recently, I found myself in a rather precarious situation involving two enthusiastic police officers while wandering in a Yorkshire wood. During my holiday, I had indulged in a medley of enjoyable activities, duties, and explorations—what one might refer to in a more mundane sense as doing nothing. At that moment, I was attempting to throw a large Swedish knife at a tree, practicing what I had hoped would be a useful skill in knife-throwing, reminiscent of the violent escapades found in Stevenson’s novels.

Out of the blue, I was approached by two policemen who seemed to emerge from the very fabric of the forest itself. Their presence, in some inexplicable way, sparked memories of joyful Elizabethan comedies. They bombarded me with questions about the knife, my identity, the reason behind my actions, my address, profession, religious beliefs, opinions regarding the Japanese war, and even the name of my favorite cat. They also accused me of damaging the tree, a claim I regrettably had to refute since I had failed to hit it. Nonetheless, the philosophical implications of this encounter were intriguing. After a lively conversation lasting about thirty minutes, during which I showcased an envelope and an unfinished poem—read with great scrutiny, I hope to some benefit—the elder of the two officers ultimately became convinced of my authenticity. He accepted that I was indeed a journalist for the DAILY NEWS, a revelation that seemed to instill a common fear among those in authority, and acknowledged my residence with certain well-known and affluent individuals in the area.

The leading officer grew increasingly amiable, even claiming to be a reader of my work. Once that admission was made, everything appeared to be resolved. They decided to let me go without further ado.

“But,” I inquired, “what about the tree that has suffered at my hand? You rushed in like gallant knights to save that Dryad, tethered to the ground. You, the noble protectors of humanity, are not deceived by the apparent stillness of nature, a stillness akin to the silence of a waterfall—a profound and unyielding quietude. You understand that a tree is merely a creature anchored to the earth by one leg. You would not permit marauders armed with Swedish knives to spill the verdant lifeblood of such a being. Yet, if that is the case, why am I not in custody? Where are my shackles? Surely, the facts I have provided you—that my name is Chesterton, that I am a journalist residing with the esteemed Mr. Blank of Ilkley—should not alter your assessment of whether I have committed an act of cruelty against this vegetable life. The tree remains damaged, regardless of its proud reflection that it was harmed by a gentleman associated with the Liberal press. The bark will not heal faster simply because the injury was inflicted by someone staying with Mr. Blank in Ilkley. The tragedy of that tree, now a mere shadow of its former grandeur, laid low by my clumsy throw, cannot simply be overlooked due to my temporary association with affluent people. It is astonishing that you lack the authority to detain even the most prominent and fashionable individuals for such a transgression. If that is the case, what prompted your intervention in my actions?”

I directed the latter part of my speech to the silent woods, as the two policemen had disappeared almost as swiftly as they had arrived. It’s entirely possible that they were fairies. If that were the case, their rather illogical understanding of crime, law, and accountability would receive a whimsical explanation; perhaps if I had lingered until moonrise, I might have witnessed small police officers frolicking on the grass, or scuttling about with glow-worm belts, arresting grasshoppers for damaging blades of grass. However, assuming they were indeed genuine policemen, I found myself in a peculiar predicament. I had been accused of something that was either an offense or was not. I was released because I managed to demonstrate my connection to a prominent household. The implication was unsettlingly clear: either throwing a knife in a secluded wood is not inherently wrong, or knowing a wealthy individual somehow exonerates one from guilt. Imagine, for instance, a much poorer individual—a laborer tirelessly seeking work, frequently changing accommodations, possibly even struggling to pay rent. If this person had been overcome with the vibrant allure of the ancient wood and engaged in the same knife-throwing, yet could only describe his last dwelling as a miserable place from which he had been evicted. As I strolled home through a dimming and purplish twilight, I pondered how he would have fared in a similar situation.

Moral reflections abound in this scenario. We English often take pride in our supposed illogicality; while this may not be inherently harmful, it can lead to greater issues. There is no deep spiritual malaise in boasting about one’s flaws, but when individuals begin to brag about their virtues, they become intolerable. However, I must assert that illogicality within our laws or our constitution could pose serious dangers, especially if a significant national vice or collective temptation arises that many exploit amid the disarray. Much like a drunk person requires stringent guidelines, a sober individual may follow their instincts with more freedom.

Consider one of the absurdities in British law—take, for example, the stipulation that a person ceasing to be a Member of Parliament must become the Steward of the Chiltern Hundreds, a title initially intended to curb some banditry in that region. Clearly, such illogicalities carry little weight since there is no substantial incentive to abuse them. Former MPs have no overwhelming desire to hunt down robbers in the hills. However, should there arise a genuine danger that respectable, elderly politicians leaving public office might want to exploit this loophole (perhaps if it were financially beneficial), then dismissing the illogicality as inconsequential would be naive. In such a case, the illogical nature of the law would matter significantly, as it could become a justification for misconduct. Only the most virtuous among us can lead wild lives without consequence.

This dynamic is precisely what occurs in situations involving police inquiries, such as the one I’ve recounted. A deeper national flaw emerges—far greater than the vice of alcohol—namely, the tendency to hold gentlemen in high regard. Snobbishness, much like intoxication, possesses a certain grand allure. Yet it harbors a uniquely insidious quality that often seeps through the actions of even the kindest individuals, those with open hearts and welcoming homes. This pervasive English vice deserves scrutiny, more so than any contagious disease. If one were to distill the essence of the worst and most corrupt elements of England into simple terms, it would not be found in foul language or vulgar disputes. Instead, it resides in the fact that when the best kind of working-class individual seeks to commend someone, they refer to them as “a gentleman.” It never occurs to them to equate that praise with titles such as “marquis” or “privy councillor”—they are merely designating a social rank, not genuinely evaluating a person’s character. This enduring temptation to idolize social status inevitably influences and distorts our policing methods.

In conclusion, we must be vigilant and precise in our reasoning, for we must self-monitor our inclinations. The power of wealth, especially in its most nefarious forms, is on the rise in our contemporary world. A truly good and just society, devoid of such temptations, might not require strict regulations to safeguard against the influence of powerful financiers. However, the absence of this corruption would likely have led to a more decisive response long before.
