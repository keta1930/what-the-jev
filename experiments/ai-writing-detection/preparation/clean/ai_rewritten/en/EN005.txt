For three days and nights, the sea relentlessly battered England, reminiscent of Napoleon's fierce assault at Waterloo. This comparison feels instinctual, as the horizon was filled with waves advancing like a cavalry charge, driven by a shared intent. The ocean surged forth like a wave of armored horses, and upon reaching the land, it unleashed a cacophony akin to cannon fire. I witnessed the most ferocious onslaught at night, where the sea crashed against the shores with the force of an earthquake, sending plumes of white spray into the dark sky. In that moment, one could truly grasp the terrifying might of a wave. I often speak of a wave's furious rush, yet the truly horrifying aspect lies in its grotesque slowness. It hoists its massive weight of water with painstaking effort, reminiscent of a Titan laboring to lift a boulder before letting it slip, shattering it into dust. On that night, the waves appeared not as mere water, but as collapsing walls of a city. The waves initially reared up, seemingly reluctant to strike the earth, as if they aimed to assault the stars instead. For a brief moment, they stood tall in the air, resembling a tower; then, their shape faltered, like a structure destined to collapse. When it finally crashed, it was akin to a powder keg detonating.

I had never encountered a sea like this before. A fierce and suffocating wind swept across the land, one that felt solid enough to lean against like a wall. At any moment, one could imagine objects being uprooted—lamp-posts snapping like green stems, trees spiraling away like feathers. Had I possessed any form, I would surely have been swept away, for I walked along the stone embankment above the turbulent sea, grappling with the notion that it was an invasion of England. However, as I approached a certain point, I was taken aback by the emergence of another sound mingling with the relentless roar of the ocean.

From somewhere behind me, perhaps in a nearby amusement park, a defiant brass band played against the backdrop of nature's chaos. I couldn't identify the band, but judging by the bombastic British imperial airs it performed, it seemed likely to be a German ensemble. Regardless, its energy was undeniable, and as I drew closer, the music began to eclipse the storm. The band played rousing tunes like “Tommy Atkins” and “You Can Depend on Young Australia,” along with many others whose lyrics I didn't know but could imagine to be about “John, Pat, and Mac, With the Union Jack,” or that unwritten piece, “Wait till the Bull Dog gets a bite of you.” Personally, I harbor a strong aversion to Imperialism, yet I find a certain sympathy for Jingoism. There was something profoundly moving about this unyielding yet innocent bravado amid the ferocious threat posed by nature, which sparked two melodies in my mind. It’s easy to adopt a cheerful outlook about England, especially as an optimistic Englishman. However, beneath that triumphant brass, the voice of the invasion, the ominous undertone of the sea, persisted. In a moment of folly, unable to articulate my feelings in prose, I attempted to express them through a poem—though it was a poor one. You might label it “Doubt” or “Brighton.” Perhaps “The Patriot” or “The German Band” would suffice. I would prefer to call it “The Two Voices,” but that title belongs to a far inferior poem. It began like this:

“They say the sun is on your knees A lamp to light your lands from harm, They say you turn the seven seas To little brooks about your farm. I hear the sea and the new song that calls you empress all day long. “(O fallen and fouled! O you that lie Dying in swamps—you shall not die, Your rich have secrets, and strong lust, Your poor are chased about like dust, Emptied of anger and surprise— And God has gone out of their eyes, Your cohorts break—your captains lie, I say to you, you shall not die.)”

As I gathered my thoughts, I remembered that there remains an English countryside untouched by the Imperialists. No matter how much territory the British Empire claims, it will never truly own England. The island itself remains undiscovered, let alone conquered. With renewed vigor, I returned to the two melodies, feeling a deeper appreciation for the first:

“I know the bright baptismal rains, I love your tender troubled skies, I know your little climbing lanes, Are peering into Paradise, From open hearth to orchard cool, How bountiful and beautiful. “(O throttled and without a cry, O strangled and stabbed, you shall not die, The frightful word is on your walls, The east sea to the west sea calls, The stars are dying in the sky, You shall not die; you shall not die.)”

The two resounding noises swirled around me, the perilous call of England intermingling with the louder melody of its tranquility. I acknowledge that the final verse may have taken a somewhat haphazard and abrupt form:

“I see you how you smile in state Straight from the Peak to Plymouth Bar, You need not tell me you are great, I know how more than great you are. I know what William Shakespeare was, I have seen Gainsborough and the grass. “(O given to believe a lie, O my mad mother, do not die, Whose eyes turn all ways but within, Whose sin is innocence of sin, Whose eyes, blinded with beams at noon, Can see the motes upon the moon, You shall your lover still pursue. To what last madhouse shelters you I will uphold you, even I. You that are dead. You shall not die.)”

Yet, much like King Canute's futile attempt, the sea paid no heed to my words, nor did the German band cease its performance for anyone.
