"Have you heard the latest?" 

It was the landlord of the Blue Boar who interrupted me as I walked down the village lane, a meandering path lined with a handful of thatched cottages and several barns. His face radiated excitement, and it was clear he had some significant news to share. As a former soldier, his opinions on the war carry considerable weight in our community. I hadn’t yet caught wind of the news, but the landlord’s broad grin assured me it was something positive.

“Jim has received a commission,” he announced, his tone unmistakably conveying that this development would set events in motion. Jim is his son, a sergeant-major in the artillery, who has been stationed at the front since the early days of the conflict.

The announcement sent ripples of excitement through the village. However, we’ve become somewhat desensitized to such news, as this year has delivered an avalanche of remarkable occurrences. Just recently, thousands of soldiers from a large encampment located ten miles away descended upon our area, engaging in an intense two-day battle in the surrounding hills. They trampled through hedgerows, camped in the cornfields, damaged the apple trees in my orchard, and even commandeered tea from the local residents, transforming our quiet village into a scene of chaotic thrill.

“I’ve never seen anything like it in my life,” old Benjamin remarked to me later that evening. “I’ve lived in that bungalow for eighty-five years come Michaelmas, and I’ve never witnessed anything like this before. Yes, eighty-five years come Michaelmas. My father had that land on a peppercorn rent, and the way he lost it was like this—”

Just as Benjamin began his familiar tale of grievance, a sudden commotion among the soldiers provided me with an escape from his recounting.

Who could have imagined that we would someday hear French spoken casually in our village? Yet here we are. At the far end of the lane, a family of Belgians has settled into a small cottage, remnants of the tide of refugees that followed the turmoil of 1914. Their experiences have brought the harsh realities of war uncomfortably close, having fled the horrors of Louvain, where they hid in cellars for days while two of their children lost their lives, ultimately escaping to the coast on foot.

Every Sunday evening, you can see them bustling about, carrying their meager possessions into a nearby barn where Monday morning mass is held. A priest travels ten miles in a country cart to lead the service, gathering refugees from the surrounding area for worship. After the mass, there’s a lively exchange in French and Flemish, filled with laughter and joy.

Old Benjamin, however, is not fond of priests and harbors deep suspicions towards foreign languages. Nevertheless, the Belgians have woven themselves into the fabric of our community, and their children are starting to learn English at the local school in the valley.

In stark contrast, the sentiment towards the family residing next to the Blue Boar is far less welcoming. They are the wife and children of a German man who had worked in England for years but is currently in America. The woman is English and quite pleasant, yet the mere presence of anything German weighs heavily on the village, particularly for the landlord, whose disdain for Germans is palpable.

“I pray there will be no prisoners taken after this,” he mutters darkly whenever he hears of a new atrocity. “Vermin—that’s what they are,” he states indignantly. “They should be dealt with accordingly.”

Germans have become the go-to term for expressing disdain, even for gentle David, the laborer. He came into my orchard one evening, struggling under the weight of a fifteen-foot ladder. We had agreed that if we wanted to salvage the pears before the wasps destroyed them, we needed to act fast.

“It’s a wonderful crop,” David remarked. “I’ve known this pear tree for twenty-five years, and I’ve never seen it yield this much before.”

Once he climbed the ladder, he began picking the fruit. “Well, would you look at that!” he exclaimed. “Those wasps have already been at them.” He tossed down pear after pear, each one riddled with holes where the wasps had feasted near the stem. “Regular Germans—that’s what they are,” he grumbled. “Look at them around that hive! They’ll take all the honey and leave the bees to starve. That’s what they’ll get… Honestly, I can’t fathom what wasps and Germans are even for. It’s beyond me.”

I admitted that I found it perplexing as well. Then, while I held the base of his ladder steady, David opened up about his unease regarding enlisting.

“Most of the lads around here have gone off,” he said. “I don’t like being left behind. It feels as if you’re shirking your duty. It’s not that I wouldn’t want to go, but… (Ouch! I just got stung by a wasp) … there are so many women relying on me. I’ve got my wife, my mother down the village, and my aunt; not a single man to help them but me. After I finish my work on the farm, I tend to all three gardens and a patch of allotment down in the valley as well.”

“You’re producing a lot of food, and that’s essential work for the war effort,” I suggested.

He seemed to take heart from my words and asked if I’d like to see the potatoes he had harvested that evening—they were, as he put it, “a wonderful fine lot.”

So after he finished with the pear tree, he hoisted the ladder onto his shoulder, and we strolled down to David’s garden. There, I was greeted by rows of potatoes, some drying in the open air while others were neatly packed in sacks. Alongside them were marrows, beans, cabbages, and lettuces. He shyly offered me some of the largest potatoes, saying it was just a hobby of his.

As I departed into the twilight, my arms laden with potatoes, I encountered the landlord of the Blue Boar, his shirt sleeves rolled up as usual, showcasing his strong, tanned arms.

“Bad news about Mrs. Lummis,” he remarked, glancing toward the cottage across the road.

“What is it?” I asked, concerned. “Her son?” It had been two months since we last heard from him.

“Yes, poor Jack. She received word that he was killed near La Bassée in June. A nice fellow—and her only son.”

Then, shifting his tone to a more optimistic note, he added, “But Jim is coming home tomorrow. He’s going to get his officer’s uniform sorted out and take a break—the first one he’s had since he left a year ago.”

“You must be eager to see him,” I said.

“Absolutely,” he replied, his smile vast and infectious.
