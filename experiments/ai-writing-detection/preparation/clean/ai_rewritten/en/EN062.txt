Upon my arrival at the station, my hostess and her daughter greeted me with a pony-cart, and we set off at a leisurely pace, engaging in conversation. They posed numerous questions about London and the so-called wicked world, and I did my best to respond.

It was a season of early summer, bright and vibrant; the sky was a clear blue, the grass and hedges seemed to grow audibly, the birds filled the air with their melodies, and the sun shone brilliantly. To ease the journey, I ascended a couple of hills, though I felt little enthusiasm for the exertion.

Just after reaching the summit of the last hill, I settled back into my seat, which risked lifting the pony off the ground, when suddenly, both ladies emitted a sharp cry of alarm.

"Look!" they exclaimed in unison. "There’s Bunty!"

I turned my gaze to find a small West Highland terrier in the road, her coat as white as a freshly washed rag after a dip in a muddy ditch.

"Bunty! You naughty dog!" they scolded. "How could you go off hunting?"

In response to their reprimand, Bunty simply ducked under a nearby hedge, seeking a sliver of shade, appearing both exhausted and cautious.

"My goodness, how naughty she is!" my hostess lamented. "I left her inside the house."

"Yes," her daughter added, "if she’s going to go hunting like this, we’ll have complaints from everyone. She has never done this before."

"C’mon, Bunty!" my hostess called in the sweet tones of a dog owner who believes their pet will respond to any entreaty. But Bunty remained motionless.

"Perhaps if we drive a little further, she’ll follow us," suggested the daughter, and we moved forward a few yards, but Bunty did not budge.

We halted again, making coaxing sounds that might soften the resolve of stone, yet Bunty remained steadfast in her refusal to move.

"I think she’ll come along eventually," I proposed.

"Oh no," her elderly mistress replied, "we can’t leave her here when she has never wandered off alone before. Bunty! Bunty! Don’t be such a naughty girl. Come on, dear little Bunty."

Yet Bunty simply stared at us through her tangled fur, remaining utterly still.

I wasn't particularly fond of strange dogs, but with my hostess unable to move quickly and her daughter at the reins, it felt inevitable that I would need to intervene and retrieve the misbehaving terrier.

"Would you mind?" my hostess asked. "I assure you, she won’t bite. She’s a sweetheart."

Forcing myself to forget the potential pain inflicted by the snap of a dog's jaws, I crept toward Bunty, murmuring soft words in an attempt to win her over.

As I approached, she flopped over onto her back, raised her paws in surrender, and appeared to plead for mercy. I had no trouble picking her up and carrying her back to the cart.

Her mistresses welcomed her back with enthusiasm, masked by the pretense of reproach, and we were about to continue on our way when an agitated voice called out for us to stop. A formidable-looking woman, with a very red face and a purple parasol, confronted us.

"What," she panted, "is the meaning of this? How dare you take my dog?"

"Your dog, madam?" I began to explain.

"It’s useless to deny it," she interjected. "I saw you pick her up and carry her to the trap. This is outrageous. I will report you to the police."

At that moment, I had to admit, the dog was displaying signs of delight and recognition that had been absent before.

"But—" my hostess started, not one for conflict.

"We ought to recognize our own dog when we see her," the daughter chimed in, not shying away from a confrontation.

"Certainly," replied the furious woman, "if you actually have a dog of your own."

"Of course we do," the daughter asserted. "We have a West Highland terrier named Bunty."

"This is my West Highland, named Wendy," the woman declared coldly, "as you can see by the collar. My name is also on there—Miss Morrison, 14 Park Terrace, W. I’m staying at Well House Farm."

And indeed, it was true.

I was on the verge of pointing out that collars can be swapped easily and thus are not definitive proof of ownership, but I thought it wise to let that suggestion arise from elsewhere.

"It’s quite curious," the daughter remarked, scrutinizing the dog closely. "If this isn’t Bunty, it’s her exact double."

"It is not Bunty, but Wendy," Miss Morrison replied frostily. "I would appreciate it if you would return her to me."

"But—" the daughter began again.

"Yes, please return the dog to the lady," the mother intervened.

In the unfortunate absence of Solomon, who would have surely divided the little creature in two, we had no choice but to comply, and the two departed, the dog clearly delighted in the company.

Meanwhile, the daughter urged the pony forward, and we soon passed through the gates.

Awaiting us in the driveway was a West Highland terrier named Bunty.

"There!" the ladies exclaimed as they jumped out and rushed toward her.

"Of course, she doesn’t resemble that Wendy creature at all," the mother noted.

"Now that I take a closer look, I can see numerous differences," the daughter added.

"Nevertheless," I interjected, "you’ve transformed an honest man into a thief—a dog thief, no less—and I demand compensation."

"Indeed," the mother replied, "this is truly unacceptable. What reparation can we offer?"

Although my feelings were not entirely assuaged, the fine Clicquot 1904 that replaced claret at dinner that evening was certainly enjoyable.
