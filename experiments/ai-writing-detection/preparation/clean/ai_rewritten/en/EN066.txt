A railway station is a fascinating venue, though Ruskin had a different perspective; he viewed it with disdain, perhaps because he embodied modernity even more than the station itself. His modernity manifested as a sort of restlessness, irritability, and an almost mechanical urgency akin to a steam engine. This mindset prevented him from appreciating the tranquil, almost sacred atmosphere that a railway station can offer.

He remarked, “In a railway station, you are in a hurry, and therefore, miserable.” Yet, one need not feel this way unless they share Ruskin's modern anxieties. A true philosopher approaches the notion of arriving just in time for a train with a sense of humor or as a playful wager. 

In my experience, the best method for catching a train is to arrive late for the preceding one. If you adopt this approach, you might discover that a railway station possesses a serenity akin to that of a cathedral. The structural grandeur echoes that of significant religious edifices, with lofty arches, expansive spaces, and vibrant stained glass that usher in light. Most importantly, there is a rhythm and a sense of ritual that pervades the station. It celebrates the primal elements of water and fire, which are central to human rituals. Moreover, a railway station aligns more closely with ancient religions than contemporary ones in that it draws people in. It is worth noting that places frequented by the masses tend to preserve traditions far better than those frequented by the elite. The habits of everyday people don’t change as rapidly or thoroughly as those of the fashionable few. Ruskin might have unearthed more echoes of the Middle Ages in the London Underground than in the ostentatious hotels that stand nearby. The grandiose establishments built for the wealthy often bear names that reflect their pretentiousness. They can be elitist, like the Hotel Cecil, or, worse, cosmopolitan, like the Hotel Metropole. However, when I travel in a third-class carriage from the local circle station to Battersea and then to the circle station near the DAILY NEWS, the names of the stations resonate with solemn historical significance. Departing from Victoria, I pass through a park dedicated to St. James the Apostle, then on to Westminster Bridge, which evokes the grandeur of the Abbey; Charing Cross, a nod to Christian symbolism; a station named Temple; and Blackfriars, which harkens back to a medieval vision of brotherhood.

If you seek to uncover the past, simply follow the myriad footsteps of the crowd. At worst, the uneducated merely wear away old relics through their passage, while the educated may destroy them through their cultural disdain.

As I meander about the deserted railway station, with no specific purpose, I find immense satisfaction. I've extracted a multitude of chocolates from vending machines, procured cigarettes, toffees, and even scents that do not appeal to me, all from the same automated devices. I’ve even weighed myself, delighting in the results, and this experience reinforces my belief in the enduring relevance of popular culture. Meandering to the bookstall, I find my faith intact, even amidst the chaotic landscape of contemporary literature and journalism. Despite its more garish and clamorous elements, I still favor the popular over the pretentious and elitist. If forced to choose between subscribing to the DAILY MAIL and the TIMES—a decision that feels akin to a nightmare—I would unequivocally choose the DAILY MAIL. Even the triviality of its grandiosity is less irksome than the pettiness that is often cloaked in solemnity. People buy the DAILY MAIL yet harbor skepticism about it. In contrast, the TIMES garners belief, albeit with fewer sales. However, upon examining the modern literary output, it becomes increasingly clear that beneath its surface lies a timeless human essence, reminiscent of the name Charing Cross. Should you linger at a station bookstall for a few hours—as I often do—you might find that it gradually acquires the monumental significance akin to that of the Vatican or the Bodleian Library. The novelty is merely a façade; the tradition runs deep and is profoundly rooted. The DAILY MAIL may produce new editions, but it lacks truly original ideas. Ultimately, every article that strays from the age-old human emotions of faith or homeland circles back to the age-old human fascination with gossip. Modern writers often mock ancient chronicles for their tendency to recount bizarre events, such as lightning striking a church or the birth of a six-legged calf. They fail to recognize that such archaic tales are not obsolete; rather, they manifest in today's democratic journalism, where the savage chronicle has simply evolved to appear in our daily news.

As I drift in front of the bookstall, a striking title catches my eye, and for a brief moment, it astonishes me. The bold letters proclaim, “Get On or Get Out.” This title jolted me into recognition of a contemporary phenomenon that I find utterly distasteful: a glorification of success that equates to outshining others in any endeavor. Such a notion could imply triumph in fleeing from a battle or being the most accomplished sleeper in a row of slumbering men. Upon seeing those words, the sacred aura of the railway station momentarily dimmed. I noted that this phrase embodied a disturbing form of violent individualism prevalent in our society. In a fit of frustration and resolve, I made the ill-advised choice to purchase the book, thus transferring some of my funds to what I viewed as an adversary. I opened it, bracing myself for the expected brutality and blasphemy that would contradict the generally serene atmosphere of the station. I anticipated finding something within its pages that mirrored the infamy suggested by its title.

To my dismay, the content failed to live up to the fierce decisiveness implied on the cover. After a careful reading, I found myself unsure whether I was meant to “get on” or “get out,” yet I felt a vague inclination to choose the latter. A notable section of the book, especially towards the conclusion, delves into the life of Napoleon Bonaparte. Clearly, Napoleon made strides in life; he certainly “got on” and also “got out.” However, I struggled to grasp how the anecdotes presented in this text were meant to guide someone on the path to success. One story recounted how Napoleon habitually wiped his pen on his knee-breeches, suggesting the moral might be that doing so would lead to victory at the Battle of Wagram. Another tale described him releasing a gazelle among the ladies of his court, implying that one could achieve the status of Emperor of the French by similar means. The message seemed to be to either “get on with a gazelle or get out.” Ultimately, the book made peace with the tranquil ambiance of the station. I recognized a symbolic dichotomy that could be likened to biological classifications: courageous individuals resemble vertebrates, possessing a tender exterior and a robust interior. In contrast, today’s fearful souls resemble crustaceans—hard on the outside but soft within. Yet, within this twilight space, that softness is omnipresent.
