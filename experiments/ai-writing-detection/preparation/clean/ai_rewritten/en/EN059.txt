Recently, while I found myself contemplating the nature of ethics and the figure of Mr. H. Pitt, I was unexpectedly thrust into a jury box, tasked with the responsibility of adjudicating. This abrupt transition unfolded over several weeks, yet to me, it felt sudden and almost capricious. I was summoned for this duty simply because I resided in Battersea and my surname started with a C. As I surveyed my surroundings, I noticed that I was not alone; numerous other men, all from Battersea and sharing the initial C, were also present.

It appears that jurors are consistently summoned in this sweeping alphabetical manner. With one official decree, Battersea was stripped of all its C’s, leaving behind a community to fend for itself with the remaining letters of the alphabet. A Cumberpatch was missing from one street, a Chizzolpop from another, while several Chucksterfields were absent from Chucksterfield House. The children lamented the absence of a Cadgerboy, and a woman on the corner mourned her Coffintop, inconsolable in her grief. We settled comfortably into our seats, for we, the C’s of Battersea, are a bold and carefree lot. An oath was administered to us in a manner that was completely inaudible, delivered by a man resembling a retired Army surgeon. Nevertheless, we understood that our role was to fairly judge the case presented by our sovereign lord the King against the accused, neither of whom had yet appeared.

Just as I began to wonder if perhaps the King and the defendant were engaged in some amicable discussion at a nearby pub, the defendant’s head finally emerged above the dock’s barrier. He was accused of bicycle theft, and to my astonishment, he bore an uncanny resemblance to a close friend of mine. We delved into the particulars of the bicycle theft, diligently weighing the evidence as we were charged to do. After a brief yet reasonable debate, we reached the conclusion that the King had no involvement in this matter. We then turned our attention to a woman accused of neglecting her children, who appeared to be a victim of neglect herself.

As my eyes took in these fleeting impressions and my mind processed these superficial judgments, a deeper, almost primal sense of pity and fear stirred within me—an emotion that has eluded articulation since the dawn of humanity and serves as the driving force behind much of the world’s poetry. This mood is difficult to express, yet it can be faintly captured by the notion that tragedy is the ultimate testament to the profound value of human existence. I had never been so close to suffering and yet so distant from despair. Ordinarily, I would refrain from discussing such dark emotions, as they are challenging to convey; however, I feel compelled to mention them now for a particular reason, which I will outline shortly. These feelings led me to a unique realization concerning a social and political truth. It became vividly clear to me what the essence of a jury is and why it must never be allowed to perish.

Our contemporary era has been characterized by a relentless movement toward specialization and professionalism. We have trained soldiers who fight more effectively, singers who hit the right notes, dancers who captivate audiences, and even specialized laughers who bring joy more skillfully. This principle has been extended to the realms of law and politics, as many modern writers have pointed out. Numerous Fabians advocate for a significant portion of our political endeavors to be handled by experts, while various legal theorists argue for the complete replacement of untrained juries with trained judges.

If our world were genuinely rational, there might be no real objections to this trend. However, the true outcome of all experience and the foundation of all religious thought reveal another truth: the few essential things a person ought to grasp are paradoxical in nature. That is, although we recognize these truths as self-evident in life, articulating them often leads to apparent contradictions. For example, it is a well-known yet paradoxical truth that those who seek pleasure the most often find it elusive. Another paradox lies in the concept of courage; the idea that fearing death too intensely can ironically lead to its greater likelihood. Those who dare to climb a perilous cliff may, through their very recklessness, escape injury. The adage “whoever will lose his life shall save it” embodies this practical truth.

Among these critical paradoxes that should be imparted to every child from a young age is the following: the more attentively a person examines something, the less they may perceive its essence, and the more knowledge one acquires about a subject, the less they may truly understand it. The Fabian claim, which suggests that expertise should drive trust, would be irrefutable if it were true that those who immerse themselves in a subject gradually uncover its deeper significance. In reality, the opposite often occurs; as one studies intensely, they may lose sight of the very essence they initially sought to grasp. This phenomenon affects us all, as we often drift away from appreciating the significance of the sky or the stones around us unless we actively cultivate a sense of gratitude and humility.

It is a grave matter to single out an individual for the wrath of society. Yet, like many terrible realities, one can become accustomed to it, much like one can acclimatize to the sun's glare. The disheartening aspect of legal officials—judges, magistrates, barristers, detectives, and police officers—is not necessarily that they are malevolent (for some possess goodness) or that they lack intelligence (as several are quite astute); rather, it is that they have grown accustomed to the harsh realities of their roles.

In truth, they often do not perceive the defendant in the dock as a unique individual; instead, they view them as just another face in a familiar setting. They do not see the gravity of the court as it truly is; they merely recognize their workplace. Hence, the instinct of Christian civilization has wisely determined that every judgment must be infused with fresh perspectives and insights drawn from the community. Ordinary citizens must be brought in, individuals who can truly see the court, the crowd, the rough features of the police, the faces of the accused, and the rehearsed expressions of counsel, experiencing it all as one would a new painting or an unfamiliar performance.

Our society has justly resolved that deciding the guilt or innocence of individuals is too crucial a matter to be entrusted solely to trained professionals. It seeks clarity on this profound issue and calls upon those who possess no more legal knowledge than I do but who can resonate with the emotions I felt while seated in the jury box. When the task at hand involves cataloging a library or exploring the solar system, specialists are employed. However, when it comes to matters of real significance, twelve ordinary men are summoned from the streets. This approach, as I recall, was also reflected in the teachings of the Founder of Christianity.
