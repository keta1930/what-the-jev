Aldenham's pond, situated a short stroll from the house, is easily accessible via a well-maintained gravel path, allowing visitors to reach its edge without getting muddy, regardless of the weather. This makes it possible to gauge the previous night's rainfall by observing the water level. Some irreverently refer to it as the "hippopotamus pond," likening it to the habitat of a hippopotamus at the zoo, where the creature often remains submerged, hidden from view. Yet, for most of us, it is simply known as "the pond," a name that conveniently overlooks the presence of other nearby natural ponds and attributes its existence solely to human endeavor. In truth, it is a modest, man-made structure crafted from cement, lacking any ostentation.

The pond features seven steps, each measuring 10 inches in height, which unintentionally transform it into an effective rain gauge. When the water covers three steps, as it did last Monday, it indicates that approximately 40 inches of rain have accumulated since the pond began filling last month. This fact may astonish newcomers, but it is crucial to reveal the underlying secret: much of the surrounding land drains into the pond as well. This offers a more accurate reflection of rainfall than the official measurements reported in the newspapers. After a day of disrupted cricket due to rain, it feels absurd to be told that only .026 of an inch caused the chaos; one yearns for a more dramatic figure. The pond's record of an additional 5 inches of rain brings a sense of comfort, whereas the dry statistics from a conventional rain gauge would likely frustrate us. It speaks volumes about Aldenham's insightful perspective that he designed the pond with this understanding in mind.

In any country house, it is essential to have a designated meeting area where those who have spent their mornings writing letters can converge with others who prefer not to. I find it difficult to write correspondence in someone else's home, so once my pipe is lit, I often suggest to Miss Robinson—or whoever is present—"Let's go and check on the pond." With a cheerful agreement, she sets aside the Financial Supplement of The Times, the only part of the paper left to women after the initial scramble for cricket news. Together, we make our way to the pond, where we might encounter Brown and Miss Smith. "A lot of rain last night," remarks Brown. "It was only just over the third step after lunch yesterday." This sparks a friendly debate, with Miss Robinson convinced she stood on the second step that morning, while Miss Smith insists it appears unchanged from her perspective. Gradually, more friends join us, and we all take turns measuring. The consensus is that the rainfall has been significant, with 43 inches recorded over the past three weeks—surely a record. With that settled, we turn our thoughts to potential activities: lawn tennis, golf, croquet, or something else entirely, as we finalize our plans for the day.

Being outdoors facilitates the organization of our day’s agenda; the fresh air inspires action, and the sight of the newly marked tennis court evokes a desire for redemption following a prior defeat. Indoors, however, it is all too easy to sink into a sofa after breakfast, becoming reluctant to leave until lunch. For someone inclined to laziness, an invitation to play may be met with refusal. Yet, if you suggest a stroll to see the pond, even the most lethargic individual is likely to accept the offer for a bit of gentle movement. Once outside, they’re committed.

This pond serves its purpose splendidly during those idyllic summer days with intermittent sunshine, but it proves equally advantageous during relentless rainstorms that sweep in from morning to night. On such dreary days, even the most riveting books and the best company can fail to hold our interest for long. If our hostess were to glimpse us donning umbrellas and heading out, she'd undoubtedly find it peculiar. "Where are you headed?" she might inquire, and we could only respond awkwardly, "Oh, I was just thinking of... taking a stroll." Instead, we cheerfully announce, "I'm off to see the pond; it must be nearly full. Would you care to join me?" With any luck, she might accompany us. It brings us some solace to know that the torrential rain is ultimately contributing to the pond's fullness. We eagerly anticipate the moment when it finally reaches capacity. What will happen then? Aldenham may have a plan, but we, as guests, can only speculate. Some believe it will merely spill over onto the adjacent paths and into the kitchen garden, while I hold out hope for something more spectacular. A person with such an expansive and welcoming attitude towards rain gauges surely has something grand in mind for the occasion. I imagine a celebration of sorts, perhaps involving an outdoor play or a tank drama. Regardless, we remain optimistic that Aldenham will quickly drain the pond so we can witness its filling once again.

I must admit that he has been fortunate in his timing for establishing the pond. Nevertheless, as it stands, there are now 45 inches of rain in it, amounting to the same total that has fallen over the last three weeks. I believe it is high time for some action regarding this situation.
