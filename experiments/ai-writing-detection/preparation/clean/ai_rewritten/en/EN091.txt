This morning, the postwoman delivered a small publication titled The Superman, which piqued my interest with its focus on celestial matters, palmistry, and similar enigmas. From what I've gathered, the piece discusses the exploits of “Althea,” a conventional clairvoyant, along with other visionaries who have purportedly traveled to other planets—naturally, in their astral forms—to investigate various facets of the ongoing war. Althea and her fellow seers appear to have been quite busy exploring the cosmos, engaging with its inhabitants about the conflicts plaguing our world. They seem to believe they have uncovered profound truths. One of the more striking claims is that there is discord between Lucifer and Arniel. Lucifer, described as a fallen planetary deity, has been exiled from his position of power on Jupiter due to his insatiable thirst for dominion. According to this narrative, he is the malevolent force manipulating the Kaiser and aims to seize our planet to hand it over to Jupiter, ultimately with the goal of extinguishing the Solar Logos.

I remain uncertain about who sent me this publication or their intentions, but I must express that it is an utter squandering of postage and resources. I hope I am not perceived as intolerant towards others' beliefs, yet I must admit that discussions around astrology, palm readings, and similar topics cause me to retreat into silence. I am not referring to charlatans who intentionally exploit the gullibility of others; rather, I am speaking of earnest believers—such as my dear friend W.T. Stead, a fascinating blend of sagacity and fanciful thinking. He could astonish you with his insightful analysis of significant events, only to leave you dumbfounded with some extraordinary tale of spirit visitation or cosmic conspiracies that he regarded as genuinely tangible, as real as the ground beneath his feet.

I find myself ill-equipped to navigate this realm of enigmas. It’s not that I lack an appreciation for the emotions that give rise to such beliefs; I share in that yearning. Thoreau once stated he would give everything he owned for “one true vision,” and as long as we remain spiritually engaged, we all harbor some expectation that the veil will lift, revealing the deeper significance of our peculiar journey. For millennia, humanity has wandered through this wilderness of existence, pondering our purpose, our destiny, and the essence of our reality. The puzzle seems particularly intricate now, as we are embroiled in the act of slaughtering one another. Throughout history, there have always been voices proclaiming, “Look here!” or “Look there!” inviting others to witness their interpretations of the riddle and claiming to have deciphered the secret at the heart of our vast mystery.

As long as humanity has the capacity to think and feel, this quest for understanding will persist. We could not abandon it even if we wished to, nor would we want to, for without such inquiry, life would lose its meaning, rendering us no more significant than livestock grazing in the fields. Moreover, this quest is not fruitless. We pursue various leads, grasp at fleeting glimpses of meaning, and though many paths may lead us to dead ends, we continually learn about the mysteries of our existence. Perhaps one day—suddenly, as the majestic white peaks of the Oberland reveal themselves to the weary traveler—we will discern the destination of this protracted journey. “Do not say the struggle is in vain,” a poet, not prone to delusions, once noted. He continued:

For while the tired waves, vainly breaking. Seem here no painful inch to gain, Far back, through creeks and inlets making. Comes silent, flooding in, the main.

Though I yearn for a revelation as much as anyone else, I find myself disconnected from the community of the credulous. I align myself with the skepticism of Doubting Thomas. I lack the ability to believe in the implausible and harbor a profound distrust of dimly lit rooms and the allure of magic. Individuals consumed by their eccentric beliefs leave me perplexed and somewhat aloof. I know a man, a truly admirable individual, whose existence is inundated with visions and revelations. He seems to unearth the philosopher's stone every few days; at times, he insists that consuming brown bread will grant eternal life—or at least extend it until others grow weary of his presence. Other times, he is enthusiastic about a new taxation scheme or a different kind of bath, claiming they hold the key to the universe. For a while, he could not stop talking about dried milk; during another phase, it was acetic acid. He believes that rubbing oneself with acetic acid will make one impervious to ailments, akin to Achilles after Thetis submerged him in the Styx. He interprets the stars to validate whatever belief he chooses to hold and summons spirits as effortlessly as one might hail a cab. It is not that he lacks intelligence; in practical matters, he displays remarkable acumen. Rather, he possesses an unfathomable capacity for belief, always journeying toward his own personal Damascus.

As for me, I prefer to exercise patience. I subscribe to Wordsworth's principle of “wise passiveness.” I would find as much insight in interpreting my fate from the sole of my boot as from the lines on my palm. Both would yield merely what I wished to derive from them. The same applies to the stars. Those who claim to decipher the riddles of our experiences through the celestial bodies are either deluding themselves or attempting to deceive others. They are merely bestowing their personal whims with the authority of the cosmos. The butterfly I observe dancing in the sunshine has just as much claim to interpret the European conflict as a reflection on its aimless existence. The stars do not gossip about our affairs; instead, they offer solace, provided we take the time to be still. The “vast and contemplative night” communicates in a language that is simple, dignified, and universal.

One of the few silver linings of the war is that it has allowed us in London to attune ourselves to that language. The streetlights have dimmed, making way for the brilliance of the stars above. Most evenings, I board the last bus heading north from the City, and from my seat at the back, I am treated to the magnificent procession of stars. It is an awe-inspiring sight that humanity is privileged to witness. Emerson suggested that if we were to behold it just once a century, we would spend years preparing for such a vision. Yet it is presented to us every night, and we scarcely take notice. Despite its availability, it is undeniably worth our attention. It serves as the most effective antidote to the chaotic, frantic environment we inhabit, where we dispute, clash, and ultimately perish. The celestial display offers us a fresh perspective and a reordering of our thoughts. Even the war appears to be a trivial matter of some poorly managed institution in light of the harmonious procession of boundless worlds. I do not feel compelled to seek revelations from the stars; I do not pressure them for insights regarding the war. Simply witnessing, feeling, and remaining quiet is sufficient for me.

Thus, I sincerely hope that Althea will refrain from squandering any more postage on her irreverent nonsense.
