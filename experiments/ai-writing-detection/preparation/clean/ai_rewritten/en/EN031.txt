In the town of Belfort, I find a seat on the street and observe my surroundings. We often speak in general terms about the archetypal Man in the Street, yet the Frenchman is truly that figure. The elements of daily life that are crucial to him are intertwined with the lamp-posts and sidewalks; every aspect of his existence, from meals to his struggles, is connected to these public spaces. Initially, an English visitor might perceive a French town or village as less visually appealing than those in England. However, with a closer examination, one begins to realize that the lack of picturesque charm largely stems from the stark, flat façades of the houses that rise abruptly from the streets, resembling cardboard sets from a theatrical performance—perhaps a reflection of the rigid nature of French logic. Upon a third glance, it becomes apparent that this is primarily because French homes do not feature front gardens. The English spirit yearns for an entrance that is softened by greenery and interrupted by steps, creating a transitional space that blends indoors and outdoors—a verdant area that serves dual purposes. In contrast, the Frenchman does not require such sentimental barriers, as the street itself feels natural and familiar to him.

While the French may lack front gardens, their streets serve as a collective front garden for everyone. These thoroughfares are adorned with trees and occasionally fountains. The street transforms into the Frenchman’s tavern, where he enjoys a drink; it becomes his dining room, as he often dines outdoors. It also acts as a gallery, as the statues and monuments lining French streets represent the pinnacle of national artistry, often as steeped in history as the Pyramids. Moreover, the street serves as the Frenchman’s Parliament, since the French have historically regarded their Chamber of Deputies with less gravity compared to how the English view their House of Commons. The trivial debates of elected officials confined to formal settings seem trivial to a populace whose ancestors have heard the clarion calls of revolutionaries like Desmoulins or Victor Hugo amidst the backdrop of political upheaval. The street, therefore, is not merely a place for dining and drinking; it is also a battleground where the Frenchman fights and sometimes perishes, imbibing the streets with a significance that transcends the ordinary.

Consider, for instance, the unassuming lamp-post. In London, a lamp-post can evoke humor, as one might picture a drunken man leaning against it, reminiscing about old friendships. However, in Paris, it carries a weight of tragedy, conjuring images of tyrants being hanged and the world coming to an end. There was a satirical Republican newspaper in Paris called LA LANTERNE, and one might find amusement at the thought of a British publication titled THE LAMP POST. We have established that the Frenchman embodies the essence of the man in the street, dining and dying in that very locale. If I were to wander through Paris and witness him settling down for the night in the street, it would only reaffirm his alignment with the spirit of his culture. All that is virtuous and all that is vice in France is intertwined with this outdoor ethos. French democracy, alongside its indecencies, reflects a desire for life to unfold in the open air. In comparison, a café possesses a more public character than a British public house.

Two particular reasons prompted these reflections while I wandered the streets of Belfort. For one, this town is located near the border of France and Germany, and boundaries are among the most beautiful concepts in existence. To truly love something often means to embrace its edges. Children instinctively gravitate toward the perimeters of things, building sandcastles along the shore or playing on the fringes of grass, only restrained by rules and authority. Reaching the conclusion of one experience often signifies the dawn of another.

Consequently, Belfort appears even more quintessentially French precisely because it lies on the very edge of Germany. Despite the German influences present in the area—such as German names, larger beer mugs, and theatrical barmaids dressed in exaggerated Alsatian costumes—the dominant French character seems to be intensified by these foreign elements. All day and night, groups of weary, sun-kissed soldiers trudge through the streets, emanating an air of stubborn disdain. German soldiers may exude contempt, but French soldiers possess a dual disdain, directed both at themselves and at others. This realism, perhaps, is an integral part of a nation skilled in war, science, and other undertakings that necessitate a blend of the essential and the unpleasant. Both soldiers and civilians often sport cropped hair and possess a certain robust appearance, which to an English observer might seem nearly brutal, a quality we might call “bullet-headed.” Indeed, it is fitting that we label it thus, for the intellectual evolution of the French has often mirrored the trajectory of bullets—yes, even those that explode.

Yet another reason to contemplate the open-air politics and art of the French in this town lies in its reputation for housing one of the most iconic public monuments in the nation. From my café seat, I can gaze upon the hill that looms over the town, crowned by a substantial, flat-faced citadel, its many windows glowing in the evening sun. At the base of this steep hill rests a colossal stone lion, as imposing as the hill itself. The monument has been carved from the rock in a manner that eschews triviality; it is not designed to resemble a typical statue, nor does it seek to render the lion’s mane in delicate curls or delineate the creature sharply from the earth from which it emerges, as if shaking the world itself. The lion's visage possesses a bold conventionality reminiscent of Assyrian artistry, and its mane is rendered as a shapeless tempest cloud, as if to suggest that divine forces have adorned its neck with thunder. Even from afar, the structure appears monumental, almost prehistoric, despite its relatively recent origin. It commemorates the town's unwavering resistance against the Germans throughout a harrowing year, surrendering only at the behest of its own government. Yet, the essence of defiance and resilience has pervaded this land since time immemorial.

As I depart this town and board a train into Germany, the news becomes increasingly urgent, filtering through the streets that Southern France is ablaze, hinting at the potential culmination of a grim modern struggle between wealth and poverty. As I traverse into more tranquil regions, I catch one last glimpse of the Lion of Belfort, standing proudly against the skyline—the final sight of a great nation that has known little peace.
