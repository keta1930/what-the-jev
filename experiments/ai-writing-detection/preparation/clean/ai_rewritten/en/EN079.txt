As the New Year approached, I found myself on the brink of making a rather unconventional resolution: to abandon punctuality altogether. Just in time, I reconsidered this impulsive notion, but it was a close call.

For quite some time, I had prided myself on being punctual. This commitment had, perhaps, led to a certain self-righteousness on my part, as I was often critical of those who took a more relaxed approach to timeliness. Yet, as 1919 drew to a close, I began to wrestle with doubts. The realities of modern life were increasingly making it difficult to adhere to strict schedules. With taxis hard to come by and public transportation frequently overcrowded, maintaining punctuality often required me to walk, which consumed precious minutes. Moreover, the number of appointments I had where others did not show up forced me to reevaluate my stance. The disarray this caused was particularly frustrating during a time when I was feeling exceptionally busy. Additionally, I found that waiting for someone who was late left me feeling restless and unable to focus on anything else.

I started to question the fairness of the situation: Why was I the one left waiting, growing hungry and irritable, while others arrived just as everything was set? Shouldn’t the roles be reversed?

When conscription was introduced and military discipline became commonplace, I held out hope that it might foster a new era of punctuality. After all, one often hears about the precision associated with the Army, and my most punctual friend had been a soldier, which sparked a friendly rivalry between us. However, my expectations were dashed. Soldiers on leave exploited the goodwill of those they visited, and the first demobilized soldier I hosted kept me waiting for dinner a full forty minutes.

Ironically, this particular tardy guest was a person of considerable stature and ability. If he had been a failure, as one might expect from certain self-help literature, it would have been easier to dismiss his lack of promptness. Instead, he had enjoyed a remarkably successful career despite his chronic lateness. Conventional wisdom suggests that unpunctual individuals are disqualified from achieving success; that their unreliability renders them untrustworthy and unemployable. Supposedly, the ability to keep appointments reflects one's character and capabilities. It is said that business professionals will dismiss candidates who do not arrive on time, regardless of their qualifications. Yet, my experiences have shown that these rigid assessments can sometimes be misguided. After all, wisdom plays a crucial role in success, and what sensible individual would arrive promptly at a dentist’s office? I have avoided those early morning appointments, but it is common knowledge that dentists are rarely ready for their first patients right on schedule. Editors tend to keep visitors waiting, and theatrical managers are notorious for their lack of punctuality; in their world, time seems to hold little significance. Actors, who often lead chaotic lives with their rehearsals, remain surprisingly cheerful despite the disorder.

Until late 1919, I had held a genuine affection for punctuality, but I eventually found myself detached enough to scrutinize it critically and identify its shortcomings. There are indeed compelling arguments against the rigid adherence to schedules.

Being consistently timely can have its drawbacks. Observing the dynamics of social interactions, I noticed that those who arrive late often appear much more joyful than those left waiting for them. Delving deeper into the implications of punctuality, I came to realize that, despite its veneer of righteousness, an unwavering commitment to being on time can sometimes lead to unfortunate outcomes. The logic is straightforward: if you miss a train, you lose the opportunity to board it; and if you are not on that train and it meets with disaster, you continue to live, only to miss future chances. I recalled a significant incident within my own family that illustrated this point. A relative and her daughter had planned a holiday in the Channel Islands. A cab driver had promised them a timely ride, yet he arrived so late that they missed their train by a single minute. The following day, news broke that the ferry to the Channel Islands had tragically sunk after hitting the Casquettes. The two women, filled with gratitude for their narrow escape, sought out the cab driver to express their thanks, ultimately deciding to adopt one of his many children. This true story serves as a poignant testament to the potential hazards of punctuality. Had they caught that train, they would have perished, and the cab driver’s child would have grown up with far less opportunity.

Given these reflections, it is hardly surprising that I contemplated making a formal resolution to abandon punctuality for good. However, I ultimately decided against such an extreme approach. Instead, I resolved to adopt a more flexible attitude toward timeliness than I had previously maintained.
