One of the few gifts my father bestowed upon me was an exceptional knack for sleeping. I can confidently assert, without any hint of arrogance, that I would excel in any competition for slumber. I have the ability to sleep for extended periods, frequently, and with remarkable depth. When I lay my head down on the pillow, I slip into an unfathomable tranquility, devoid of dreams, and about eight hours later, I awaken as if I have emerged from the very depths of the universe.

This is my usual experience; however, there are times when I find myself awake in the dead of night. My sleep then transforms into two distinct segments, separated by a grim interval of unbearable bleakness. This is my hour of despair. The tide has receded, leaving behind a barren landscape of mud and desolation. It is during these moments that I confront the darker aspects of life, feeling an overwhelming sense of hopelessness regarding humanity, the ongoing war, and my own existence. There is not a glimmer of light in the dismal panorama, and I feel as though the abyss is poised to engulf me along with all that I hold dear. Attempting to reason with this dark specter—a mere figment of my mind—offers no solace. My Dismal Jemmy cannot be banished by mere argument; he can only be dispelled by the presence of some comforting companionship.

In those moments, I switch on a light and summon one of my bedside companions. They stand ready to engage in conversation or simply to keep me company, eager to accommodate my needs. I feel immense gratitude toward Gutenberg, the Master Printer. Whether German or not matters little to me; even if he had been a Prussian—which I take joy in believing he was not—I would still sing his praises, regardless of any consequences. Gutenberg is not merely a mythical figure but a historical titan who kindled the sacred flame of knowledge and shattered the darkness that enveloped society. Although he introduced us to the Daily Owl, he also granted us the freedom of time and thought, allowing us to connect with the wisdom and mirth of centuries past. Thanks to him, for just a few shillings, I can listen to Homer’s verses, engage with Socrates, and share a laugh with Rabelais. I can join Don Quixote in his whimsical pursuits and wander the hills alongside Borrow. I can carry the entire universe of Shakespeare in my pocket and summon spirits to chase away my Dismal Jemmy.

But who are these spirits? In selecting them, it’s essential to avoid those deep-thinking, overly serious types. I have no desire for the presence of Plato, Gibbon, or any of the scholarly elite at my bedside, nor do I want poets, novelists, or dramatists—while they are all admirable in their own right, they can remain downstairs. Instead, I invite only those amiable figures who, in a sense, arrive in their nightclothes and slippers, sitting comfortably to chat as if they have known me since my earliest days, just as they might have known my father and grandfather. Among them is the venerable Montaigne, a delightful conversationalist. His stories are captivating, and he displays a remarkable openness. He reveals his thoughts as casually as a child rummaging through his pockets, granting me access to his entire intellectual estate. I can explore freely without any signs warning me to stay off the grass or cautioning me against trespassing.

Then there’s dear old Boswell; how could I possibly do without him? This garrulous old companion, the finest of chroniclers and most devoted of admirers, brings joy to my bedside. When Boswell begins to recount tales and I hear the old Doctor’s familiar address of “Sir,” all my life’s worries and troubles seem to dissipate, and Dismal Jemmy evaporates like a wraith at dawn. In those moments, I am no longer confined by the constraints of time and body; I join the ranks of the eternal. I witness their triumphant detachment from the petty dramas that unfold in our world, gaining a broader perspective on the grand narrative of human existence.

Of course, I also keep the crafty Pepys by my side—his pages are more stained than any others except for Boswell’s. What an extraordinary individual he was! He lives in our imagination more vividly than any historical figure. What is the secret to his allure? It lies in his ability to capture the genuine essence of himself on paper. It may seem trivial, but I assure you, it is one of the most challenging feats one can attempt. No matter how secretive you may be, you will find that conveying the full truth about yourself is no easy task. Pepys, however, succeeded brilliantly. While Benvenuto Cellini claimed to achieve this, I remain skeptical. Benjamin Franklin came close, and St. Augustine was candid about his youthful indiscretions, but his later confessions were tinged with the piety of a saint. No, Pepys stands alone as the master of self-revelation.

I also find comfort in having the Paston Letters nearby. As I drift back to sleep, I can hear Agnes Paston’s voice echoing from the fifteenth century. Though she has been gone for five centuries, I can still perceive the laborious scratching of her quill as she sends “Goddis blyssyng” to her son in London, sharing motherly gossip and breathing life into the harsh realities of Tudor England. How charming Agnes was! Little did she realize that as she grappled with spelling and penmanship, she was penning something that would achieve immortality. Had she known, I suspect she might not have bothered; she was far too practical to indulge in vanity.

There’s so much more I could say about my bedside companions—like the imposing George Borrow, who shares tales of adventures with Petulengro's sister or confronts the Flaming Tinman; the cheerful Boston doctor who engages in lively discussions over breakfast; and the Compleat Angler, who transports you to an eternal May morning. Sainte-Beuve, too, proves to be an exceptional conversational partner. Yet, as I draw near to the end of my musings, I must conclude.

Still, one final thought lingers. The best bedside companions should be crafted from soft leather and printed on delicate pages. This way, you can converse with them comfortably, without the annoyance of having to stretch your arms from the bed, holding them stiffly.
