After years of abstaining from a certain joy, one often finds that returning to it doesn't quite match the vivid memories built up during the absence. During the years away, the absence has allowed the imagination to embellish the experience, only for the real thing to reveal itself as somewhat mundane. While enjoyable, it doesn’t reach the heightened expectations formed in the past. Many of us have come to this realization in recent months of peace, engaging in activities we longed for during the war. Although these moments have been pleasant, they often fall short of the dreams we nurtured in the trenches of France and Flanders. Even the small joys, like the relief of not having to salute or attend medical examinations, quickly lose their novelty.

However, I recently revisited a pre-war pleasure that exceeded my expectations entirely. It was every bit as delightful as I had hoped it would be. I attended a first-class cricket match at Lord's, and it was everything I remembered and more.

There are individuals who seek to "enhance cricket," which brings to mind a particular manager to whom I once submitted a play. He courteously claimed to have enjoyed reading it, only to point out various flaws in the structure. "You have two brothers here," he observed. "They shouldn’t be brothers; they should be strangers. Then one marries the heroine, which is incorrect; it should be the other brother who does. Aunt Jane seems rather dull—what if she were arrested for bigamy? And I’d cut out your third act entirely, moving the fourth act to Monte Carlo, with the heroine facing blackmail from—what’s his name? Understand?" I acknowledged that I understood. "Are you okay with my critiques?" he asked casually. I replied that he wasn’t critiquing my work; he was crafting an entirely different story—one that I had no desire to create.

This is precisely what the cricket "brighteners" are attempting. They are designing a new game, a variant that those of us who truly appreciate cricket have no interest in watching. If someone claims that they find Lord's or the Oval tedious, I wouldn’t be surprised; what would astonish me would be if they found it more tedious than I find Epsom or Newmarket. Cricket doesn’t appeal to everyone, just as racing doesn't. Yet those who enjoy cricket appreciate it for what it is, without a desire for alterations from those who don’t share the same passion. I’m certain that Lord Lonsdale would not appreciate my attempts to "brighten" Newmarket for him.

The current state of Lord's, unchanged from five years ago, is more than sufficient for me. I would not change a single aspect of it. Hearing the pavilion bell ring again was akin to experiencing the most beautiful sound imaginable. The most enchanting note resonates at 11:20 in the morning; later, it loses some of its initial vibrancy. When conversations arise about the scores of operas, I find myself smiling with pity. They have yet to experience true music. The sound of ice clinking against glass can be pleasant on a fine day, but it pales in comparison to the magic of the Lord's bell.

As was my custom before the war, I purchased a copy of The Daily Telegraph upon entering the grounds. Normally, I wouldn't subscribe to this paper, but I have always held it in high regard, believing it possesses qualities that elevate it above any other London publication charging a similar price. Given that the seats at Lord's are notoriously hard, having a Daily Telegraph folded twice beneath me provides a comfort akin to that offered by good literature. My friends had previously noted that as summer progressed, my views became increasingly conventional, only to wane with autumn’s arrival. I must have been subconsciously influenced by the leading articles.

Rain interrupted play for a couple of hours. In the past, I would have been irritated by this and would have lamented my bad luck. But this time, I felt fortunate to be reliving all the familiar sensations in a single day. It was delightful to witness the clouds parting and hear the crowd erupting in applause at the first signs of clearing skies; to cheer the brave umpires when they finally decided to inspect the pitch; to observe the groundsmen springing into action, indicating a favorable decision; and to see the umpires, dressed in white coats, return with the ball and bails. Then, we could settle back into the rhythm of the day’s play.

While the pace of cricket might seem slow to a fan of league football, I don’t consider that a flaw. A piece by Lamb would likely feel sluggish to someone accustomed to the rapid developments in a work by William le Queux, who craves a new plot twist in every chapter. I won’t dispute anyone’s claim that a day at Lord's is dull; if that’s their belief, they are free to seek entertainment elsewhere. But I will firmly hold my view, as steadfast now as I was before the war, that a day at Lord's is a day filled with joy. If they allow me to enjoy the traditional Lord's, I will promise not to disrupt their football experience.
