The conclusion of my writing often finds itself truncated, and regrettably, I belong to that unfortunate category of individuals for whom the ending is crucial. This predicament is entirely of my own making, stemming from my propensity to take an excessive amount of time to arrive at my main argument. Recently, someone justifiably pointed out that I am ill-suited for writing prefaces, and they were correct; I invariably end up composing a preface to the preface, which leads to my untimely interruption.

In the previous piece I authored, I expressed support for three specific principles: first, the legal ramifications for the dissemination of intentionally false information; second, a differentiation in the reporting of immoral acts, distinguishing between those transgressions that any sane individual can recognize within themselves and those they would do better to overlook; and third, a strong advocacy for the requirement of authorship in the majority of published articles. It was at this juncture that my writing was abruptly halted—not because of spatial constraints, but rather due to my own disregard for them. Nevertheless, there remains further discourse that warrants attention.

It might be an overstatement to claim that I aspire to see anonymous articles regarded with the same disdain as anonymous letters. For the foreseeable future, the concept of a leading article that encapsulates the editorial stance of a publication must remain valid; we have all penned such articles and should not disparage anyone for doing so. However, I firmly believe that anonymity in writing should come with a valid justification, much like that of a leading article. Anonymity should be an exception, while signed contributions should be the norm. Ideally, individuals should always be prepared to disclose the identity behind any anonymous piece they have written. The journalistic custom of treating the origins of articles as a closely guarded secret is merely a facet of the larger conspiracy that places journalists in a position reminiscent of dubious organizations like Jesuits or Freemasons.

As has been frequently noted, the concept of anonymity would be acceptable if it were rooted in noble intentions. Imagine, for instance, that we could genuinely believe that the writers at the Thunderer newspaper were a valiant group of youthful idealists committed to dismantling Socialism—Municipal and National alike—without caring about personal accolades. Unfortunately, this is far from our reality. What we recognize, or rather, what we understand, is that the criticisms leveled against Socialism in the Thunderer stem from a disarray of contradictory and largely malign motivations, none of which would benefit from exposure. A contractor whose buildings have been condemned might write anonymously and find refuge in the Thunderer. Likewise, a disgruntled Socialist who has fallen out with their peers may also seek anonymity within its pages. Both a monopolist who has lost their grip and a demagogue without followers can pen their grievances under the veil of anonymity. While there exists a youthful zeal that prompts individuals to forgo their names, there also prevails a more prevalent and aged apprehension that prevents them from revealing their identities.

Furthermore, I wish to emphasize another principle that could enhance the integrity of journalism: it should be a standard practice to print not only the editor's name but also that of the publication's owner on every issue. If a publication is owned by shareholders, a list of those shareholders should be included. If, as is often the case in this particularly undemocratic era, a single individual owns the paper, that person's name should be prominently displayed, preferably in bold red letters. This transparency would enable readers to discern the interests being represented. My colleagues in Manchester are currently in a state of frenzy over the influence of brewers and the perils of allowing them into political office. However, the public is generally aware of a man's influence through beer; the topic is simply too engaging for anyone to overlook such personal connections. In contrast, an individual may wield substantial power through journalism, and the average citizen remains blissfully unaware of this control. Time and again, we have witnessed a Mr. Robinson suddenly elevated to the peerage in the Birthday Honours list, often without any evident rationale. Even the society columns, which we peruse with great interest, offer little insight beyond labeling him a sportsman, a benevolent landlord, or an enthusiast for badger breeding. I would prefer that Mr. Robinson's name be familiar to the British populace before such an elevation, allowing them to appreciate the public service for which they owe him gratitude. It would be preferable for them to recognize his name from the front page of Tootsie’s Tips, The Boy Blackmailer, or Nosey Knows—those vibrant financial publications that contributed so much to the Empire while narrowly escaping criminal charges. Had they seen his name there, they would have a more accurate and nuanced understanding of the accolades in the society column that proclaim him a true gentleman and a devout Churchman.

Lastly, I propose that, while it is beyond the reach of legal enforcement, it should become customary for editors to ensure the publication of letters containing clear and actionable complaints. There has been an alarming trend of editors becoming far too lax in this regard. In earlier times, an editor took on the role of an unofficial public servant, charged with the responsibility of conveying public discourse. If an editor chose to suppress content, it was expected that they would have a compelling reason, such as the material being defamatory or indecent. In contrast, today's editors often view themselves as creative artists who can curate and censor information with the same casualness as a poet or caricaturist. They may include a particular letter simply because they deem it clever, while dismissing others they consider trivial. The suppression of an article may occur if they perceive it as incorrect, while a more concerning piece might be withheld because they deem it appropriate. The traditional view that the editor is merely a conduit for public expression—a true "organ" of opinion—seems to have been entirely lost. In contemporary times, the editor is not just the medium; they are also the performer manipulating that medium. 

This shift encapsulates the pressing danger of our era. The oppression witnessed in the past often involved individual actors—both the oppressors and the oppressed were straightforward and isolated in their struggles. The aristocrat may have harbored disdain for those beneath them, yet they also resented their peers. The capitalist often operated as an individualist. However, in our current age, even capitalists have adopted a façade of Socialism. They now possess the advantage of scientific advancement and collective organization, enabling them to potentially establish a tyranny far greater than anything previously experienced.
