Stevenson, as we recall, once dedicated his birthday to a young child—was it a girl or a boy? This child had a deep appreciation for birthdays, while Stevenson had reached a stage in his life where such occasions had lost their charm for him. Many of us, if we live long enough, inevitably encounter this sense of indifference. The thrill of birthdays dissipates along with the toys that once sparked that joy. I remember a time when life seemed to revolve around the cycle of birthdays, with the tedium of waiting being broken only by delightful events like Christmas, Easter, and school vacations. However, for many years, I have approached my birthday with a kind of shock, as if I were acknowledging the presence of an old friend with whom I’ve lost touch, and then I would move on without further reflection on the day.

Today, however, feels different. One cannot simply overlook the significance of turning fifty; it is a milestone that demands recognition. Fifty! The Psalmist suggests that our limit is seventy, which makes fifty a striking reminder of the passage of time. The arithmetic is simple, yet the implications are profound: only twenty years left, and those years are often marked by decline, like the withering of leaves. I cast my mind back two decades, and it seems like a mere blink of an eye. Yet, during those earlier years, the notion of reaching fifty felt almost unfathomable—it was a distant reality meant for others; it signified being among the older generation, a time when one would be perceived as an artifact of the past.

Now, standing at fifty years of age, I do not feel like an artifact at all; instead, I feel as youthful as I ever did. I had feared that entering middle age would bring with it a sense of nostalgia for the joys of my youth, a longing for the past that would make life feel unbearable. I worried about how life would be once cricket—a cherished pastime—lost its importance in my daily routine. The thought of never again experiencing the thrill of a perfect shot to the boundary or the satisfaction of catching an opponent in the slips painted a bleak picture of the future. Yet, I’ve come to realize that the essence of youth is not contingent upon cricket or childhood delights. It is rooted in an acceptance of the possibilities life offers. So, do not assume, my younger friend, that your ability to leap five feet eight inches makes you younger than I am just because I have no desire to jump anymore. The spirit of youth is a far deeper and more persistent quality than mere physical prowess. It can flourish just as vibrantly at eighty as it does at eighteen; only its expressions may shift over time. Holmes famously declared that he never felt old, proclaiming, “I am eighty-three young today.” Likewise, Johnson, despite his age and ailments, insisted he was “a young fellow”—and indeed, he embodied a sprightly spirit that defied the ravages of time.

Youth is, in truth, not merely a physical condition but rather a matter of the soul. One can feel spiritually aged at twenty-five or retain a lively, youthful spirit at eighty. Byron lamented his own decline at the tender age of thirty-four, expressing sentiments of lifelessness and decay. While there may have been an element of self-dramatization in his words, the fact remains that he had aged into an old man by thirty-six, whereas Browning, who lived to seventy-seven, maintained an unyielding spirit of youth to the very end, encapsulated in the vibrant finale of “Asolando.”

When contemplating old age, it is a common misstep to assume that the spirit must diminish alongside the body. Naturally, if the body is treated poorly, its consequences will affect the spirit. However, the natural decline of our physical capabilities does not have to weaken the healthy spirit; in fact, it can fortify it. This spirit, while perhaps lacking the fervor of youth, can burn more steadily with the passage of time. In our younger days, our restless spirits often long for unattainable dreams.

We look before and after, and pine for what is not.

Yet, as we age, we learn to find satisfaction in the more immediate realities of life. The scope of our aspirations may narrow, but the vastness of our existence remains unchanged, leading to the realization that what truly matters is often right at our fingertips. Our souls, once preoccupied with the pursuit of lofty ambitions, return to discover that the spiritual fulfillment we sought was always within reach. By focusing on these immediate gratifications, we uncover another truth: this is the genuine path to those lofty aspirations, as the grandeur we seek is not found externally, but resides within us.

I fear I might be preaching now, and that is not my intention, yet if there were ever a time to reflect on such matters, it would be as I celebrate my fifty years. To counter my musings, I will share a brief anecdote that the current Bishop of Chester recounted over dinner, which offers a practical approach to maintaining a youthful heart. In his younger days, he worked with Archdeacon Jones of Liverpool, who, at over eighty, had once been a tutor to Gladstone. During a conversation steeped in nostalgia, the Bishop sought to draw out the Archdeacon’s memories of yesteryear. Suddenly, the Archdeacon shifted the topic, asking, “What was the Philharmonic concert like last night?” When the Bishop expressed surprise, the Archdeacon explained, “Though I am an elderly man, I strive to keep my heart young, and the best way to achieve that is to ensure my thoughts remain aligned with the vibrant life around me rather than dwelling on the past.”

The reality is that each phase of our journey holds its unique attractions. None is necessarily superior to another, though I have always favored the stage in which I currently find myself. At twenty, I believed there was no age quite like that, and now that I am fifty, I have transferred my enthusiasm to this present age. I genuinely believe that if fortune permits me to reach sixty, I will proclaim that there is no age like sixty. And why not? The beauty of witnessing the sun rise over morning hills is matched by the pleasure of strolling homeward as evening shadows stretch and the coolness of twilight sets in.
