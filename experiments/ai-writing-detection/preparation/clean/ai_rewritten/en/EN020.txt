Recently, a friend of mine stopped by and, noticing my loyal Airedale—affectionately named "Quilp"—whose tail was wildly wagging in anticipation of a stroll, commented that I might soon be required to pay a guinea to keep that dog once the new taxes were implemented. I expressed my hope that Mr. McKenna would not act so foolishly. In fact, I was quite confident that he would not. I know him well and have always viewed him as a sensible individual. I suggested that if he must impose taxes, they should be based on our incomes, but questioned why he should interfere with how we choose to spend our earnings. Why should he deprive a less fortunate person of the companionship of a dog, making it a luxury for only the affluent?

Quilp's tail mirrored the passion of my assertions. He understood that the conversation revolved around him, and his large brown eyes sparkled with an almost human intelligence. His expressive brows communicated a sense of self-pity and longing. He was certain that, regardless of the outcome, I was on his side, and with just a hint, he would have readily shown my friend the rough side of his tongue. However, he is a well-behaved creature and knows how to keep his composure around guests.

What would be the consequence of such a high tax? I continued, my voice rising with fervor. It would strike a blow against the democracy of dogs, reducing the entire canine population to a pampered class of degenerates. Is there anything more distasteful than a rotund woman in furs, cradling a lap dog dressed in similar finery? This scenario degrades the noble lineage of dogs just as much as a footman adorned in gold buttons and braid diminishes the dignity of humanity. Yet, it is precisely these degenerate dogs that a high tax would protect. Genuine companions like Quilp, who offer unwavering loyalty, run errands, carry burdens, and bark in our defense—those sincere and faithful dogs that have inspired countless poets—would be taxed out of existence.

Now, as for cats, I continued—(at the mere mention of the word, Quilp tensed with excitement)—cats are an entirely different matter. Personally, I would not be bothered if Mr. McKenna decided to tax them a guinea for every whisker. The only moment in a cat's life that I find tolerable is when it is still a kitten. Who was that French thinker who suggested that women should be born at seventeen and perish at thirty? I feel that cats ought to pass away as soon as they transition from kittens to full-grown cats.

My friend responded coolly, asserting that cats hold a superior spiritual status compared to dogs. According to him, the dog is merely a servant, a subordinate creature perpetually awaiting its master's command. Look at Quilp now, he said, what a display of servility. Cats, on the other hand, do not prostrate themselves to humans. They may enjoy being petted, but they do not form attachments to the hand that strokes them. They are not parasites; instead, they embody independence, living life on their own terms and remaining indifferent to approval, never calling anyone their master. This is precisely why the French regard them as superior to dogs.

I retorted, with a touch of fervor, that I do not care what the French think.

But they are our allies, my friend replied sternly. Conversely, the Germans tend to prefer dogs. I hope you are not in favor of Germany.

When it comes to the cat-and-dog debate, I unabashedly align with dogs, I replied boldly. I detest these efforts to insert bias into the conversation. Furthermore, I would like to remind you that it was a remarkable Frenchman, none other than Pascal, who bestowed the highest praise upon dogs. "The more I see of men," he said, "the better I like dogs." I challenge you to find any French source that offers such commendation for cats.

Continuing my argument, I maintained that the dog is a generous, warm-hearted, and chivalrous creature, one who will frolic with you, grieve for you, or lay down his life for you. Literature is replete with tales of canine heroism. Who has trekked Helvellyn without being haunted by the memory of the shepherd’s dog that inspired both Scott and Byron? Or traversed the Pass of St. Bernard without recalling the devoted hounds of the renowned monastery? In contrast, the cat is a secretive and aloof being, selfish and enigmatic, reminiscent of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde. Observe her purring on the hearth in front of the fire; she appears to be a picture of innocence and blissful contentment. But do not be deceived, my dear friend—wait until night falls. Where is the demure Mistress Puss then? Is she at home, keeping watch alongside the loyal dog Tray? No, she could not care less if the house were ablaze or if burglars were ransacking it. She is out on the prowl, indulging in clandestine escapades that seem so Oriental and ominous, steeped in a sense of devilish intent. I can understand why cats have long been associated with witchcraft; their very presence almost compels me to believe in such things, despite my rationality. I can entertain any notion about a cat. They are heartless and mercenary. The term "cat" has become synonymous with everything mean-spirited, spiteful, and malicious. To call a woman "an old cat" is perhaps the most unflattering remark one could make.

In contrast, the dog is an open book, wearing his heart on his sleeve. His life unfolds before you as clearly as day. While he may have his indiscretions, he harbors no secrets. You can glimpse his flaws instantly, but his virtues are boundless. A cat is as distant from your life as a lizard, while a dog is as familiar as your own thoughts or shadow, and his steadfastness is one of the few comforts in a world often devoid of loyalty. Recall the words of Charles Reade: "He was only a man, but he was as faithful as a dog." That was the highest praise he could offer his hero—comparing his faithfulness to that of a dog. And consider the contributions of dogs—they draw carts in Belgium, gather sheep in the Yorkshire fells, tend to cattle by the roadside, and guard the lonely homesteads from prowlers, always vigilant, always obedient, perpetually the friend of humanity, regardless of how friendless one may feel… Shall we take a walk?

At the mention of a walk, Quilp sprang toward me, bursting with enthusiasm. "Good dog," I exclaimed. "If Mr. McKenna imposes a guinea tax on you, I shall never utter a kind word about him again."
