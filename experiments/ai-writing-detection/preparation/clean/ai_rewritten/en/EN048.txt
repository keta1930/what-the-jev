As I exited a Tube train yesterday evening, I heard someone request my assistance: “Will you please give this gentleman an arm to the lift? He is blind.” I obliged, and, as is often the case with individuals who are visually impaired, I discovered that my new companion was remarkably animated and upbeat. The cheerful disposition of the blind continually fascinates me. It seems that when the external light fades, an inner brilliance ignites, illuminating the shadows that surround them. Outside, the night may be dark and foreboding, yet within, there exists a warmth and radiance. The world becomes confined to the realm of one’s thoughts, yet this very limitation appears to elevate the spirit, causing it to soar with greater fervor. It was the renowned blind English poet who, during his own darkness, inspired the character of the blind Samson to declare:

He that hath light within his own clear breast May sit i' th' centre and enjoy bright day.

Numerous accounts suggest that, contrary to expectations, the cheerfulness of those who lose their sight often gains newfound strength rather than diminishes. In all the instances I have encountered, I cannot recall a single case where blindness led to a decrease in joy. Instead, it seems that the zest for life is amplified. Recently, Mr. Galsworthy penned a letter to the Times expressing sympathy for the plight of the blind, only to be met with an overwhelming wave of dissent from blind individuals themselves. Dr. Campbell, the founder of the Normal School for the Blind, is a prime example of someone who embraced life with incredible enthusiasm, achieving what many thought impossible, such as cycling and mountain climbing despite his condition.

The experience of Mr. Pulitzer, the distinguished owner of the New York World, was similarly striking. He lost his sight abruptly while observing a sunset from his Mediterranean villa. He remarked, “How quickly the sun has set,” only to be told by a companion that it had not yet set. In that instant, he became completely blind. Yet those who knew him claimed that his mental vivacity never diminished during his years of blindness; in fact, it flourished.

My friend Mr. G.W.E. Russell posits that the perceived advantage of the blind over the deaf and mute in terms of cheerfulness might be more illusory than substantial. He notes that the blind feel less aware of their misfortune in social settings, while the deaf and mute experience a profound sense of isolation. This is certainly true; during conversations, the blind engage on equal footing with sighted individuals, while the deaf and mute find themselves trapped in an agonizing silence. Their ability to see becomes a burden rather than a boon, as they witness the movements of lips and signs of laughter, intensifying their sense of exclusion. As a result, their demeanor may appear gloomy. Conversely, in solitude, the deaf and mute may find that they possess an advantage. They can still perceive the vibrant colors and motions of life, whereas the blind are deprived of that visual experience and face limitations in movement that the deaf do not.

Mr. Russell concludes that while the blind experience their happiest moments when surrounded by others, the deaf and mute find joy in solitude. There is certainly some truth in this assertion, yet I maintain that the prevailing belief is accurate: in terms of accepting adversity with a cheerful spirit, the loss of sight is less burdensome than the loss of hearing and speech. This distinction is evident for a fundamental reason. Ultimately, the primary joy in life stems from the ease of communication and connection with others. I relish the sight of a golden sunset, stroll through beech woods in spring—indeed, throughout all seasons—and observe the apples ripening on trees and hedgerows brimming with blackberries. Yet, these visuals serve as the backdrop for my narrative—the scenery, rather than the essence of the play itself. It is the human interactions that impart vibrancy and depth to life. The ears and voice are the conduits for the lively exchange of thoughts, and within this exchange, the blind person fully participates and often thrives. Their affliction can heighten their engagement in the tapestry of human experiences, granting them a unique joy in simple interactions. Not only do they feel at home within the human community, but they often become its focal point, exemplifying Johnson's criteria for a good companion: they are “a clubbable man.”

Moreover, in the enjoyment of the world's beauty, it can be argued that the blind may derive as much mental stimulation as the deaf and mute. While they may not see the sunset, they are acutely aware of the cuckoo's call, the lark's song, the gentle buzz of bees, and the rustling of corn. If, as is often the case, they possess an affinity for music, they inherit a treasure trove of auditory experiences that can transform their lives into an ongoing celebration. Have you ever listened to Mr. William Wolstenholme, the composer, as he improvises on the piano? If not, you may not fully grasp the delightful world of sounds that the blind inhabit. Of course, this comparison is somewhat skewed; for musicians, sound is life, while silence is akin to death. Few stories are as heart-wrenching as that of Beethoven, who, in his desperation to hear his timeless symphonies, broke piano strings in vain. Had he been stricken with blindness instead of deafness, it is plausible that the tragedy of his existence would have been significantly alleviated. Imagine the tranquility that would have washed over him had he been able to hear his Ninth Symphony. In contrast, blind Milton, seated at his organ, likely experienced less tragedy and perhaps more happiness than Milton would have felt had he been deaf and reliant on a futile ear-trumpet. It seems that without the inspiration of music, he may not have crafted the remarkable verses that, as Macaulay eloquently noted, “would not have misbecome the lips of those ethereal beings whom he saw with that inner eye, which no calamity could darken.”

While it is true that, in a tangible sense, blindness may be one of the gravest afflictions we can endure, I am focusing solely on its spiritual ramifications. In this regard, the loss of hearing and speech appears to impose a more profound sense of desolation than the loss of sight. The former condition fosters spiritual isolation, while the latter can enhance the intimate connections we share in life. A man who became blind later in life once expressed his gratitude that it was his sight he lost rather than his hearing, stating, “One has shut me off from the sun; the other would have shut me off from life.”
