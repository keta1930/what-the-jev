Food often serves as a more spiritually invigorating topic than the weather. While discussions about atmospheric conditions tend to be limited and lack the heat of debate—since we all agree on what constitutes a pleasant day versus a less favorable one—conversations about food are boundless. The diversity of tastes in culinary preferences can create a unique bond among individuals. As the expression goes, a shared disdain for something like tapioca pudding can reveal a deeper camaraderie; if someone detests it, they might just be a kindred spirit.

Everyone has their preferred meal, and for me, lunch holds a special appeal. However, when I claim lunch is my favorite, I’m not suggesting that it should replace breakfast, dinner, or tea. In fact, I often skip lunch altogether due to the demands of my work in journalism. Today, for instance, I will likely miss out on it. What I truly mean is that, in its essence, lunch captivates me most because of its inclusive nature.

At home, we typically enjoy breakfast and dinner, but lunch is a meal that we indulge in while out in London. The city offers an incredible array of choices. Whether it's a lavish six-course meal paired with champagne overlooking the river or a simple poached egg while playing dominoes under skylights, the options are endless. We have the freedom to either sit or stand, and if we wished, we could even recline in a Roman-style manner. Time is equally flexible; we can savor our meal for hours or rush through it in mere minutes. Each day could present a different dish, or some might choose to stick with a consistent favorite, like a chop and chips. Although I remain perplexed by the appeal of chips, which seem to provide little sustenance while being quite cumbersome, perhaps they serve a different purpose altogether. But, I digress.

There exists a certain romance surrounding lunch; it conjures images of significant conversations with stockbrokers, playwrights, and publishers—individuals who can be likened to demigods in their realms. When it comes to critical discussions, whether it’s about the latest financial maneuvers or the title of a new play, the invitation remains constant: “Let’s have lunch, and we can discuss it.” Once the waiter takes your coat and hat, and you glance uncertainly at the menu, there’s a camaraderie that develops. When asked, “What will you drink?” the polite response is often, “Oh, anything you’re having,” demonstrating your willingness to share. Then, with that settled, you dive into the conversation, and the fabric of history is woven anew, one lunch at a time.

Lunch not only serves as a platform for business discussions but also acts as a catalyst for rekindling friendships. How many times have you encountered an old friend in the Strand, only to be greeted with, “Hello, old chap! It’s been ages; you must join me for lunch soon”? The enthusiastic reply, “Absolutely! I would love that,” often leaves you with a warm glow that lingers until the next day, when the moment is all but forgotten. Dinner invitations carry a formality, tea invites are unnecessary, and breakfast requests are often impractical. However, lunch invitations possess a casual warmth that makes them delightful in their own right, standing alone and not contingent on any other meals to follow.

While I could continue discussing the pleasures of lunch in London, I must briefly touch upon the experience of lunch in the countryside. I’m not referring to the bliss of dining al fresco, which is undoubtedly a heavenly experience and perhaps too obvious to elaborate on in a brief essay. Instead, I focus on lunch at a country house. One of the most enjoyable aspects of this setting is the freedom to sit next to whomever you wish. At dinner, you might find yourself seated next to someone entirely unappealing; breakfast presents the challenge of arriving at the right moment to claim a seat next to someone special; and during tea, your chair could easily be taken as you step away for a moment. But at lunch, you simply follow the person of your interest in, and there you are—securely placed.

Yet, there exists a unique environment that surpasses both urban and rural experiences, one that encapsulates the joys of lunch more than any other: lunch on a train. This experience should ideally happen around 1:30 PM, when you’re far enough from London to feel genuine hunger. As the landscape whizzes by, you approach the picturesque West; rivers appear, and quaint villages come into view, while you glide through ancient towns with reverence. Inside the train, the waiter places the potatoes beside you and then discreetly retreats.

Well, that’s a risk he’s taking. I believe that a true potato aficionado must be a remarkable person. The simple act of enjoying lunch while traversing the countryside, gazing at the scenery, creates a delightful experience that transcends the mere act of eating. It becomes a celebration of the moment, shared with the thrill of travel and the anticipation of what lies beyond the next bend.
