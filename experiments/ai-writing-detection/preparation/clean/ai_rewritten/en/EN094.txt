Recently, I found myself in conversation with a man who approached life with a measured perspective regarding a topic that had become a matter of public speculation. He remarked, "If you had asked me about the veracity of such claims four months ago, I would have responded with a definitive 'Yes.' However, after the extensive disinformation surrounding the so-called Russian army, I now require solid proof before believing anything. I was once convinced by the tales of a phantom army emerging from Archangel! There are those who assert they never bought into that narrative; some even claim they have always been skeptical. But I can assert with confidence that I was indeed a believer, and I had never encountered a rumor that seemed so well-supported by diverse and compelling evidence. Yet, it turned out to be false. Now, I find myself transformed. I no longer consider myself a believer; I have become a skeptic."

This shift in perspective is likely a common experience. A person who maintains the same level of belief today as they did six months prior is someone who has not absorbed the valuable lessons that reality imparts. We exist in an environment saturated with immense whispers, and the majority of these whispers are misleading. Even the term "Official" no longer holds the weight it used to; it is not the information I receive that I find compelling, but rather the silence surrounding what I am not told that drives my quest for truth.

Recall the infamous exchange during a libel case involving a London newspaper many years ago. When asked, "What did you instruct him to do?" the response was, "I told him to tell the truth." "The whole truth?" came the follow-up. "No, just selected truths."

In navigating the realm of rumors, we must be vigilant against our innate propensity to believe what we wish to be true. A prime example is the reported victory in Poland in November 1914. Strong evidence suggests that a significant portion of Hindenburg's forces nearly fell into a trap; had Rennenkampf arrived on schedule, the encirclement would have succeeded. However, it did not happen. Despite this, nearly every correspondent in Petrograd heralded news of a decisive victory with unwavering confidence. The correspondent for the Morning Post described it as an event "terrible yet sublime," comparing it to the devastation left by Napoleon in Russia. Even Lord Kitchener, speaking in the House of Lords, proclaimed that Russia had achieved the greatest feat of the war. Shortly thereafter, the equally unfounded rumor of Hindenburg's "victory" ignited jubilant celebrations in Berlin. This blind belief was fueled by a desire to embrace comforting narratives.

Fear also serves as a fertile ground for the spread of rumors. The notable case of the alleged concrete fortification at Maubeuge illustrates this point well. We received extensive accounts detailing how German agents acquired the land, installed a concrete platform in secrecy, and utilized a massive 42-cm howitzer to bombard Maubeuge. Almost immediately, tales emerged of similar concrete constructions popping up across Britain—in places like Willesden and Edinburgh. We began to suspect anyone with a concrete garage or workshop foundation of being a potential German operative. I must confess that I, too, fell prey to such fears, fixating on a particular factory in London, even though I had no substantial evidence beyond its German ownership and strategic location. I was ensnared by the myth of Maubeuge and the anxieties it engendered.

Yet, the truth remains: there was never a concrete fortification at Maubeuge, nor was a 42-cm howitzer ever deployed against that stronghold. The property was owned by upstanding French citizens, and an apology from the Matin for slandering them remains accessible for those interested in the war's myriad myths.

I bring this topic to light not merely to revisit historical inaccuracies but to underscore the severe injustices we may inflict upon the innocent when we accept rumors about our neighbors without verifying the facts. Consider the heart-wrenching tale recounted during an inquest for an elderly woman in Henham, Suffolk. Her husband had served as the village schoolmaster for twenty-eight years, and the couple had invested in their son’s education, sending him to Germany to learn the language. Given the financial constraints typical of a village schoolmaster, I can envision the sacrifices they made to ensure their son had a promising start. After completing his studies, he ventured to South America, where he found work as a language instructor. When war broke out, he felt compelled to return and enlist.

In the interim, however, malicious rumors began to circulate in their village. It was alleged that he had become an officer in the German Army. Based solely on this unfounded gossip, the Chief Constable ordered the couple to leave their home and avoid the East Coast. This decree was effectively a death sentence for them. The weight of this order overwhelmed the old man, leading him to take his own life. When the son returned home, he discovered his father had died and his mother was left in a state of devastation. In an attempt to help her recover, he took her to the seaside, but upon their return to the village, she, too, succumbed to despair and committed suicide. The jury, rather than attributing the tragedy to "Slander-induced Death," opted for the more clinical verdict of "Suicide while of unsound mind." Such was the caution of the jurors.

So, how do these rumors spread? There are numerous avenues. Let me illustrate one such pathway. Recently, in his critique of the war, Mr. Belloc observed the following:

"The official German communiqué released last Saturday serves as a fine example for analysis. I cite it as it was presented in the Westminster Gazette, a publication renowned for its extensive German insights since the war began."

He then proceeded to quote the communiqué. I couldn't help but smile at the cleverness of Mr. Belloc. He refers to a document that appeared in every newspaper across the nation but specifies the Westminster Gazette. The reason for this selective mention is clear: to lend credence to his parenthetical assertion about the publication's remarkable reputation for German information.

While the Westminster Gazette enjoys a solid reputation, the insinuation that it possesses some unique insight into German affairs is unfounded. Imagine if you, an ordinary citizen, were accused of having special connections with Germany during this tumultuous time. Even if you were as innocent as the Suffolk schoolmaster, you might still find yourself under the scrutiny of your fellow citizens and the watchful eyes of law enforcement.

Allow me to provide another example. A friend of mine, a journalist with liberal leanings, recently attended a private dinner with M. Painlevé, a French academician, Senator Lafontaine from Brussels, and two other deputies from France and Belgium. The following day, the Daily Express reported that he had dined with "three or four foreigners" to discuss peace. The next issue of the London Mail raised the question, "Who were the foreigners with whom he dined?" This illustrates how insinuations can breed suspicion and elevate a simple dinner into a web of speculation. My friend chose not to respond, recognizing that certain publications can be safely ignored, regardless of their claims. I mention this to demonstrate how rumors take root and evolve.

What is the lesson to be drawn from all of this? It aligns with the sentiments expressed by my earlier acquaintance. We should approach all rumors with skepticism, whether they pertain to events or individuals. When Napoleon's marshals boasted of victories, he demanded, "Show me your prisoners." Similarly, when confronted with a rumor, we should not accept it blindly. Instead, we ought to inquire, "Show me the facts." And before we embrace those facts, let us ensure they are comprehensive and not merely fragments of a larger story.
