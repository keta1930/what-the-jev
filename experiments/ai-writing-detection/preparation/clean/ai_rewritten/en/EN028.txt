"Look at Peggy with that dreadful cat again—the one-eyed creature from next door," someone exclaimed. I turned my gaze outside and indeed saw Peggy coming down the garden path, cradling in her arms the one-eyed feline from over the fence. Despite its physical flaw, Peggy has a deep affection for this animal; in fact, I sometimes wonder if her fondness is even greater because of its singular eye. It seems she believes that by caring for the cat, it forgets its imperfection and finds joy once more. Her love extends to all creatures with four legs. I've witnessed her spend an entire day gathering handfuls of grass from the orchard, eagerly delivering them to the donkey or horse in the neighboring paddock. When there are no animals to engage with, she improvises by donning horseshoes on her hands and feet, mimicking the sound of a horse trotting about. Though her compassion embraces all four-legged beings, her deepest affections appear to be reserved for those who are disabled or disadvantaged.

Children often embody a rare quality of charity that allows them to overlook imperfections. The instinct to turn away from physical flaws is deeply ingrained; it seems to be an evolutionary trait aimed at preserving the ideal forms of life. Society often equates these ideal forms with beauty, while deviations from them are branded as ugly. In this light, feeling discomfort at the sight of something imperfect—be it a withered hand or a one-eyed cat—reveals an unconscious role as guardians of nature’s standards. When we recoil at the sight of a defect, we are, in effect, upholding the traditions of what is considered beautiful.

However, it is entirely possible to develop a fondness for those who are physically different and to perceive them as beautiful. Take, for instance, the story recounted by Descartes. He reminisced about a childhood companion who had a squint, stating that throughout his life, he held a special affection for individuals who bore the same trait. In this instance, the memories associated with that squinted gaze infused a sense of charm into what might initially seem a deformity. For Descartes, that squint became a symbol of a happier, more innocent time, transformed in his mind into something lovely.

Memory is not the sole catalyst for this transformation; intellectual empathy can also play a crucial role. Wilkes, a man notorious for his unattractiveness, boasted that he could win the affection of any woman with just a half-hour head start over his competitors. When one of his female supporters defended him against a remark regarding his squint, she declared, "Of course he does; but he doesn't squint more than a man of his genius ought to squint." It wasn’t only women who were captivated by Wilkes. Few can forget the moment Tom Davies introduced him to Dr. Johnson, who despised Wilkes' radical political views and would have preferred to avoid him entirely. Initially, Johnson remained aloof, but eventually, he succumbed to the allure of Wilkes' conversation.

Similarly, Johnson regarded his wife as the epitome of beauty, despite the fact that most others saw her as plain and unremarkable. He would refer to her as a "pretty creature," even after her death, indicating that his perception of her beauty was deeply rooted in his affection for her.

Here, I believe, we touch upon a fundamental aspect of beauty. It relates to the soul more than it does to mere physical attributes. We begin with a common bias in favor of certain forms—those that nature has instilled in us as ideals. We strive to perpetuate these forms. Yet, when conventionally attractive features are paired with spiritual ugliness, they lose their appeal. Conversely, a physically flawed appearance can radiate beauty if it is associated with inner goodness. The spirit often reigns supreme over the senses. Francis Thompson captures this beautifully when he writes about the soul overshadowing physical features.

Nevertheless, beauty can also be a practical consideration. Imagine if humanity had evolved with only one eye, centrally located in the forehead; the sight of a person with two eyes might evoke the same revulsion we reserve for physical oddities today. We would likely view the two-eyed individual with a blend of curiosity and disdain, much like how we currently find ourselves observing the bearded woman in a sideshow. Our motivations for gazing upon such individuals would stem not from admiration but from a sense of discomfort and a reassuring acknowledgment of our own normalcy. We would take pride in our single eye, convinced that it represented the true standard of beauty, while viewing the two-eyed person as a bizarre deviation.

In essence, beauty serves as a tribute to necessity. Humanity has evolved in ways that favor certain physical traits, such as having two eyes for better depth perception, just as having four fingers and a thumb is advantageous for manipulation. Our physical attributes have been shaped by the demands of survival. Consequently, the more effectively these traits fulfill their purpose, the more we deem them beautiful. Our conceptions of beauty, therefore, are not fixed; they are contingent upon our biological needs. Two eyes are essential for navigating our world, and thus, we come to cherish them. The more adept they are at their functions, the more we are drawn to them, declaring them beautiful.

I think Peggy, cradling her one-eyed cat under the sun, remains untouched by our conventional notions of beauty. Eventually, as she grows older and her tastes become more aligned with societal standards, she may adopt a more traditional perspective. But for now, her innocent embrace of the imperfect cat serves as a poignant reminder of the beauty that can be found beyond the constraints of physical appearance.
