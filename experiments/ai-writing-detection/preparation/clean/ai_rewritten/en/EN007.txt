Recently, I stumbled upon a magazine with a rather jaded cover that revealed some intriguing details about skating attire. It seems that for pond skating, the appropriate outfit consists of a brown homespun jacket adorned with a fur collar, while those heading to private rinks don a gray herringbone suit paired with a taupe alpine hat. How many barren years I have spent as a skater without such guidance! During a recent practice session attempting to master a counter turn, I found myself observed by a hockey player, who had nothing better to do while waiting for his companions than to watch my clumsy attempts. At last, he broke the silence, asking, “What’s the objective of that game, anyway?” It had never crossed my mind to contemplate that question. He appeared thoroughly uninterested, and I recalled that the magazine’s images depicted skaters in their meticulous costumes as having vacant expressions, seemingly devoid of enthusiasm. I have aspirations to learn the "rocker" and the "mohawk"; perhaps if I discovered the correct attire for skating on a smaller river, it might inspire me. Yet, the thought of boredom—why, even hockey, with all its excitement, doesn't bore me unless I stop to observe it! I can understand why those who participate might look disinterested. Even Alexander, who was engaged in far more imaginative pursuits than hockey, seemed to share that ennui—poor soul; if only he had embraced the art of fancy skating earlier in life! I have yet to meet anyone who claims to have fully mastered it.

Pond skating, particularly under the moonlight, holds a special charm for me. The hollow nestled among the hills always seems to retain a hint of mist, regardless of how clear the sky might be. The moonlight, which appears so bright and clear when looking up, transforms into an ethereal blend of pearl and smoke around the pond and hills. The shore, which felt like cold iron beneath my feet as I approached the ice, takes on an indistinct quality when viewed from the pond’s center, reminiscent of a dream’s fading memory. Gliding across the surface feels akin to flying within a dream; one floats effortlessly while the world glides beneath. Speed is achieved without strain or intent, as no traces are left behind, and nothing waits to be reached. Gazing upward, I notice the mist hovering above; the moon appears ensconced in a "hollow halo" at the bottom of a crystalline cup, and I find myself encased in something similar. The mist, pale and opalescent, swirls past, emerging from nothing and fading into nowhere. Like me, the moon serves as the focal point of a circle that is vaguely defined, filled with a continuous stream of impressions that flow through a softly glowing halo of awareness.

While the mist enchants the emotions like delicate, enchanting music in the moonlight, it also offers a different kind of beauty in sunlight. More often than not, when I venture to skate on our quaint little river, winding a mile from the mill-dam to the railroad trestle, the hills are enveloped in silver mist, creating vignettes with softly blurred edges. The scene evokes the essence of Japanese paintings on white silk, with colors appearing muted yet gentle through the frost-laden air. At the mill-dam, the hockey players enthusiastically clash, but I pay them no mind. Soon, I glide beyond the initial bend, where their raucous sounds diminish, echoing softly like the distant chatter of a political gathering of crows. A fine layer of silver powder blankets the ice, just enough to obscure previous tracks and provide me with a fresh canvas to inscribe with delicate grapevines and intricate arabesques. The river winds ahead, resembling an unbroken path adorned with soft, blurred shadows of violet, indigo, and lavender. To one side, birch, oak, maple, hickory, and clusters of hemlocks lean towards the ice, their presence infusing the air with a hint of green. On the opposite bank, rounded masses of scrub oak and alder recede from the water’s edge like billowing clouds of reddish smoke. As the river narrows and meanders, it opens into a marshland, where I weave my curves around the straw-colored tussocks. Despite the pristine snow, I discover earlier tracks; a crow has left behind parallel patterns, alternating footprints with long dashes where it dragged its middle toe while walking. Beneath a low shrub that has scattered its seeds, a delicate embroidery of tiny bird footprints unfolds in irregular curves, forming a circular design. A gentle glide toward the bank brings me into the company of a group of pine siskins diligently rifling through the seed cones of birch and alder, their low chattering creating a soft symphony. Further along, where the river narrows again, I spot the swift line of a rabbit, its path seemingly defined with mathematical precision. There are no tracks to pursue; did it flee from its own shadow or that of a hawk?

The mist clings to the bases of the hills, leaving the upper ridges subtly shrouded and softening the rounded peaks. The snowy slopes are intricately etched by fine brush and trees, reminiscent of Dürer’s engravings—like the fur on Saint Jerome’s lion or the feathers of the cock in a coat of arms featuring a skull. From behind the veil of the southernmost hill, I catch a faint sound, as if from invisible lips blowing an ethereal horn.

It is the distant yet familiar prelude of the noon train; I pause, eagerly awaiting its next sign. Eventually, I hear its rhythmic thrum, which quiets as it halts at the flag station below the hill. From the unseen locomotive, a plume of silver vapor rises above the mist, breaking into puffy clouds at the top, resembling a still image of a submarine mine explosion—a tremendous burst of energy frozen in time, akin to a frosted elm tree. Then, quick puffs of dark smoke emerge, their sound reaching me only after the train has breached the veil of enchantment, transforming into a mundane reminder of the approaching mealtime. It leaps high across the narrow trestle, crossing my little river, before vanishing between the sandy banks. The mist settles back into its tranquil layers, restoring silence, allowing me to hear the melodic creaking of four starlings in an apple tree as they feast on a few rotten apples still clinging to the upper branches. I turn and carve my way down the winding curves of the river, not pausing for intricate designs or embellishments. The chaotic hockey game at the mill-dam continues unabated; the players remain absorbed in their pursuit, their focus unwavering.

Their attention is riveted on a battered disk of hard rubber. I begin to reconsider my initial assessment of them; what an extraordinary exercise of imagination must be involved in concentrating their faculties on such an object, transcending the call of hunger and the allure of beauty. Could it be for them what the mystic perceives in the "great syllable Om," the means to attain Nirvana? I find I cannot reach that state; all I can do is ponder what the hockey players might gain that could rival the richness of experiences they overlook.
