In relation to admirals, I have a story to share that, despite its seemingly fanciful nature, is entirely true.

Once upon a time, there was a painter with a penchant for history, driven not solely by artistic passion but also by a desire for financial gain—after all, even idealists understand that money is as vital to their pursuits as fuel is to a vehicle. This blend of motivations led him to Portsmouth, home to the HMS Victory, where he intended to create a painting depicting the illustrious ship with Admiral Nelson aboard. As history tells us, Nelson was on the brink of death, and the artist, spurred on by the desire for both artistic acclaim and some new curtains for his wife, set out to make his work as captivating as possible. Not only did he anticipate selling the painting, but he also planned to produce reproductions to capitalize on the wave of naval fervor sweeping the nation.

After securing permission from the relevant authorities, the artist boarded the Victory, established his easel on the deck, and immersed himself in his task. The monotony of painting was pleasantly interrupted by the banter of the old sailors stationed on the ship, who also served as guides for the many tourists visiting. These seasoned gentlemen, convinced they had personally fought alongside Nelson and witnessed his demise, provided a wealth of opinions on art that were difficult to counter. As a result, the artist found himself fully engaged, painting, chatting, and absorbing knowledge from these informal experts. Days passed swiftly, and before he knew it, the painting was complete.

Up to this point, everything seems plausible. However, an extraordinary twist awaited.

On the final evening of his stay, while enjoying an early dinner with a friend before catching a train back to London, the artist began discussing his recent work. He expressed general satisfaction but lamented that the painting’s potential success could be significantly enhanced if he could find a model who bore a striking resemblance to Nelson. 

"It seems to be a universal truth," he mused, "that contemporaneous individuals cannot possess identical facial features. This is a given. Oddly enough, despite the countless faces in existence, no two are exactly the same, even with their two eyes, a nose, and a mouth. There are always subtle differences." At this point, he was relishing the sound of his own thoughts. "This holds true for those alive today. However," he elaborated, "there's no reason to assume that over the years, Nature could not start replicating faces, sparing herself the effort of constant variation. If that's a sound hypothesis—and surely it is," his friend chimed in with agreement, "then why shouldn't there be someone today, over a century later, who looks exactly like Nelson? This individual wouldn’t necessarily be a sailor—probably not, actually, since Nelson's visage wasn’t particularly maritime in character. But whoever he might be, even if he were an archbishop, I," the artist insisted, "would have no qualms about approaching him to pose for me."

His friend concurred that this was a commendable position to take, demonstrating the artist's genuine intent.

"Nelson’s face was quite distinctive," the painter continued. "Such a large and expressive mouth is rare. Yet, it is entirely plausible that a perfect doppelgänger exists, and no matter who he might be—even if he were an archbishop—I would unabashedly approach him and ask him to model for me."

(To clarify for any female readers who may notice the repetition, I assure you that it is intentional. This stylistic choice aims to lend authenticity to the dialogue, as men often repeat themselves in conversation.)

His friend once more acknowledged the artist’s determination as praiseworthy, and together they made their way to the train station, still engrossed in their discussion.

Upon entering their carriage, they were immediately drawn to a modestly dressed man in black who bore an uncanny resemblance to the famed figure from Trafalgar.

"My goodness!" the painter whispered excitedly. "Do you see that? There’s the very man! The resemblance to Nelson is astounding. I’ve never seen anything quite like it. I must speak to him; this is the most extraordinary luck!"

Adopting a particularly suave and respectful demeanor—after all, while the man didn’t appear to be an archbishop, he could still hold some significance—the painter approached the stranger and presented his business card.

"Excuse my boldness, sir," he began, "but I am an artist currently working on a painting of Nelson aboard the Victory. I have all the necessary elements, but what I truly require is a brief sitting from someone who resembles the great Admiral—someone like yourself. It may come as a surprise, but you, sir, are so strikingly similar to the illustrious warrior that it quite takes one's breath away. It’s remarkable! Might I—would it be—could you—would you be so kind as to allow me to paint your portrait? I promise to minimize any inconvenience to you—I’ll arrange for your time to fit mine."

"Of course, I will," replied the man. "I’m a professional model, and I've been posing as Nelson for years. In fact, I just finished a sitting for another artist this very afternoon."
