Over a month ago, as I prepared to depart from London for a vacation, a friend entered my Battersea flat and observed my chaotic packing.

“You seem to be heading off somewhere,” he remarked. “Where are you headed?”

With a strap clenched between my teeth, I managed to respond, “To Battersea.”

“The cleverness of your statement is lost on me,” he replied.

“I am indeed going to Battersea,” I clarified, “but via Paris, Belfort, Heidelberg, and Frankfurt. My remark holds no humor; it is simply a fact. I intend to traverse the globe until I rediscover Battersea. Somewhere amidst the seas of dawn or dusk, in the farthest reaches of the earth, lies a small isle that I aim to uncover: one adorned with gentle green hills and towering white cliffs. Travelers inform me that it is known as England (while Scottish travelers assert it is called Britain), and I have heard whispers that at its core lies a lovely place called Battersea.”

“I guess it’s unnecessary to remind you,” my friend said, with an air of superiority, “that this is Battersea?”

“Unnecessary indeed,” I replied, “and it lacks spiritual truth. I cannot perceive any Battersea here; neither can I discern London or England. I cannot even see that door or that chair; a veil of drowsiness and familiarity has clouded my vision. The only path back to clarity is to venture elsewhere. That is the essence of travel and the joy of holidays. Do you think I travel to France merely to see France? I will certainly enjoy both countries, but they are not my destination. My quest is to find Battersea. The true purpose of travel is not to step onto foreign soil; it is ultimately to return to one’s own homeland as if it were foreign. I must warn you, though, this Gladstone bag is quite heavy, and should you utter the word ‘paradox,’ I might just throw it at you. I didn’t create this world, nor did I make it paradoxical. It’s not my fault, but rather the truth, that the only route back to England involves departing from it.”

Yet, when I eventually returned to England after merely a month of travel, I was taken aback to realize that my earlier statement held true. England greeted me as both stunningly new and beautifully familiar. Arriving at Dover is indeed the ideal way to enter England, as it allows one to first behold the lush, gentle gardens of Kent, which, while perhaps exaggerated, still typify the rich rustic charm of the country. Coincidentally, I struck up a conversation with a fellow traveler, an American lady who had explored Europe but had yet to set foot in England. Her enthusiasm was expressed in that straightforward, exuberant manner typical of Americans, who, in my view, are the most idealistic people on earth. Their only risk is that an idealist can easily slip into idolization. Indeed, the American idealism extends even to the glorification of wealth. However, as a talented American short story writer might suggest, that is a tale for another time.

“I’ve never been to England before,” the American lady said. “Yet it’s so beautiful that it feels like I’ve been away from it for ages.”

“As a matter of fact, you have,” I replied. “You’ve been away for three centuries.”

“What an abundance of ivy you have!” she exclaimed. “It covers the churches and envelops the houses. We have ivy too, but I’ve never seen it grow in such a manner.”

“I’m intrigued to hear your perspective,” I said. “I’m currently compiling a list of the things that are genuinely better in England. A month on the Continent, coupled with a modicum of intelligence, reveals that many things are indeed better abroad. In fact, most of what the DAILY MAIL touts as English is notably superior elsewhere. But there are elements that are quintessentially English and undeniably good: kippers, Free Trade, front gardens, individual liberty, the Elizabethan drama, hansom cabs, cricket, and Mr. Will Crooks. Most importantly, there exists the cherished and sacred tradition of a hearty breakfast. I cannot envision Shakespeare starting his day with rolls and coffee like a Frenchman or a German. Surely, he began with bacon or bloaters. A realization dawns upon me; for the first time, I grasp the true significance of Mrs. Gallup and the Great Cipher. It all boils down to a mere misinterpretation involving a capital letter. I rescind my objections; I embrace everything; bacon must have penned Shakespeare.”

“I can’t focus on anything but the ivy,” she remarked. “It appears so inviting.”

As she admired the ivy, I picked up an English newspaper for the first time in weeks and read a speech by Mr. Balfour. He argued that the House of Lords should be preserved because it embodied a form of enduring public opinion in England, rising above the fluctuations of political parties. Mr. Balfour is a sincere patriot, a man who contemplates the public’s needs with considerable seriousness, and he possesses notable intellectual prowess. However, after reading his words, I felt a heavy sense of sadness as I realized I had to add yet another entry to my list of uniquely English phenomena: the distinctively English variety of humbug. In France, things are critiqued and defended based on their true nature. The Catholic Church is criticized for being Catholic and defended for the same reason. The Republic is supported because it is Republican and attacked for the very same reasons. Yet here we have one of England's most capable politicians assuring everyone that the House of Lords is not really what it seems, but something entirely different; that the foolish accidental peers he encounters each evening somehow possess insight into the psyche of the masses. If you wish to understand what the impoverished desire, he suggests, you must consult the wealthy, and if you seek the truth about Hoxton, you ought to inquire at Hatfield. If a Conservative defender of the House of Lords were a logical French politician, he would simply be a deceiver. However, in the realm of English politics, he becomes a poet. The English tendency to believe that everything is as it ought to be, combined with their unwavering optimism and vivid imagination, often obscures even the most apparent truths. In a cold, logical sense, Mr. Balfour is aware that most Lords who aren’t titled by accident have acquired their status through bribery. He knows this, and as Mr. Belloc aptly noted, everyone in Parliament is well aware of the names of those peers who bought their titles. Yet, the allure of comfort and the pleasure derived from self-reassurance are so compelling that this knowledge fades from view. Ultimately, he earnestly invites the English people to join him in venerating a noble and public-spirited Senate, having entirely forgotten that this Senate is, in fact, populated by individuals he himself has scorned and adventurers he has ennobled.

“Your ivy is so wonderfully soft and thick,” the American lady observed. “It seems to cover nearly everything. It must be the most poetic aspect of England.”

“It is indeed beautiful,” I replied, “and, as you noted, it is very much part of England. Charles Dickens, who was perhaps more English than England itself, wrote one of his rare poems celebrating the beauty of ivy. So let us, by all means, admire the ivy—so deep, warm, and imbued with a genial gloom and a grotesque tenderness. Let us appreciate the ivy; and let us fervently pray to God that it does not strangle the tree.”
