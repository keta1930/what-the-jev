Lying in bed could be a truly exquisite and unparalleled experience, provided one had a colored pencil long enough to create art on the ceiling above. Unfortunately, such tools are rarely a standard feature of modern homes. I think it might be possible to achieve this effect with a few buckets of paint and a broom. If one could wield the broom with sweeping mastery, applying thick layers of color, it might cascade down in vibrant torrents, resembling a fantastical rain of hues. However, this notion comes with its drawbacks. In truth, I believe one must limit themselves to monochrome in this artistic endeavor. The white ceiling, in this case, becomes immensely valuable; it stands as the sole practical use I can conceive for such a white expanse.

Had it not been for the enlightening experience of lying in bed for an extended period, I might never have stumbled upon this realization. For years, I sought out unblemished surfaces in contemporary homes to sketch upon. Unfortunately, standard paper felt far too small for any grand allegorical work; as Cyrano de Bergerac aptly states, “Il me faut des géants.” Each time I searched for open spaces within the modern rooms we inhabit, I was met with disappointment. I encountered a relentless array of intricate patterns and small objects that seemed to weave a dense curtain between me and my creative aspirations. Upon inspecting the walls, I was astonished to find them adorned with wallpaper, which itself was cluttered with uninspired designs that bore a striking resemblance to one another. I could not fathom why a single arbitrary motif, devoid of any significant religious or philosophical meaning, should be plastered across my pristine walls like an unsightly rash. I suspect that the Bible’s admonition against “vain repetitions” refers to wallpapers. The Turkey carpet, too, was a chaotic blend of colors, reminiscent of the Turkish Empire or perhaps the confection known as Turkish Delight. Though I’m uncertain what Turkish Delight truly is, it evokes thoughts of the Macedonian Massacres. Everywhere I roamed, armed with my pencil or paintbrush, I found that others had inexplicably beaten me to the punch, marring the walls, drapes, and furnishings with their childish and primitive designs.

It wasn’t until I lingered longer than usual in bed that I uncovered a pristine canvas above me. At that moment, the expanse of white emerged, a celestial ceiling that could symbolize purity and freedom, perhaps even the very definition of Paradise itself. Yet, like all visions of heaven, this one proved elusive; it appeared more stark and remote than the blue sky visible through the window. My ambitious idea of painting it with the bristly end of a broom was quickly discouraged—though I shan’t specify by whom, as that person has been stripped of all political agency. Even my modest suggestion to use the broom’s opposite end to make charcoal in the kitchen fire was met with disapproval. Nevertheless, I am convinced that the original inspiration for the elaborate ceilings of palaces and cathedrals sprang from individuals in my position. Surely, it was through the ancient and noble act of lying in bed that Michelangelo envisioned transforming the Sistine Chapel’s ceiling into a grand portrayal of celestial drama, worthy of divine interpretation.

The contemporary attitude towards the act of lying in bed strikes me as disingenuous and unhealthy. Among the many signs of modernity that suggest a decline in values, none is as alarming as the elevation of trivial behaviors at the expense of significant moral principles and profound human connections. It seems that in our current climate, it is deemed more damaging to accuse someone of poor taste than to call into question their ethical integrity. Today, cleanliness is not next to godliness; rather, cleanliness is treated as mandatory while godliness is often seen as a vice. A playwright may critique marriage, provided they accurately reflect societal manners, and I have met pessimists influenced by Ibsen who believe it wrong to consume beer while considering the ingestion of prussic acid permissible. This hypocrisy is especially pronounced regarding hygiene, particularly concerning the act of lying in bed. Instead of viewing it, as it should be, as a matter of personal choice and comfort, many now perceive rising early as a moral obligation. While early rising can indeed reflect practical wisdom, it is neither inherently good nor bad in comparison to its opposite.

Misers reportedly rise with the dawn, while I’ve been told burglars often wake just before nightfall. The true danger to our society lies in the potential for its mechanisms to become overly rigid, even as its spirit becomes increasingly capricious. A man’s minor choices and routines should remain adaptable, fluid, and imaginative; what should remain steadfast are his principles and ideals. Yet, in our current reality, the opposite holds true; our opinions shift frequently, while our lunch remains unchanged. I would prefer for individuals to hold strong, enduring beliefs, but as for their meals, they ought to enjoy them in the garden, in bed, on the roof, or even perched high in a tree. Let them debate from the same foundational ideas, but do so in a bed, a boat, or a balloon. This troubling rise of rigid habits reflects an excessive focus on virtues that mere convention can assure, while neglecting those deeper values that cannot be guaranteed by routine—values of spontaneous compassion or candid honesty. Should we ever be called to such an abrupt moral challenge, we may falter. A man can become accustomed to rising at five in the morning, but adapting to being persecuted for one’s beliefs is often a fatal first trial. We ought to pay closer attention to the potential for the heroic and the unexpected. I suspect that upon leaving my bed, I may embark on some act of remarkable virtue.

For those who master the fine art of lying in bed, there exists one crucial caution. Even for those who manage to accomplish their tasks while reclining (such as journalists), or especially for those whose professions cannot be conducted from bed (like whale harpooners), it is essential that this indulgence remains rare. However, that is not the caution I wish to emphasize. The real warning is this: if you choose to lie in bed, do so without any pretext or justification. I am not referring to those who are genuinely unwell. But for a healthy individual, if you lie in bed, let it be without any shred of excuse; only then will you rise revitalized. Should you do so for some secondary hygienic rationale or with a scientific justification in mind, you may find yourself ascending as a hypochondriac.
