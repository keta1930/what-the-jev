I have yet to encounter a narrative about the war that resonates with me as deeply as the account Mr. Alfred Noyes shared in the newspapers regarding his visit to the Fleet. This tale centers on the Battle of Jutland. "In the very hottest moment of this most stupendous battle in all history," he recounts, "two grimy stokers' heads arose for a breath of fresh air. What domestic drama they were discussing the world may never know. But the words that were actually heard passing between them, while the shells whined overhead, were these: 'What I says is, 'e ought to have married 'er.'"

If you find this story unappealing, you will struggle to grasp the essence of the English spirit. Some individuals among us may never truly understand it. In the war's early days, a close friend of mine was deeply troubled by the song "Tipperary." He questioned how a nation could send its soldiers into battle singing "Tipperary" when their opponents marched forward chanting "Ein' feste Burg." Framed in that manner, I had to concede that the situation appeared grim for us. It seemed "all Lombard Street to a China orange," as an old saying goes. While "Tipperary" was somewhat refreshing and original for a music-hall tune, it stood in stark contrast to the sentimental "Keep the home fires burning," which dominates today’s landscape. I still experience a bittersweet pleasure whenever I hear it on a street organ; its joyful melody is inevitably intertwined with the profound emotions of those unforgettable days, much like a butterfly caught in a tempest.

Yet, in the end, it was merely a music-hall tune, and comparing it to Luther’s powerful hymn would be akin to pitting a pop-gun against a formidable artillery piece. The resonance of Luther's hymn has echoed through four centuries and will continue to reverberate through time until the end of days. It embodies the epic clash of elements, akin to the rumble of mountains and the swell of oceans. Nowhere else is the embodiment of Power so vividly expressed in song. The lyrics are just as formidable as the melody. Carlyle captured their raw, robust strength in his renowned translation:

A safe stronghold our God is still, A trusty shield and weapon; He'll help us clear from all the ill That hath us now o'ertaken….

On the surface, it indeed appeared that "Tipperary" stood little chance against such a powerful opponent. However, those familiar with the English temperament understood that it was not as dire as it seemed. I set aside the notion that a light-hearted tune is far more effective than a solemn one for practical purposes. "Tipperary" lifts the spirits and quickens the pace, while Luther’s hymn would weigh heavily upon one’s journey. The mind would struggle under its gravity; one might either descend into madness or resort to reckless behavior to regain composure. It is a foolish philosophy to believe that serious endeavors necessitate a constant state of exaltation, that one must always remain tense and focused. Wearing a long face does not enhance performance; on the contrary, it hampers it. If one is consumed by discussions of lofty ideals all day, not only do they become a nuisance, but they might also be either insincere or unhinged. We are not beings with wings but rather creatures who walk. Even the journey to Mount Pisgah requires us to "foot it," and the more cheerful and grounded we remain en route, the quicker we shall reach our destination. The most noble Englishman ever known, deeply serious yet filled with innocent mirth, laid down his head upon the block with a jest. Let us navigate by the stars, certainly, but let’s not lose sight of the immediate tasks that are much closer than celestial bodies—

The charities that soothe and heal and bless Are scattered all about our feet—like flowers.

It is precisely the dark gravity of the German psyche that has driven them to madness. They have not learned to engage in play; they have not grasped the art of laughing at themselves. Their somber religion has devolved into a bleak irreligion. They have become heavy without acquiring a light-hearted spirit. Luther's spirited battle hymn has morphed into a materialistic battle cry, wherein "the safe stronghold" is now the City of Krupp rather than the City of God. They lack both the intellectual clarity of the French and the earthy humor that characterizes the English. This very humor has posed a conundrum for Europe. It has baffled the French just as much as the Germans since the French genius is inherently dramatic and thrives on grand ideas and passions expressed with great fervor. It was assumed that because British soldiers sang "Tipperary," surrounded by a milieu of everyday amusement, devoid of heroics, and rarely discussing "glory," patriotism, or the Fatherland, they were devoid of any significant passion. Some of our more fervent citizens at home entertain a similar notion. They continue to believe we are a nation of "slackers" simply because we do not join their outcries.

The reality, however, is that the English spirit tends to be wary of overt emotion and theatricality. There is a certain embarrassment in making "a fuss," and heroics are met with disdain. The average Englishman often conceals his feelings, even from family members, masking affection with a veneer of indifference, and resorts to humor to avoid appearing foolish. It is not that he lacks deep passions; rather, he feels uncomfortable discussing them. He is too self-conscious to trust his own tongue on such grand themes for fear of "making an exhibition of himself," which he dreads above all else. This tendency toward reticence has its unsightly aspects, but it also possesses admirable traits. It fosters a cool, practical mindset and cultivates a commonplace, good-humored atmosphere. It provides reserves of strength that those who live on their "top notes" do not possess. It continues to hum "Tipperary" as if it were free of cares, indifferent to ideas or causes. And then, when the moment arrives, the immense passion that has been quietly contained bursts forth into actions that are as glorious as those achieved on the plains of windy Troy. Look to the stories of the Victoria Cross recipients, and then consider whether a nation capable of such noble heroism deserves to be subjected to the tyranny of any petty official who seeks to belittle us.

Those two stokers, lifting their heads for a moment of fresh air amidst the chaos of battle, exemplify the quintessential English character. Death loomed all around them, and any instant could have marked their last. Yet, they maintained such complete control over themselves that during their brief respite, they could divert their thoughts to a simple matter of daily conduct: "What I says is, 'e ought to have married 'er." This may not embody the essence of heroics, but it captures the very fabric of heroism itself.
