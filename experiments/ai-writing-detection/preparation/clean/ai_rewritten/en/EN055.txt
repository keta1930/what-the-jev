Occasionally, I have shared true stories within this newspaper column, and while I don’t claim that my approach is unique among other columns, I have discovered that real-life anecdotes often convey my thoughts more effectively than any other means. Thus, I want to recount the tale of an unusual cab driver I encountered just three days ago, an episode that, despite its seemingly trivial nature, stirred a genuine emotional response in me, teetering on the edge of despair.

On the day I met this peculiar cabman, I had just finished lunch at a quaint restaurant in Soho, surrounded by a few close friends. My companions are split between those who are staunch skeptics and those who believe fervently in various ideas. Our lunchtime conversation veered into profound and unsettling philosophical concepts. Ultimately, we grappled with the fundamental question: can a person truly be certain of anything? In my view, certainty does exist. I argued passionately with my friend, waving an empty bottle as if it were a sword, saying that if it were impossible to grasp certainty intellectually, then what was this certainty that we could not grasp? If I had never encountered certainty, I would be unable to assert that something is not certain. Likewise, if I had never experienced the color green, I would be incapable of claiming my nose was not green; it could indeed be as green as could be, for all I know, if I had never seen green before. Our voices rose in heated debate, filling the room with fervor, for metaphysics stirs deep emotions. The divide between us ran deep, touching on the essence of broad-mindedness and intellectual openness. My friend maintained that he opened his mind like the sun unfurling the fronds of a palm tree, endlessly and infinitely. I countered that I opened my mind as one opens their mouth, with the purpose of eventually closing it around something substantial. I was, in fact, doing just that at that moment. I pointed out how absurd it would be to keep my mouth agape indefinitely.

Once our argument subsided, or at least was temporarily interrupted—because such discussions can never truly conclude—I departed with a friend who, amidst the chaotic atmosphere of a General Election, had somehow become a Member of Parliament. I accompanied him in a cab from the corner of Leicester Square to the members’ entrance of the House of Commons, where the police greeted me with a surprising level of tolerance. We still jest about whether they believed he was my guardian or that I was his.

It is essential to recount this story with precise detail. After dropping my friend off at the House, I continued to my destination, a short distance away in Victoria Street. Upon reaching my office, I exited the cab and offered the driver more than the required fare. Instead of the usual skepticism or attempts to negotiate, the cabman regarded the money with a genuine and almost childlike astonishment. “You do realize, sir,” he said, “you’ve only given me 1s.8d?” I responded, somewhat taken aback, that I was indeed aware of that fact. “Now you know, sir,” he continued in a friendly, reasonable tone, “that isn’t the fare from Euston.” “Euston?” I echoed, the word sounding as foreign to me as a distant land. “What does Euston have to do with this?” “You hailed me just outside Euston Station,” he stated with remarkable precision, “and then you said—” “What in the world are you talking about?” I interjected, trying to maintain my composure. “I picked you up at the south-west corner of Leicester Square.” “Leicester Square?” he exclaimed, unleashing a torrent of disbelief. “We haven’t been near Leicester Square today! You hailed me outside Euston Station, and you said—” “Are you mad, or am I?” I asked, striving to remain calm.

I scrutinized the cabman. No ordinary dishonest driver would concoct such a substantial and intricate falsehood. This man was not dishonest; if anything, he appeared profoundly sincere. His face was heavy and simple, adorned with large, frog-like blue eyes that seemed to radiate humility. I glanced up and down the street, noticing that an unusually dense twilight was creeping in. In that fleeting moment, the familiar fear of skepticism crept into my mind. What, indeed, is certainty? Can anyone be sure of anything? It was astonishing to think of the skeptics who relentlessly ponder whether an afterlife exists. The more intriguing question for true skepticism is whether we have ever lived before. What is a minute ago, when analyzed through a rational lens, other than a memory and a mere representation? The shadows deepened around us as the cabman continued to recount with meticulous detail the gestures and words I had used from the moment I had supposedly hailed him outside Euston Station. How could I be certain—my skeptical friends would argue—that I hadn’t hailed him there? I stood firm in my belief, while he remained equally resolute. He was as honest as I was, a representative of a profession I considered far more reputable than many. At that instant, it felt as though the universe itself had shifted slightly, unsettling the very foundations of reality. Yet, for the same reasons I hold faith in democracy, free will, and the enduring nature of virtue, I refused to succumb to madness. I reiterated that I had genuinely picked him up at Leicester Square. He persisted with his unwavering sincerity, “You hailed me outside Euston Station, and you said—”

Just then, an extraordinary transformation swept across his face, illuminating it with genuine astonishment, as if he had been switched on from within. “Why, I beg your pardon, sir,” he said, almost stammering. “I beg your pardon. I beg your pardon. You took me from Leicester Square. I remember now. I beg your pardon.” With that, this remarkable cabman cracked his whip, urging his horse onward, and he drove away. I solemnly swear, under the banner of St. George, that this entire encounter is true.

As I watched the peculiar cabman recede into the distance, swallowed by the encroaching twilight, I pondered whether I was mistaken in sensing an otherworldly, even demonic, quality about him when viewed from behind. Perhaps he had come to challenge my commitment to the certainties I had defended earlier that day. Regardless, it brought me comfort to reflect on the fact that my understanding of reality, though shaken for a brief moment, remained intact.
