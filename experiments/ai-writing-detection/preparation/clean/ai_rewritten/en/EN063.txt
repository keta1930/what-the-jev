Experiencing a tinge of primal jealousy, I find myself reflecting on the recent flooding of London while I am confined to the countryside. It seems that my beloved Battersea has been particularly blessed with the convergence of waters. It goes without saying that Battersea is already one of the most exquisite places inhabited by humans. Now, with the added beauty of expansive sheets of water, the scenery of my cherished town must be truly unparalleled. Battersea must resemble a vision straight out of Venice. The boat responsible for delivering meat from the butcher must have glided along those shimmering lanes with the elegance of a gondola. Similarly, the greengrocer ferrying cabbages to the corner of Latchmere Road must have rowed with the ethereal grace of a gondolier. There is something profoundly poetic about an island; when a neighborhood is submerged, it transforms into an archipelago.

Some may argue that such romanticized perspectives on floods or fires lack a grounding in reality. Yet, I contend that this optimistic viewpoint is just as practical as any other. The true optimist, who perceives in such occurrences an opportunity for delight, is often more logical and sensible than the typical “Indignant Ratepayer,” who merely sees a chance to complain. Genuine pain—like the agony of a toothache or the torment of being burned at Smithfield—is tangible and can be endured, though it seldom brings joy. However, our experiences of toothaches are rare, and such severe misfortunes occur infrequently. Most of the inconveniences that prompt men to curse or women to weep are largely sentimental or imaginative—issues that exist primarily in the mind. For instance, adults often grumble about waiting at a train station. Have you ever heard a child voice such dissatisfaction? No—because to a child, a train station is a cavern of wonder and a palace of poetic delight. The red and green lights at the signals appear to them as new celestial bodies. When the wooden arm of the signal drops swiftly, it is as though a grand king has signaled the commencement of a thrilling tournament among trains. I share this childlike wonder in these moments. Even those who merely stand and wait for the 2:15 train can have their thoughts filled with rich and rewarding reflections. Many of the most profound moments of my life have been spent at Clapham Junction, now likely submerged. I have found myself in such deep contemplation there that I might not have noticed if the water had risen to my waist. Yet, as I’ve mentioned, the emotional perspective one adopts determines the experience of these annoyances. 

Consider the common belief that chasing after one’s hat is an unpleasant endeavor. What makes it so for a well-adjusted and virtuous mind? It’s not merely the act of running, which can be exhausting. Those same individuals often run much faster during sports, eagerly pursuing an unremarkable leather ball with more enthusiasm than they would for a fine silk hat. The notion that running after one’s hat is somehow humiliating stems from the implication of comedy. Indeed, it is comedic; however, humanity is inherently comedic, and much of what we do is tinged with absurdity—eating, for instance. The most humorous actions are often the most worthwhile, such as the pursuit of love. A person chasing after a hat is far less ridiculous than one pursuing a spouse.

A man could, with a proper mindset, chase after his hat with genuine fervor and joy. He might envision himself as a spirited hunter in pursuit of a wild creature, for no animal is quite as elusive as a hat on a windy day. I suspect that in the future, hat-hunting on blustery days will evolve into a sport for the upper class. Social gatherings will take place on hilltops during gusty mornings, where participants will be informed that a professional has released a hat in a specific thicket—whatever the technical term may be. This activity would seamlessly blend sport with a sense of humanitarianism. The participants would feel they were not causing harm; rather, they would be providing pure, almost riotous joy to onlookers. The last time I witnessed an elderly gentleman chasing after his hat in Hyde Park, I remarked that a heart as kind as his should be filled with gratitude, reflecting on how much innocent joy his every gesture was bringing to the crowd at that moment.

This same principle of perspective can be applied to other common domestic frustrations. For instance, a man might be troubled by a fly in his milk or a piece of cork in his wine and assume he is irritated. If he takes a moment to consider the patience of anglers waiting by dark pools, he may find his spirit uplifted by a sense of calm. I have known individuals with modern sensibilities who, in their distress, resorted to theological language devoid of doctrinal significance when faced with a stubborn drawer they couldn’t open. One friend of mine was particularly tormented by this issue; each day, his drawer would jam, leading to a daily outburst that mirrored its rhyme. I suggested to him that this sense of frustration was entirely subjective and hinged on the belief that the drawer should easily yield to his efforts. “But if,” I proposed, “you visualize yourself struggling against a formidable and oppressive foe, the endeavor shifts from exasperating to exhilarating. Imagine you are hauling a lifeboat from the sea. Picture yourself rescuing a fellow being from an Alpine crevasse. Or perhaps, recall the childhood joy of a tug-of-war between the French and the English.” Shortly after this conversation, I departed, but I remain confident that my words had a positive impact. I envision him now, gripping that drawer handle with a flushed face and bright eyes, rallying himself with words of encouragement, all while imagining the applause of a cheering crowd.

Thus, I do not believe it is merely fanciful or implausible to suggest that even the floods in London can be embraced and appreciated from a poetic standpoint. The situation seems to have caused little more than inconvenience, and inconvenience, as I have posited, is merely one perspective—often unimaginative and coincidental—of a truly romantic scenario. An adventure is simply an inconvenience viewed through the wrong lens. Conversely, an inconvenience can be seen as an adventure if perceived correctly. The water that enveloped the homes and shops of London must, in fact, have only amplified their inherent charm and mystery. As a Roman Catholic priest in a story once said, “Wine pairs well with everything, except water,” and similarly, water complements everything except wine.
