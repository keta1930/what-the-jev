For a considerable period, I found myself meandering through the tranquil streets of the intriguing town of Besançon, which is nestled like a peninsula encircled by a river in the shape of a horseshoe. While guidebooks may inform visitors that this town is the birthplace of Victor Hugo and a military outpost replete with forts near the French border, they fail to capture the enchanting details that make this place truly special. The tiles on the rooftops here seem to possess a unique charm, a quaint and delicate hue that distinguishes them from those found in other towns worldwide. They evoke the imagery of ethereal clouds at twilight or the shimmering scales of an extraordinary fish. The allure of Besançon lies in its ability to captivate the eye at every turn, whether it's a meticulously carved face peering out from a street corner, a glimpse of verdant fields through a weathered archway, or an unexpected splash of color adorning a spire or dome.

As evening approached, the fading light made the town's colors, so simple yet so intricate, blend together into a scene reminiscent of a fairy tale. I decided to take a moment to rest outside a café adorned with a row of small ornamental trees. Soon, the driver of a horse-drawn carriage, a type of vehicle I would refer to as a fly, joined me. He was a robust and dark-skinned Frenchman, a figure not frequently encountered but still representative of France; he resembled a Rabelaisian character—large, swarthy, and with a face the color of ripe plums, reminiscent of a personification of indulgence. If one were to imagine Falstaff, the quintessential English character, it would be difficult to envision him in any guise other than that of a jovial, jiggling figure, embodying the comedic spirit of London streets. In contrast, this Frenchman exuded an air of solemnity and dignity, as if enjoyment itself were a sacred ritual. After engaging in a conversation marked by the admirable politeness characteristic of French culture, he subtly suggested—without a hint of eagerness or embarrassment—that I join him for an hour-long ride into the hills that lay beyond the town. Despite the encroaching darkness, I readily agreed, drawn by the allure of a long, winding road that beckoned me like a silken thread.

We passed through a robust, squat gateway constructed by the Romans, a moment I vividly recall as a sort of omen. As we exited the city, I simultaneously heard three distinct sounds that together formed the essence of France. These sounds create what a poet once described as a “tangled trinity,” a concept I have no intention of unraveling. What those three elements signify, how they coexist, and whether they can somehow be harmonized is a mystery that eludes me. At that moment, I heard the brass band in the casino gardens behind me, playing a lively tune from a Parisian operetta, its spirited notes contrasted sharply with the bugles echoing from the hills above, heralding the eternal loyalty and readiness of the French people. Interwoven with these robust sounds was the faint, melodic toll of the Angelus, creating a rich tapestry of sound that enveloped me.

This convergence of symbols instilled within me a distinct sensation that I had left not only France but perhaps even the civilized world itself. The landscape around me was wild enough to support such a notion. While I have encountered taller mountains, I had never seen rocks towering so dramatically. The sheer height was astonishing, with craggy formations jutting upward like gothic church spires, and cliffs dropping abruptly and straight down, reminiscent of the fall of Satan from heaven. The experience of riding through this rugged terrain was not only awe-inspiring but also somewhat disorienting, a sensation that many who have traversed mountain roads at speed might recognize. It was as if the entire earth was revolving around my head; the hills did not merely rise and fall like massive waves but appeared to spin around me like the enormous sails of a windmill, forming a colossal wheel of angelic wings.

As we ascended further into the deepening hues of sunset, this dizzying effect intensified, blurring the boundaries between the earth and sky. Towering walls of wooded rock loomed above me, giving the impression of a great, protective canopy. I gazed at the slopes below, which seemed to cascade toward the river, only to feel as though they were climbing upward to meet the heavens. The twilight deepened, drawing the shadows longer and accentuating the stark chasms and towering spires of that surreal landscape. Above me, the imposing figure of the driver loomed, his broad back shrouded in mystery, reminiscent of the enigmatic presence of Death in Watts' painting. Feeling increasingly fanciful, I sought to ground myself in ordinary conversation. I called out to the driver in French, inquiring, "Where are you taking me?" His answer came without him turning around, delivered in the same language: "To the end of the world."

I refrained from responding, allowing him to guide the carriage up treacherous, steep paths until we stumbled upon a few lights beneath a low canopy of diminutive trees, where two children—one notably beautiful—were engaged in a game of ball. Soon, we emerged onto the main street of a quaint hamlet, where the words LE BOUT DU MONDE—French for "the end of the world"—were boldly inscribed on the wall of a local inn.

Without exchanging a word, the driver and I settled outside that inn, an unspoken understanding hanging in the air like a shared secret, natural in this ultimate location. I ordered bread and a robust red wine that bore no label, savoring its quality. Across the way stood a modest church, adorned with a cross and a rooster perched atop it. To me, this represented a fitting conclusion to the world; if the tale of existence were to end here, it would do so on a note of beauty. I mused over whether I could truly find contentment in this place, where the best elements of Christendom converged—a church, children at play, fertile land, and a tavern where men could engage in conversation. However, amid these thoughts, an unusual doubt and desire began to swell within me, prompting me to rise abruptly.

“Are you not satisfied?” my companion asked. “No,” I replied, “I am not satisfied, even at the end of the world.”

After a brief silence, I continued, “You see, there are two ends of the world. This is the wrong end of the world; at least, it is the wrong one for me. This is the French end of the world. I crave the other end of the world. Please, take me to the other end of the world.”

“The other end of the world?” he queried. “Where might that be?”

“It is in Walham Green,” I whispered, my voice hoarse with urgency. “You can see it on London’s omnibuses. ‘World’s End and Walham Green.’ While I cherish this place and its vineyards, and I admire your free peasantry, I long for the English end of the world. I appreciate you as a brother, but I yearn to encounter an English cab driver, who will humorously inquire about my fare. Your bugle calls stir my emotions, yet I find myself longing to see a London policeman. Please, oh please, take me to see a London policeman.”

The driver stood silently, his silhouette dark against the backdrop of the setting sun, and I was unable to discern whether he comprehended my sentiments. I climbed back into the carriage, hoping for understanding.

"You will grasp my feelings," I said, “if you ever find yourself an exile, even for the sake of pleasure. The yearning of a child for its mother, the longing of a man for his homeland, as a fellow countryman of yours once expressed. However, as it seems a rather lengthy journey to the English end of the world, we might as well return to Besançon."

As the stars began to twinkle among those timeless hills, I mourned for Walham Green, feeling the weight of my longing for home.
