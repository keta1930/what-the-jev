With the decline of swords as fashionable accessories, walking sticks have undoubtedly taken their place. This modern trend certainly brings with it certain benefits. Even the mightiest of heroes must have found their swords sheathed at times during their adventures; in such moments, what would they hold in their right hand? Yet, walking sticks lack any scabbards. We clutch them continuously, ever prepared to hail a cab, to showcase a scenic view, or to jab a foe if necessary. Amidst this, we brandish them defiantly against the world.

My first cane was a malacca, adorned with a silver collar and a polished horn handle. For weeks, it gazed longingly at me from a shop window until a fortunate birthday gift finally allowed me to claim it. That afternoon, we returned to school together, and nothing could quite match the joy of having such a stick on the last day of summer break. Of course, its beauty was destined to be short-lived, but even in its demise, it retained a certain charm. I had left many a parental umbrella neglected in train carriages without a second thought, but my malacca was lost in a hotel in Norway. To this day, when the curtains are drawn and we gather by the warm fire, sharing tales of distant adventures, I find myself casually tapping the logs with the poker, nonchalantly mentioning, "I suppose you didn’t happen to stay at Vossvangen? I left a malacca cane there once. Quite a nice one too." This has led my friends to believe I have left a piece of myself in nearly every European town, a legacy bestowed by my beloved stick.

My most recent acquisition is an ebony stick, topped with ivory. Even if I were to spend another fortnight abroad, I couldn't imagine taking this cane with me. It simply doesn’t belong in the countryside; its essence is firmly rooted in Piccadilly. Perhaps it could survive a journey to Paris, provided it could withstand the ocean voyage. However, I cannot envision it crossing the Channel; I feel inadequate in a cap by its side. If I could step onto the boat wearing a silk hat and then retreat below decks, that might be different, but I often feel unwell below, which would not suit its elegance. Now, it languishes in the corner of my room, yearning for an evening at the opera. I used to look down on men who brought canes to Covent Garden, yet I now understand their plight. An ebony stick with an ivory top demands a certain level of respect. I’m already contemplating a silk-lined cape, and I’ve decided that my gloves must feature black stitching.

This latest stick, which I received just this morning, has stirred my thoughts about replacing the ordinary one I lost during an Easter train journey. That notion was rather foolish. I require a stick of more rugged stock, one that isn’t afraid to accompany me while I sport a soft hat. It should be the type of stick I can casually drop or even kick, one that can be wielded to swat away dandelions, and one that, if left behind in a train, won’t leave me feeling guilty enough to report it to Scotland Yard. In essence, I seek a companionable stick for day trips—a true country stick.

The quest for the ideal country stick is a challenging one. It must possess sufficient thickness to endure rough usage, yet still be slender enough to produce a pleasing whistle as it slices through the air. Its handle should be curved, allowing me to pull down the branches bearing blossoms I desire or retrieve the lunch basket that I value even more. Yet, it must also be straight enough to function as a makeshift golf club. It should lack embellishments to avoid being ostentatious, yet have a band that allows for a score count when I throw stones at it. You begin to realize how elusive the perfect stick truly is.

I suppose we must all concede certain features that our personal sticks may do without. As for me, I am adamant—my stick must serve well for hitting and be equally adept at taking a hit. We can all agree that a stick is something to grasp while walking. However, there are moments when we take a seat, particularly if we find ourselves at the beach; at such times, our stick must stand tall in the sand while we take aim from a distance and hurl stones at it. Regardless of the sea’s beauty, it can only be fully appreciated in this manner. Scenery should be savored slowly; we must absorb it gradually. As we contemplate whether a shot at the band scores a two or merely a one just below, with our arms engaged in the act of throwing, we become ideally open to the beauty surrounding us.

Moreover, for my country stick, it is essential that I can grasp it by the wrong end and still strike a ball. Thus, it should have no ferrule, and the handle must be heavy and straight. This is how golf came to be; its originator wandered the fields after a picnic lunch, playfully batting the cork from his bottle. Initially, he might have taken seventy-nine swings from one field gate to the oak tree in the next; later, it became fifty-four. Then, in a moment of clarity, he recognized the game. We cannot claim he lacked a love for Nature. The urge to knock a ball around, to engage in whimsical games with a stick, often arises in a man when he is filled with joy; one might reasonably attribute this happiness to the beauty of streams, hedges, and sunlight filtering through the trees. So let my stick boast a heavy, straight handle and be ferrule-free. And rest assured, I will have an old golf ball tucked in my pocket.

In the city of London, we tend to be less discerning. Primarily, we require a stick for leaning on while conversing with an unexpectedly encountered acquaintance. Following the initial greeting, and upon realizing we have little else of significance to share, the presence of our stick alleviates the awkwardness. It provides both moral and physical support, much like a cigarette offers in a drawing-room setting. For this purpose, size and shape are of little consequence. However, it is crucial that it isn’t too slippery, or in our nervousness, we might drop it entirely. My ebony stick with its polished ivory top—

Yet, I’ve already concluded that this ebony stick feels out of place with my everyday hat. It now stands in its corner, awaiting the opera season, while I am left to find another stick for more rugged endeavors.
