A friend of mine, someone who knows me well enough to sense my hidden thoughts, recently posed an intriguing question: "Have you ever encountered 'Alpha of the Plough?'"

"I’ve never met him," I replied without hesitation, feeling quite unabashed. We both chuckled at the exchange.

"Really? Never at all?" he pressed.

"Not once," I confirmed. "And I can assure you, I never will."

"Not even in the mirror?" he quipped.

"That’s not really 'Alpha of the Plough,'" I countered. "That’s merely a facsimile. It could be a convincing imitation, but it’s not the actual person. I can perceive fragments of him—his hands, feet, and arms. By closing one eye, I can deduce the outline of his nose. If I push out my upper lip, I might notice he has a moustache. However, the entirety of his face remains concealed from me. I cannot describe even with the aid of the imitation what impression he leaves on those who observe him. Now," I paused to assess my friend thoughtfully, "I can gauge what you are like. I can take you in at a glance, and you can do the same with me. Yet, the one person we cannot perceive in entirety is the very individual we most desire to understand."

"That's a bit of a blessing," he remarked.

I’m not entirely convinced he was correct. In this intricate world, there are compelling arguments on both sides. It might be fortunate for some of us to remain perpetual strangers to ourselves, not just in our physical forms. We lack even the awareness of our own voices. Mr. Pemberton-Billing has never experienced the sound of his own deep voice in the House of Commons, while Lord Charles Beresford is oblivious to how a foghorn sounds when it articulates. You and I cannot fathom the impression our demeanor leaves on others. If we did, many of us would likely be devastated. The truth might be so shocking that we would wish we had never been born at all.

Consider, for instance, the distinguished politician Mr. Sutherland Bangs, M.P., encountering himself at a dinner party one evening. Mr. Sutherland Bangs harbors a pleasant self-image, envisioning himself as a charming, eloquent fellow, skilled in conversation, exuding a carefree nature, with a fashionable appearance and a refined accent. However, seated next to his own self at dinner, he would realize that he is, in fact, a pretentious bore, that his conversation is filled with banalities, his breezy demeanor grating, and his elegant accent nothing more than an annoying affectation. He would vow never to want to see that version of himself again. Returning home, he would feel as humbled and degraded as Mr. Tom Lofty in The Good-Natured Man when his true nature was revealed. "You ought to have your head stuck in a pillory," Mr. Croaker admonished Mr. Lofty. "Stick it wherever you like," Mr. Lofty replied, "for, by the Lord, it makes a poor showing wherever it is." Mr. Sutherland Bangs would certainly resonate with that sentiment.

Yet, while encountering our true selves might bring some of us profound surprise and even discomfort, it would also serve many of us a beneficial purpose. Burns articulated this sentiment beautifully in his well-known lines:

"O wad some pow'r the giftie gie us  
To see oursels as others see us:  
It wad frae monie a blunder free us  
An' foolish notion."

We would each uncover truths that could work to our advantage alongside our dismay. You, for instance, might discover that the argumentative prowess you take pride in is merely perceived by me as irritating obstinacy, while I might learn that the clever wit I believe I exhibit causes you fatigue. Jones might find his eyeglass falling from his eye, realizing it serves only to make him look foolish. Brown would confront the unattractiveness of his cant, and Robinson would regret his bullying nature and prickly demeanor. Gaining this objective perspective on ourselves would undoubtedly be beneficial.

However, this view isn’t necessarily the definitive or complete perspective. Recall Sherlock Holmes’ clever notion about John and Thomas. When they converse and struggle to connect, Holmes observes that it’s hardly surprising, considering that six identities are involved in the dialogue. "Six!" you might exclaim, surprised. Indeed, there are six: John’s ideal self—how he perceives himself; Thomas’s ideal view of John—how he sees John; and the real John, known solely to his Creator. The same applies to Thomas, who also embodies three different versions. The ideal self that John envisions is entirely dissimilar to how Thomas perceives him, and neither of these aligns with the authentic John, leading to a miscommunication between John and Thomas—that is, between you and me.

If I (John) could see myself through your (Thomas’s) eyes, observing my appearance, mannerisms, and behaviors, it would serve as a crucial corrective. It would cultivate the faculty of self-criticism that most of us lack. This ability is simply the skill to observe ourselves from an outsider’s perspective, devoid of bias and interest. Few possess this capability, except perhaps in fleeting moments of clarity. We struggle to apply it even to our creations. When you paint, write, or craft a joke, you can be certain that you will likely misjudge its quality. You perceive it subjectively as part of your identity, which means you don’t truly see it. If you set it aside for a year and then encounter it unexpectedly, much like a stranger would, you may finally grasp why Thomas appeared unfazed. It wasn’t due to jealousy or hostility, as you once assumed; rather, he perceived it clearly while you could not.

Even the most notable figures can fall prey to this blindness regarding their own work. This phenomenon is evident in the case of Wordsworth. He stands among the three greatest poets this country has ever produced and was also a discerning critic of poetry. Yet, he authored more pedestrian prose than any of his contemporaries. He seemed oblivious to when his writing reached sublime heights and when it descended into mere drivel. This lack of awareness stemmed from his inability to step outside the self-induced trance of his own perception.

I have occasionally felt frustrated by the phrase, "What do they know of England, who only England know?" It often serves as a rallying cry for a superficial brand of Imperialism. Yet, I also recognized a kernel of truth in it during a solitary moment in the Alps, surrounded by a vast expanse of peaks and glaciers. As I awaited my guide’s return, who had ventured ahead to scout the terrain, I found myself gazing, much like Richard, "towards England." At that moment, I felt as though I was witnessing it through an imaginative lens, comprehensively and clearly, as I had never done in all my years spent within its borders. I beheld its lush pastures and moorlands, its mountains and lakes, its cities and its populace, along with its grandeur and its squalor, as if I were seeing it projected beyond the horizon. I perceived it with newfound eyes and a refreshed mindset, understanding it in a way I never had before, certainly loving it more deeply than I ever thought possible. I discovered that in leaving England, I was able to truly see it.

This is precisely what we need to do with ourselves from time to time. We require a journey away from our self-centered perspectives to view ourselves through untainted eyes and impartial judgment.
