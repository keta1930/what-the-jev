The other day, I set off from Keswick, a rucksack slung over my shoulders, a Baddeley guide tucked into my pocket, and a companion accompanying me. Walking with a partner adds to the experience; as Sterne once expressed, "Give me a companion by the way," even if it’s just to notice how shadows stretch as the sun dips lower. That’s about all the conversation one needs. Engaging in chatter at the outset is fine, but as the journey progresses and you become absorbed in the task at hand, silence becomes more appealing. Often, you may find yourselves trailing one another, saving your discussions for the inn later and the comfort of a post-dinner pipe. A shared laugh, a spontaneous song, or a brief review of the map is more than sufficient for the trail.

At the head of Derwentwater, we boarded a boat and glided across the lake to the small bay nestled at the base of Catbells. We disembarked, adjusted our packs, and embarked on our journey over the mountains and through the passes, relishing a week immersed in the profound solitude that this region has to offer. We didn’t adhere to a strict itinerary. While I enjoy creating plans for hiking expeditions, I find even greater joy in deviating from them. The freedom that walking imparts is one of its greatest pleasures. You are not bound by schedules, nor confined to a specific route, nor beholden to anyone. If a particular path captures your interest, you can follow it; if you prefer a different pass, it’s yours to choose; and if the allure of the mountain peaks calls to you, then over Great Gable and Scafell, Robinson and Helvellyn you shall traverse. Every shortcut beckons, every trail offers a chance for adventure. The stream cascading down the mountainside becomes your cup of wine. Kneeling on the stones, you lean down to drink from it, savoring the refreshment that only the mountains can provide. Then, with a renewed spirit, you continue on your way, singing:

Bed in the bush with the stars to see. Bread I dip in the river— There's the life for a man like me. There's the life for ever.

What other freedom compares? You have severed your ties with the world, distancing yourself from telegraphs, newspapers, and the chaos of the life you’ve left behind. You stand alone among the solitary hills, beneath an expansive sky, surrounded by the elemental forces that have existed since time immemorial and will endure long after the tumult of humanity fades away. The sounds of life—the curlew’s whistle, the bleating of sheep—only deepen the sense of ancient isolation. These echoes have resonated off the crags for millennia; their harmonies, alongside the rush of winds and waters, will continue to reverberate for countless more years. It feels like you have transcended time, entering a realm where a thousand years is but a fleeting moment. Here, there’s no calendar to dictate your existence. The buzzard, startled from its perch in a gully, circles above with wings spread wide, tracing a lineage as old as the mountains themselves. The breathtaking view of the Langdale peaks, which greets you as you reach the summit of Esk Hause, is the same sight that greeted the first intrepid soul who ventured into these wild mountain fastnesses.

As the sun tilts toward the horizon, reality sets in. You are reminded that, while surrounded by eternal wonders, you are still very much mortal, feeling the fatigue in your feet and realizing the need for rest and refuge for the night. Where shall we go? The valleys beckon from all directions. We could head toward the expansive Newlands Vale, the cheerful Borrowdale, the serene Ennerdale, or—yes, tonight we’ll dine at Wastdale, at the cheerful old inn once run by Auld Will Ritson, a sanctuary for cragsmen. It’s a place where, on New Year’s Eve, climbers gather to share stories of their mountain exploits, discussing handholds and footholds, joining in the lively chorus of “The rope, the rope,” led by the landlord who heartily sings:

I'm not a climber, not a climber, Not a climber now, My weight is going fourteen stone— I'm not a climber now.

Tonight, we won’t see Gaspard—the spirited and fearless guide from the Dauphiné, a favorite among those who frequent the lonely inn at Wastdale. He’s off fighting a more formidable enemy than the rocky heights of Great Gable and Scafell. However, Old Joe, the shepherd, will be there—Old Joe, who has never traveled by train or set foot in a town, renowned for his ability to pull the ugliest faces in all of Cumberland. He reserves them for moments of good cheer and for his friends in the back parlor. Perhaps tonight, we will witness his eyes rolling as he passionately belts out the chorus of “D’ye ken John Peel?” Yes, Wastdale will be our stop for the night. Thus, we will traverse Black Sail and descend the rugged mountainside to the inn, its whitewashed walls greeting us from the encroaching shadows of the valley.

What about tomorrow? Well, tomorrow shall mirror today. We will rise early, shouldering our rucksacks at dawn to make the most of our time in the mountains, as the first principle of any journey is to start early. Seize the day, and then you can dawdle to your heart’s content. If the weather is warm, you may take a refreshing dip in the frigid waters of the tarns that lie open to the sky in the hill hollows—tarns with names that evoke beauty and waters of such clarity that even Killarney would envy them. By nightfall, we may descend through the clouds along the wild path of Rosset Ghyll and find rest and nourishment at the hotel near Dungeon Ghyll, or, if we manage the day well, we could continue on toward Coniston via Blea Tarn and Yewdale, or through Easedale Tarn to Grasmere, eventually reaching the Swan at the foot of Dunmail Raise. We must stop at the Swan. Was it not the Swan from which Wordsworth’s “Waggoner” so triumphantly departed? Was it not the Swan where Sir Walter Scott enjoyed a pint during his visits with Wordsworth at Rydal Water? And isn’t there, behind the Swan, a fold in the hills where Wordsworth’s “Michael” endeavored to build his sheepfold? Yes, we will certainly stay at the Swan, come what may.

And so the delightful days pass, some drizzly, some sunny, others a blend of both, but each filled with joy. We lose track of the days of the week, hear no news beyond the shifting weather and mountain trails, and encounter few souls apart from fellow wanderers like ourselves—those with ropes over their shoulders if they’re climbers, or rucksacks on their backs if they’re tourists—each with no destination in mind except a distant valley inn where we can restore our energies, and where the promise of tomorrow’s adventures will be enticing.

I intended to extol the virtues of walking, yet I find I’ve been singing praises of Lakeland instead. However, it seems these two celebrations are inextricably intertwined. For if I haven’t conveyed that the most exquisite views this land has to offer can be best appreciated on foot, then I have indeed written in vain. Those who traverse Lakeland on horseback or in a vehicle miss out on its hidden wonders and the enchantment it holds.
