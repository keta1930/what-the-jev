Recently, I experienced a moment of sheer surprise when I managed to catch a train heading towards the Eastern Counties. I barely made it, and while I hurried alongside the train, I became aware of an unusually high number of carriages labeled "Engaged." The notices were visible on five, six, seven, or even eight carriages, with large, imposing men gazing out from the windows, their expressions reflecting a peculiar pride. Their demeanor seemed especially impenetrable, and their faces wore an unusual calmness. It was not Derby Day, as the train was headed in the opposite direction and it was not the appropriate time. It couldn't be the King or the French President either. While such prominent figures typically enjoy privacy during their travels, they also attract crowds and police presence when they board a train. Yet, here there was neither a gathering of onlookers nor any ceremonial security.

Who were these formidable individuals occupying a substantial portion of the train, outnumbering a gathering of revelers yet exhibiting a peculiar delicacy that seemed more fitting for the royal entourage? Was it conceivable that rather than the Royal Family visiting the Tsar, the Tsar was actually visiting us? Or perhaps there was a breakfast gathering of the House of Lords? My curiosity deepened as the train slowed at a station en route to Cambridge. The large, inscrutable men disembarked, followed by their distinguished companions, each of whom appeared to be dressed uniformly, with neatly cropped hair and a peculiar chain linking them together.

Across the carriage, I made eye contact with the only other passenger. He was a small, weary-looking man, who I later learned hailed from Cambridge. Judging by his appearance, he seemed to be a local tradesman, perhaps a journeyman tailor or a clock mender. In an effort to strike up a conversation, I expressed my curiosity about where the convicts might be headed. His mouth twitched in a wry smile, hinting at the irony that often accompanies the plight of the common man, and he replied, “I don’t suppose they’re goin’ on an ’oliday at the seaside with little spades and pails.” I found his humor delightful and continued to play along, suggesting that maybe academics were being escorted to Cambridge in similar chains. As someone familiar with the dons, he found this notion amusing. However, our laughter soon gave way to a somber silence, and I noticed a profound sadness in his bleak, gray eyes, which seemed emptier than the vast ocean. I understood his thoughts mirrored my own; despite our attempts at humor, we both recognized the underlying seriousness of our reality. Eventually, he broke the silence with, “Well, I s’pose we ’ave to do it.” In his initial jest, his silence, and his concluding remark lay the essence of three fundamental truths about English democracy: a deep sense of humor, a poignant sense of pathos, and a pervasive sense of helplessness.

It is worth emphasizing that genuine democracy strives to draw out those who are shy, similar to a gracious hostess at a gathering. For the practical operations of a political entity, as well as the social dynamics of a tea party, it is clear that those who humble themselves will find elevation. Conversely, those who seek to elevate themselves must be brought low, ideally without resorting to physical force. People often perceive democracy as rough and unruly, which is a misconception rooted in historical ignorance. It is aristocracy that tends to be coarse and disorderly, as it appeals to those who possess self-assurance. Democracy, on the other hand, seeks to engage those who may hesitate to govern themselves; according to Christian principles, the very individuals who ought to rule are precisely those who lack the audacity to take charge. My encounter with the man on the train serves as a compelling illustration of this principle. The discussions surrounding crime and punishment typically highlight two rare and extreme perspectives.

On one hand, we have the stark sentimentalist who suggests that no issues exist, advocating that physical kindness could resolve everything, as if all that was required was to offer a pat to figures like Nero or Ivan the Terrible. This naive belief in the redemptive power of comfort is not sentimental; rather, it is a form of snobbery, as it implies that the privileged classes should inherently be virtuous, which is an absurd notion. On the other hand, we find the even weaker type of sentimentalist who advocates violent retribution, proclaiming, “Flog the brutes!” or sharing crude fantasies about what they would do, assuming the offender was incapacitated. While this perspective is certainly more feeble, both types lack balance and strength. In modern discussions, one rarely encounters either of these extremes on a train. The people you meet in such settings often resemble the man I spoke with: emotionally grounded yet intellectually conflicted. Rather than reveling in the gruesome possibilities of punishment, they lament the fact that any action must be taken. "I s’pose we ’ave to do it." This conveys the mindset of a rational individual, and a sane person can be defined simply: they can harbor tragedy in their heart while keeping comedy in their mind.

The challenge in addressing the issue of how to treat criminals with decency lies in the detachment exhibited by both sides of the argument. Those who condemn wrongdoing often approach the subject with as much coldness as those who orchestrate it. Humanitarianism can sometimes be as harsh as inhumanity itself.

To illustrate, I find the practice of flogging in modern prisons to be a vile form of torture. Despite the scientific pretenses surrounding it—photographs, medical supervision—it reduces punishment to a base level, akin to the most barbaric practices. The cat-o'-nine-tails is merely a modern version of the rack, stripped of any intellectual justification. When I express this belief, I often encounter phrases in humanitarian literature that describe the lash as "a relic of barbarism." This statement is feeble and unhelpful when challenging such practices. It’s akin to a person walking naked down the street, and our response is to critique their outdated fashion choices. There’s nothing inherently wrong with being a remnant of barbarism; after all, humanity itself is a remnant of barbarism, and civilization is, too.

However, torture cannot simply be dismissed as a relic of barbarism. In truth, it is a remnant of sin. Historically, torture has thrived alongside the most sophisticated cultures, reaching its peak in artistic expressions during the late Roman Empire, the ornate sixteenth century, the centralized French monarchy before the Revolution, and continues to exist in refined forms within certain modern societies. This is a sobering realization we must confront. As we become more educated and refined, we must recognize that we are not inevitably moving away from the specter of torture. In fact, we may be edging closer to it. It is crucial to be aware of our direction if we are to evade the immense hidden cruelty that has accompanied every civilization throughout history.

The train glides faster through the bright fields of England. The prisoners have been taken away, and their fate remains unknown to me.
