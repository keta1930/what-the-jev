Let’s discuss—well, anything that comes to mind. Take goldfish, for example.

Goldfish can represent a sense of tranquil nostalgia or the futility often associated with mid-Victorian life, depending on their surroundings. When they are found outdoors, in their natural habitat from which civilization has extracted them, they might embody bravery, loyalty, or steadfastness; I can’t quite say. I can only describe how I see them now, commonly in the garden or within the confines of a drawing-room. In their lily-draped pond, nestled deep within the old tiled terrace where the blackbird sings its morning melody, they evoke memories of sundials, lavender, and other cherished relics of the past. Conversely, in their inexpensive glass bowl perched on a three-legged table, beneath the silent watch of a cloth-covered canary, they conjure images of antimacassars, horsehair sofas, and an overall sense of gloom. It seems rather unfair that the goldfish has so little say in their circumstances. They appear charming in the pond, yet I suspect it was not a quest for beauty that led them there. Instead, it’s likely the human desire to cast objects into the water that drew them in. Anyone who has sat before the sea, captivated by its beauty, cannot resist the urge to throw stones into it; the physical satisfaction of the act combined with the visual delight of the splash leads to a unique sense of joy. Similarly, near the pond, these same impulses arise, and since ants’ eggs don’t create splashes and seem messy on the surface, a shimmering gold or silver fish serves to enhance the experience.

You may not have thought about it in this way while feeding the goldfish, but surely you’ve pondered why they favor ants’ eggs above all other foods. Ants’ eggs are not the kind of food one would instinctively choose; they must surely be an acquired taste. This raises the question of how goldfish came to develop such a preference.

I speculate—though I confess my ignorance in these matters—that there was once a time when goldfish enjoyed an unrestrained existence. They swam in the ocean or rivers, battling for survival while Nature, as is her way, provided them with suitable nourishment. In my own explorations, I’ve encountered numerous ant hills, but never while I was swimming. I’ve journeyed through seas, rivers, pools, and lakes, yet Nature has never presented me with ants’ eggs. Surely, if such delicacies were abundant in their environment, I would have noticed them. I imagine that a goldfish could gradually develop a taste for them over generations, but only if they were consistently available, drifting downstream wherever he swam.

However, since it appears that the goldfish has indeed acquired this taste, it suggests that it has been imposed upon him through captivity. I theorize that the ancestral wild goldfish was a more formidable creature than we typically assume. If given the right diet, he would likely have resisted confinement to a terrace pool. Domestic life would not suit him; he would have dragged in a shrieking child leaning over to feed him. Over time, as various experiments were conducted, ants’ eggs were introduced as a means to keep him slim (consider how insipid a diet that is), effectively suppressing his spirit. Just as a person can develop a fondness for something unappealing, like ammoniated quinine, when faced with enough adversity, the goldfish has adapted in captivity to embrace the once-despised omelette.

Now, let’s examine the situation of the goldfish residing within the household. While his diet remains unchanged, his environment is vastly different. When placed on a table in the center of a room, a single flick of his tail allows him to traverse the entire drawing-room. To an outsider, this space might seem ordinary, but for him, the view through the curved glass must be nothing short of mesmerizing. The outdoor goldfish should not take pride in his freedom; in his limited realm of water-lily roots, what does he truly understand about the rich vistas his indoor counterpart enjoys as he glides from china dog to ottoman and then from ottoman to Henry’s father? Ah, what a life! It’s possible that over the years, he may become accustomed to it, perhaps even bored, which is why I often advocate giving him glimpses of the dining room or bedrooms on Wednesdays and Saturdays. Nevertheless, his inaugural day in the bowl must feel like the grand unveiling of an unimaginable paradise.

Furthermore, consider the thrilling life he leads. At any moment, a cat might leap up and snatch him away, a child might accidentally topple him, or adults might neglect to feed him or refresh his water. The temptation to lift him out and give him a rub must be irresistible to those outside his bowl. All these perils are avoided by the goldfish in the pond; he enjoys a sheltered yet uneventful existence. When it’s his time to pass away, he does so unnoticed and unmissed, while his indoor counterpart may face tears and a solemn farewell.

Upon reflection, I realize I was mistaken in labeling the indoor goldfish as an emblem of mid-Victorian futility. A piece like this should impart lessons to both the writer and the readers. I now understand that he represents enterprise and resilience, restlessness and the essence of Post-Impressionism. He is not a relic of the mid-Victorian era, but rather a creature of the Fifth Georgian period.

That encapsulates all I wish to convey regarding goldfish.
