Many years ago, I experienced an uncomfortable incident that has stayed with me. My house-master caught me reading in bed well past the designated hour of midnight. My roommate, Smith minor, who shared the dim light of a candle with me, was also lost in a book. What irked me most about the situation was not merely the reprimand but the fact that Smith minor was engrossed in Alton Locke while I was caught with Marooned Among Cannibals. If only our house-master had entered the room the previous night, he would have seen me reading Alton Locke instead. For a fleeting moment, I considered explaining this to him, but after some thought, I decided against it. There was a risk he might misinterpret my intentions.

Intellectual snobbery is something to which many of us can relate. While a detective novel may have provided us with great enjoyment, we often hesitate to admit it publicly. We prefer to reference a work like The Egoist, even if our familiarity with it is just superficial. We strive to create the illusion of being well-read companions with such literature, hoping to impress others enough that they might introduce us to even loftier works. Regardless of their merit, we would rather be seen with a copy of Marcus Aurelius than with one by Marie Corelli. I once knew someone who always carried a Russian novel in its original language, not because he could read Russian, but due to the possibility that one day his "pockets would be exposed" in the public arena. He believed that one could never be too prepared, yet the only incident that ever occurred was him being stranded for twelve hours at a remote train station in the Highlands. Following that experience, he concluded that Russian literature was overrated.

I would like to think that I have outgrown such tendencies, but I find myself questioning whether that is genuinely the case. Recently, I found myself in the company of people whom I felt embarrassed to be with; to feel shame about one’s companions is an indication of snobbery. At that time, I was attempting to entertain myself—and perhaps others—by crafting a burlesque story styled as if it were a collaboration between Sir Hall Caine and Mrs. Florence Barclay. To accomplish this, I needed to delve into the works of these two well-known authors. For several weekends, I could be seen traveling to and from the countryside with their books tucked under my arm. Balancing one book is relatively simple; however, managing two is quite a different challenge. There were numerous occasions when one of those books would slip from my grasp while I waited for a train. I can hardly recall a station along the southern railways where I didn’t inadvertently drop a Caine or a Barclay on a Saturday. The books would often be returned to me by fellow passengers—polite individuals who, despite their courteous demeanor, wore a sympathetic smile upon recognizing the author’s name. “Thank you very much,” I would stammer, feeling the heat rise to my cheeks, perhaps concocting a tale about a sick friend who needed the books or claiming I required them as makeshift paperweights. But they never believed my excuses. They understood that they would have likely said something similar in my position.

It is all too easy to assume that others share our flaws. Perhaps even Jack the Ripper justified his actions by claiming they were part of human nature; he might have penned an essay like this one, pondering the allure of violence. I realize that I may unjustly accuse you of harboring similar snobbish tendencies. Yet, I cannot help but wish to put you on the stand. If, during a discussion about heredity—a topic you had studied extensively—I mistakenly assumed you were familiar with Ommany’s Approximations, would you feel compelled to correct me? Or would you allow me to continue, under the pretense that you were well-acquainted with it? If I pressed you directly, would you sheepishly admit that while you hadn’t actually read it, you had certainly heard of it and perhaps even skimmed some excerpts? I suspect I could coax you into this admission, perhaps even leading you to declare that you had ordered it from the library, before I revealed the unfortunate truth that Ommany’s Approximations was an invention of my imagination.

It is ridiculous that we—yes, I include you in this observation—should act in such a manner. There is no book for which we need to feel shame, whether we have read it or not. In the spirit of honesty, I must confess that I have read only three of Sir Walter Scott’s novels and have started but never completed two of Henry James’s. To further complicate my self-image, I can say that I manage quite well in discussions within Scottish and Jacobean circles with just those five books. When the topic arises concerning which of Scott’s works is his finest, I can easily suggest one of my three selections, presenting myself as if I have made an informed choice among a vast array of options. If one of my chosen novels happens to be the recognized masterpiece, then all is well. But if it isn’t, I still gain a reputation for having made a clever choice in rejecting the obvious. My standing with Henry James is not quite as solid; however, I can at least take comfort in believing that the two novels I abandoned cannot possibly represent his best work. With a bit of finesse, I can passionately defend this position against an imaginary critic who might argue otherwise.

There are times when I feel as though I have delved into both their complete works, as well as Ommany’s Approximations and numerous other titles that you might assume I’m familiar with. By projecting an image of being well-versed in these literary treasures, I’ve merely perpetuated the impression that has formed in my mind over time. I don’t take advantage of this façade; if it turns out that we have both been deceived in our assumptions, I will be just as taken aback and indignant as anyone else involved.
