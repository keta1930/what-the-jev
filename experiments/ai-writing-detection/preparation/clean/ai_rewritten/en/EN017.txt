For a long time, I have harbored a desire to become a grocer. Just imagine being surrounded by an array of delightful items—sardines, bottled raspberries, sweet biscuits topped with sugar, preserved ginger, hams, and brawn neatly encased in glass. These are the very things that enrich life! Picture strolling up a ladder in search of nutmeg one moment and diving beneath a counter in pursuit of cinnamon the next. Serving little girls a ha'porth of pear drops and dignified patrons like you and me a pint of cherry gin—doesn’t this embody the essence of a noble profession? One day, I plan to open my own grocery store, and you’ll find me in the evenings donning an apron behind the counter. Keep an eye out for the currants displayed in the window when you drop by; I have a creative vision for their arrangement. But, I implore you, resist the temptation to buy any. We grocers exhibit currants purely for aesthetics, relishing the chance to run our fingers through them when business is slow. I have an excellent selection of shortbreads, madam, if I can locate the box, but alas, no currants available this evening, I must insist.

Indeed, being a grocer seems to promise a fulfilling life; however, I realize that it doesn’t offer a true sense of engaging with the world. A grocer, despite selling both fragrant soaps and pilchards, would risk becoming rather insular. Our connection to the outside world is limited, often filtered through the medium of potted lobster, which tells us little about the people we serve. Selling potted lobster does not reveal the essence of an individual. To a grocer, all customers appear the same, as long as their money is valid. Reflecting on this, I now see that my choice to pursue a career as a grocer was perhaps premature. That path may be more suited for one’s later years. In youth, when personal connections matter more than material goods, there is truly only one profession worth pursuing—the role of a bookstall clerk.

Working behind a bookstall is, without a doubt, an avenue to genuinely experience life. This realization struck me rather suddenly last Monday as I stood before a station bookstall, pondering who might buy the tie-clips displayed. The answer came to me as I boarded my train—ask the person behind the bookstall; he would certainly know. Indeed, he would be familiar with all who purchase his newspapers, books, and pamphlets, and understanding this clientele provides insight into the world around us. While one cannot judge a man solely by the lobster he consumes, one can glean much about him through the literature he selects.

For instance, I once shared a train carriage on an eastern route with a middle-aged woman. The moment we departed from Liverpool Street, she produced a bag of shrimps, gripping each one firmly by the head and tail before consuming it. Once she had finished, she discarded the shells out of the window, wiped her hands, and settled in with her newspaper. What was she reading? You may not believe it, but it was The Morning Post. Isn’t that revealing? The shrimps alone wouldn’t tell you much about her, and neither would the choice of newspaper, but together they paint a vivid picture. Just imagine the delight of the bookstall clerk as she approached, bag of shrimps in hand, and requested The Morning Post.

The bookstall clerk’s day can never be monotonous. I envision him mentally categorizing the appropriate paper for each customer. This man will surely ask for Golfing—wrong! He wants Cage Birds; that gentleman over there seeks The Motor—ah, The Auto-Car will suffice. Over time, he would begin to identify various types of customers, learning to differentiate between those who favor The Dancing Times and those who prefer The Vote, The Era, or The Athenaeum. Delightful surprises must occasionally knock at his door, like the rare day when a man in a checkered waistcoat makes the unexpected purchase of both Homer’s Penny Stories and The Spectator. On such occasions, his faith in humanity might waver until he reassures himself that the man is likely an escaped criminal in disguise, perhaps overacting the part. In that moment, he would pass on The Winning Post and The Animals’ Friend to the pursuing detective with a sense of solemn reverence. What a fascinating existence!

Beyond merely selling papers, the clerk has a deeper understanding of his customers. He knows who is likely to pick up those little sixpenny books filled with humorous tales—a riddle that often eludes the rest of us. By now, he can easily identify the kind of man who needs a few good jokes to share during his weekend at old Robinson’s. Our bookstall clerk takes the initiative; as soon as he spots this gentleman approaching, he swiftly retrieves the book, dusts it off, and presents it to him. He has also developed an instinct for recognizing the hapless individuals who frequently misplace their indiarubber umbrella rings. From halfway across the station, he can spot them and prepares a new card with a knowing smile. (“Or we would let you have seven for sixpence, sir.”) Even when faced with those more elusive characters—those who could be in the market for either a fountain pen with case or the Life and Letters of Major-General Clement Bulger, C.B. at a discount—our clerk is always ready. If his first guess misses the mark, he swiftly recovers with a second try. “Bulger, sir. One of our greatest soldiers.”

Last Monday, as I contemplated these thoughts, I made a definitive decision to abandon my aspirations of becoming a grocer. While browsing through the bookstall, I stumbled upon a small tome priced at sixpence in cloth and a shilling in leather, titled Proverbs and Maxims. It contained a wealth of wisdom—thousands of insights that have guided humanity since time immemorial, from “What ho, she bumps!” to “Ich dien,” and beyond. The notion struck me that I could derive an engaging article from it, and so I purchased the book. Unfortunately, I left it on the train before I had the chance to delve into its contents. I plan to visit the bookstall again next Monday to acquire another copy. That should work out just fine; you won’t miss it.

Yet, I find myself pondering how the bookstall clerk will perceive me. A man who repeatedly buys Proverbs and Maxims—what a curious character I must appear to him. Well, as I said earlier, they truly see life.
