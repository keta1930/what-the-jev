Mr. George Robey, C.B.E., often referred to as the Prime Minister of Mirth, tends to embody a more destructive philosophical outlook rather than one that offers reassurance in his songs. His hallmark on stage is a cheerful cynicism that emerges from a life untroubled by financial woes. However, there was a moment when he adopted the persona of a landlady, singing a tune that offered solace to those who find themselves in less fortunate circumstances: "It's a blessing that you never miss the things you've never had." This sentiment and Tennyson’s renowned assertion, "'Tis better to have loved and lost than never to have loved at all," present an interesting point of discussion. While they are not in opposition to each other, reflecting on my unfortunate friend, the Golden Eagle, I find myself leaning entirely towards Mr. Robey’s optimistic view. It is indeed a blessing to be oblivious to what we have never possessed, and conversely, it is a true misfortune to lose something cherished and struggle to forget it or refrain from longing for it. In essence, for those who are unwilling witnesses to such tragedies, it is preferable to have never known such treasures.

Let us move beyond the introduction.

It is undoubtedly challenging to be perceived as powerful while lacking the means to assist a trusting individual who relies on one's support. This is a general observation, yet it was brought to mind by a recent comment about Baedeker. "Will those of us who have retained our Baedekers have the courage to carry them?" someone posed, and my thoughts immediately drifted to an Italian city and the proprietor of the Aquila d'Oro.

During my brief stay at his establishment, I received an unprecedented level of attention from the host. Indeed, the dedication I received from him and his entire staff was remarkable. The Golden Eagle, however, went beyond mere hospitality; he actively sought me out. Every time I left or entered the hotel, he was there, a large, soft Italian man usually dressed in his shirt sleeves and wearing the loose slippers that often discomfort British travelers. An astute observer recently remarked to me, "No foreigner ever has a good dog," and it might be equally said that no Latin individual is ever properly shod. Yet, the Golden Eagle was not merely careless; his role demanded a certain level of decorum, and he was simply a vigilant proprietor, perpetually concerned with his business affairs.

Though other guests were present, enjoying superior accommodations and finer wines, it was I who captured his focus. I was the one he awaited, upon whom his large, mournful eyes rested with unending hope. The Golden Eagle bore a grievance, and I, having been given a writing desk in my room, continuously equipped with a notebook and a collector of photographs, was engaged in studying the city—likely for the purpose of a book. I was in a position to alleviate his grievance, potentially restoring him to a state of prosperity and tranquility.

What, then, was this grievance? The sorrow etched into his expansive, pale face was not merely temperament or a reflection of his nationality; Italian features often convey disillusionment and fatalism more frequently than joyfulness. The sadness in his broad shoulders stemmed from the same source. Baedeker, after years of commendation for the Aquila d'Oro, had suddenly stripped his establishment of its asterisk in the latest edition. The Golden Eagle had lost his star. In this case, it was not better for either him or me that he had lost something he cherished. Truly, it would have been preferable for both of us had he never possessed that star at all.

He remained baffled as to why it had been taken from him. He and his wife had put forth their utmost effort; patrons were satisfied and returned time and again, yet the star had vanished. Was his hotel not clean? The linens were soft, and the service was commendable. He personally supervised everything, as did his wife. They left no stone unturned in their quest to ensure guest comfort. While foolish innkeepers might be penny-pinchers, he was not. He understood that when it came to dining, frugality was unwise. Were not the meals generous and varied? Could I name a more plentiful breakfast at 4 lire or a superior lunch at 5? Were they not served promptly? Was not his wine both sound and reasonably priced?

Yet, inexplicably, four years ago, the star had disappeared from his hotel. It was outrageous, an affront. Four years prior—without warning and for no discernible reason.

Upon viewing the new edition of Baedeker left behind by a visitor, he could scarcely believe his eyes. He summoned his wife and the entire household; they were equally incredulous. It felt like a thunderbolt had struck, an earthquake of misfortune. After all their hard work, their sincere desire to please, and the regular customers who returned contented—was that not the true measure of success? Clearly, the guidebook had erred, committing a grave injustice.

What did he theorize had transpired? His only assumption was that an undercover agent of Herr Baedeker had experienced some unfortunate incident during their stay—perhaps an unavoidable mishap in the kitchen that had offended the guest. Yet how profoundly unfair! One should never judge based on a singular lapse. There were numerous competitors still shining with stars while he remained in the dark!

Thus, throughout my stay, the Golden Eagle lamented daily, always concluding with a plea for my assistance in reclaiming the star—hoping I would leverage my influence on his behalf.

Looking ahead, one can only assume that a new system of guidebook evaluation will emerge. If the Golden Eagle has weathered the storms of war, he may find himself more accepting of his fate; perhaps a new travel guide will bestow upon him a star of its own. However, I do not intend to remain with him to find out for certain.
