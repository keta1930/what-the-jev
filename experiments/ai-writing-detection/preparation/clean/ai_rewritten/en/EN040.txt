As I sat in the garden moments ago, a writing pad resting on my knee, I found my thoughts drifting through the realms of the sky and the earth, searching for inspiration. My attention was suddenly captured by a distressing scene unfolding nearby. A small greenfly had become ensnared in a spider's web, desperately flapping its delicate wings in attempts to break free. The threads of the web were so fine they were nearly invisible, yet they were strong enough to support the spider, who was approaching its trapped prey with a menacing air. My initial disdain for the greenfly faded away, replaced by a fierce animosity towards the plump spider, who seemed to hold all the cards. In that moment, I thought of the Hun encircling the devastation of innocent Belgium. What chance did the vulnerable little insect have against the deviousness of this creature, which set its gossamer traps in the breeze and waited patiently until its victim was ensnared and powerless? What kind of justice exists in nature that permits such an uneven struggle?

At this point, the spider had reached the hapless fly and wrapped another filament around it. In a terrifying flash, the spider darted to the top of its web, disappearing into the structure of the arbor, pulling the new thread tight around the victim, which continued to flutter for a brief while before succumbing to its fate. Just then, I was summoned for lunch, and while seated at the table, I recounted the tale of the spider and the fly, my feelings for the spider unmistakably hostile. “That,” Robert, who had returned from the front, declared, “is merely a sentimental perspective. My sympathies lie entirely with the spider. It is a friend to humanity, a predator of insects, a cleaner of the garden. It plays a crucial role in maintaining nature's balance. Furthermore,” he continued, “I have seen you squash greenflies yourself. You did so because they were a nuisance. Why should you object to the spider doing the same kind of necessary work to earn its keep?”

“Ah,” I replied weakly, “I suppose it’s because he does it for a living.” “And you,” he interrupted, “do it for a living as well, since you desire your fruit trees to bear fruit, your roses to flourish, and your cabbages to thrive. Who is more ruthless than you when it comes to slugs and other pests that crawl or fly? No, no, we are all in pursuit of a living—yourself no less than the spider, and the spider no less than the fly.” “We are all Huns,” I remarked. “What a loathsome world it is.” “Not at all,” he countered. “It’s a rather delightful world. Here’s to the spider’s health!”

“And you feel no compassion for the fly?” I asked. “Not in the slightest,” he replied. “I am on the side of righteousness.” “Whose side is that?” I pressed. “Mine,” came his response. “We all must act according to our perspectives. That’s what the greenfly does. That’s what the spider does. We will never align all points of view in this world. We will continue to scramble for survival until the end. Sometimes the greenfly prevails, sometimes the spider does. Look at that cherry tree in the orchard. A month ago, it was heavy with fruit. Now, not a single cherry remains. The blackbirds and starlings have stripped it bare. Their perspective is that the cherries exist for their enjoyment, and they are correct. They are oblivious to the property laws that humanity creates for its own protection. There’s no sense in approaching them to show your title deeds or remind them of the police and anti-theft laws. Our moral framework is ours alone, not theirs.

“We are all products of our own viewpoints,” he continued. “Before Jones next door acquired a car, he felt very strongly against motorists—he often called them road-hogs and insisted he would tax these ‘land-torpedoes’ out of existence. He was filled with sympathy for the children coming home from school. Now, however, he drives as aggressively as anyone else, honking his horn loudly, and claims it’s outrageous how parents allow their children to play in the streets. Nothing has changed except his perspective. He simply shifted to a different position and now views everything from a new angle. Samuel Butler captured the absurdity of it long ago:—

What makes all doctrine plain and clear? About two hundred pounds a year. And that which was proved true before Prove false again? Two hundred more.”

“Are our viewpoints then solely dictated by our selfish desires, like those of your spider friend, who has likely consumed my greenfly by now?” “No, I don’t mean to imply that. I believe that underlying all our individual viewpoints, there exists a greater motive within human society—a world spirit, the mind of the race, or whatever term one prefers—that is more significant and virtuous than we are. The collective movement of humanity is, barring rare exceptions, loftier than its individual expressions. We respond to an abstract notion of justice, an ideal righteousness that is purer and better than our capacities allow. At our core, I believe we are superior to what our actions depict, more noble than our limited perspectives enable us to be, and in our moments of clarity, we catch a glimpse of that Jacob's ladder that Francis Thompson described, with its ascending angels, at Charing Cross. Someone once called Shelley ‘an ineffectual angel,’ and I think that many of us embody that same quality. Consider the current tragedy engulfing the world in horror. We are fighting fiercely for our own views, yet deep down we feel ashamed of the spectacle and recognize that humanity is better than its actions. One day, perhaps, the ineffectual angel will find its wings and rise beyond the spider’s perspective… And now, let’s go check on the spider’s progress.”

We ventured out to the garden and approached the web. However, the small green body was gone, and the spider was busy digesting its meal somewhere out of sight.
