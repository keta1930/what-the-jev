Deep beneath the frost, forces begin to stir restlessly in the earth's hidden depths. Bulbs test the limits of their confinement, as February’s quietude is imbued with a subtle tension. It’s as if the light’s power, pushing upward from the core of the earth and pouring down from the sun’s growing strength, has disturbed the dormant seeds, prompting them to respond to their liberator. Yet, the protective mother urges them to remain patient, whispering insistently, "Not yet, not yet!" It is wiser for them to remain behind the frozen barrier than to emerge prematurely into a world where the cold still prowls like a predator. The snow blankets this eager life, a gentle reminder of restraint, though the stirring is felt in February's woods, much like a swimmer sensing the varying currents in a spring-fed lake. Only the stars, thick and closer than in the months of lush foliage, shine brightly and calmly above. In February, the Milky Way reveals itself in divine clarity to the solitary souls—herders, mountaineers, fishermen, and trappers—who roam in the starlit hours of this somber and serene season. It is during the extended, frigid nights that the sky blooms with the brightest stars.

February is attuned to the rhythm of twilight’s wings. Birds that seemed to have abandoned us return, adventurers not overly fond of safety, eager to discover how the snow bunting and pine finch have raided the evergreens’ cones. Meanwhile, chickadees, sparrows, and crows monitor the more domestic food sources from their established perches, with a sparrow often managing to irritate even a duck into relinquishing her share of cracked corn. Perched along a brown-cloaked oak branch in the increasing light, crows display their regal feathers, glistening under the sun. In the shadowy depths of the hemlocks, the great horned owl has likely been busy amid the snow, crafting a nest that will soon cradle sturdy little fluffballs in March. The echoing love call of her mate reverberates through the woods. By the time the wren has settled her nest, these winter hatchlings will carry the weighty wisdom of their storied lineage.

There is no other time quite like the end of February for cleansing the brooks. Rushing yellow waters carry a somber assortment of torn leaves, twigs, acorn caps, and stranded bark rafts, remnants that will never seed. From either bank, depending on the ambitious flow of the flood, a figure stands, staff in hand, boldly signaling the water princess to break free. She then dances and sparkles over the gleaming gravel, slipping from beneath the ice that has trapped her in the uplands and flowing down into the softer valleys, where her vibrant presence will awaken the frogs in their muddy homes and coax the fish, hidden in lightless pools, to rise to the surface she brings.

As summer approaches, the winds and birds carry seeds from the frozen mountains, nurturing alpine flowers—lilies that lean against the unyielding snows, bright poppies, and delicately fringed herbs that change names with each new country. It seems only fair that flowers gracing the icy heights in July should also bloom in the warmer lowlands during February. The vibrant spectacle of blues, magentas, and scarlets adorning the harsh upper slopes of the Rockies, where summer nights can be bitterly cold—why wouldn’t such beauty thrive in the gentler warmth of a valley barn when icicles hang from the eaves? But nature dictates otherwise. The challenges posed by mountain storms can be borne by the delicate blooms, yet the treacherous winter of the lowlands, with its tempting suns followed by fierce desolation, is for flowers bred in a different lineage.

The light of February is clear yet tentative, akin to a delicate wine, far from the robust essence of April. This month lacks intoxication; instead, a subtle anticipation pulses through the air over silent fields, stemming from the secret stirrings of life held captive. Spring and sunrise are perpetual marvels, but the early hints of this wonder barely suggest the exuberance that is to come. Even the creatures of the forest tread with solemnity, grateful for any kindness offered by the sun, which hangs over a landscape draped in gray. Yet, they are acutely aware that their prolonged trials are far from over. Deer wander pathetically at the edges of farms, gazing at the cattle feasting on winter's pasture from the haystacks, often venturing after dark to clear fences and pilfer from this disheveled larder. No chipmunk dares to peek from the fence’s top rail. The woodchuck, after his solitary excursion on Candlemas—a duty performed for the enlightenment of mankind—has withdrawn from sight, whether in sunlight or shadow, and shows no inclination to stir just yet. While snowdrops may feel a flutter of unease, he is well aware of the ominous Ides of March! Among the quietest of Northern woods inhabitants, the otter glides from one ice-laden waterfall to another. The only lively figure left is the cottontail, who, pursued and polite, endeavors to avoid offense, save when hunger drives him to nibble on winter cabbage—yet he remains whimsical. The mink, too, flits like a ghost through the landscape.

As March approaches, mosses, the first to feel its tread, reveal a brighter hue in the swamps. Pussy willows have awakened a gray dawn in vine-filled alcoves, where the day’s light barely penetrates, and the blush of red willows hints at the possibility of cowslips blooming beneath the bank. The blue jay has alluded to this in his endless chatter, reluctant to foresee arbutus but hinting that as soon as the leaves start to peek through the snow... Once, he discovered a hepatica bud on the last day of February... Just last week, he conversed with his old companion, the muskrat... And when one can spot red pebbles in the creek at five o’clock in the afternoon... However, it is futile to anticipate yellow orchids on the western knoll this spring, since some people found them there last year, and after that, one might as well... Of course, cowslips beside red willows are strikingly beautiful, just as blue jays amid cedars with their blue berries... His tales are endless, but he has witnessed much throughout his life. February, it seems, relies on the unyielding and triumphant faith of her blue jays.
