As I was perusing an American journal, I stumbled upon a curious statement that compared the act of drinking beer from porcelain to the absurdity of giving Nietzsche a friendly slap on the back. The imagery struck me as amusing and led me to contemplate the peculiar notion of why one wouldn’t drink beer from porcelain. Typically, beer is enjoyed from sturdy earthenware mugs, pewter pots, or glass vessels, but never from fine china. If someone were to offer you a drink in a porcelain cup, the experience would feel diminished, as if the essence of the beverage had been compromised. Conversely, if you were to be served tea from a pewter container, it would evoke a similar sense of degradation. The rationale that one drink is coarse while the other is refined fails to explain this dissonance. People favor beer in glass containers, where the quality of the glass often enhances the experience. Yet, there seems to be an inherent discord between beer and porcelain, a mismatch that we can’t quite articulate.

I suspect that the issue isn’t that porcelain alters the flavor or quality of the beer itself. Instead, it’s a matter of an unspoken sense of appropriateness that is violated by such a pairing. The expected harmony is disrupted, leading to a dissonance that resonates within some deep, perhaps inexplicable, part of our being. The realm of taste illustrates these complex affinities and aversions in their most basic form. Who, for example, was the first to determine that the seemingly incompatible combination of mutton and red-currant jelly creates a delightful gastronomic experience? What stroke of inspiration led an unknown cook to pair apple sauce with roast pork, transforming it into something exquisite? Who was the culinary Prometheus that revealed the perfect companionship of a clove with apple pudding, a match that had awaited discovery through the ages?

In a broader sense, the world presents an endless treasure trove of materials, from which humanity, that intrepid adventurer, continuously unearths new forms of harmony. Musicians harness the vibrations of the air, uncovering the principles of musical concord, and from that understanding, the profound mystery of song emerges. Poets take words, weaving their rhythms into harmonious expressions of thought. Scientists observe the fall of an apple and glimpse a universe in symphonic motion, leaving the mind in a state of awe. Chefs transform the humble pig and the apple into a lyrical experience for the dinner table. Ultimately, the Great Adventure is the fervent quest for harmony in all things—both grand and trivial, spiritual and material. We all participate in this pursuit, guided by those who lead us to the pinnacle of revelation—whether it’s Bach, crafting that timeless Concerto for Two Violins that transports us to fields of asphodel; Wordsworth, gazing thoughtfully over Tintern Abbey and capturing the essence of something deeper that permeates the natural world; or Botticelli, skillfully intertwining the divine lines of the Madonna of the Magnificat into an enduring harmony that resonates within us forever. 

Though we may not be the leaders of the adventure, we too experience moments of clarity—those fleeting glimpses where our minds align with our surroundings, where the world’s beauty or the bonds of human connection evoke a sense of understanding that we yearn to grasp and claim as our own. 

"A mirage," you might dismiss as a cynical thought—"just a trick to keep us moving through the metaphorical desert, a carrot dangled before the donkey of mankind." Yet, looking at the current state of the world, it’s difficult to argue that if harmony is indeed the ultimate goal of our collective endeavor, humanity should perhaps abandon the mission. Given the turmoil we witness daily, it can feel as though man is not only the most dissonant creature on the planet but also the most brutal. Dryden’s famous lines, which once rang with optimism, now sound like bitter satire:

From harmony, from heavenly harmony, This universal frame began; From harmony to harmony, through all the compass of the notes it ran, The diapason closing full in man.

If Dryden were to observe Europe today, he might discover a significant flaw in his lofty ode.

However, the narrative of humanity is extensive, and we cannot fully comprehend its trajectory by evaluating any single event, no matter how monumental. We remain in the tumultuous infancy of our existence. While our actions may be horrendous, underlying them is a motive that distinguishes our current conflicts from those of the past. This motive centers around the concepts of human liberty, the sanctity of public law, and the right of every nation—regardless of size—to exist free from the threat of force. In ancient or medieval times, could one have found a war fought for such a grand idea? Tyranny reigned supreme then. The Pax Romana was merely a peace achieved through universal subjugation, not the liberty rooted in law that we strive for today. Never before has democracy, embattled yet resolute, challenged the principle of tyranny for global supremacy.

I can almost hear your thoughts as you consider the Allies. Liberty! Does Russia truly represent freedom? Yes, in the context of today’s circumstances, even Russia embodies liberty, for this is not merely a conflict initiated by bureaucratic forces, but one fueled by the fervor of the Russian populace. Setting aside Russia, who can deny that the cause of human freedom rests with us, while the forces of ancient tyranny lie with our adversaries? Might we not glimpse in the chaos of war the twilight of those old gods—those deities of blood and iron that have subjugated humanity throughout its history? Even amid this cacophony of destruction and death, could there be the dawn of a new humanity—the promise of a liberated world?

Perhaps, in a future when the tragedies of today are but a distant memory in the grand narrative of mankind, it will become clear that Dryden was not mocking us, but rather that this profound discord heralds the arrival of a final harmony toward which our better selves yearn. It may seem an arduous vision to cling to in the present moment, but we must hold onto it or risk accepting the horrifying alternative—that this world is, in truth, a madhouse. Can you accept that notion? Is it worth living under such a belief? If not, then we must rally behind the former vision, each contributing in our small or significant ways to ensure it prevails.

I may have drifted somewhat from the initial contemplation of beer and porcelain, yet I believe the progression of thought connects the two, revealing a vast landscape through the lens of a seemingly trivial observation.
