In an American publication, I came across an amusing anecdote: "An elderly woman was being guided to the location where a hero met his demise. 'I’m not surprised,' she responded. 'It's so slippery that I almost fell myself.'"

This tale, which has long been part of English folklore, is commonly associated with Nelson and his ship, the Victory. It has become such a well-known quip amongst playful tourists visiting that vessel that the more astute guides make it a point to mention it first. Yet in America, this story may still hold a certain novelty and could very well begin a new chapter in its life. It is likely to persist indefinitely in English-speaking regions, reviving with each retelling and finding fresh audiences who encounter it for the first time.

It raises an interesting question as to why we often feel irked when an editor or comedian presents us with a joke that has been recycled. This irritation seems to stem, at least in part, from the fact that we have invested our money—whether for the newspaper or the ticket to a performance—and thus, we feel cheated. We believe we are purchasing a fresh and original experience, so when confronted with an old and perhaps even familiar joke—one we might have told ourselves—it heightens our indignation. One wonders, however, what a comedic publication or show devoid of any old material would even resemble. That remains a mystery.

Curiously, our displeasure does not solely encompass the age of the joke, even if it resides in our personal memory. We also resent the laughter of those in the audience who are hearing it for the first time—perhaps three-quarters of the crowd. There’s an unspoken question that arises: how dare they find it amusing when they haven’t encountered it previously? Recently, while seated next to a candid friend known for their generally good-natured demeanor, I found myself amused by a distinctly humorous remark from one of London’s absurd comedians. Engaged in a contest of memory with another performer, he concluded the debate with the claim that he could remember London Bridge when it was merely a cornfield. To me, this was as fresh as it was nonsensical, and I reacted accordingly. However, my friend was incensed. “Good heavens!” he exclaimed, with the disapproving click of his tongue that often accompanied such judgments, “How could you resurrect that? It’s as ancient as the hills.” His expression shifted to one of stern disapproval.

What we must remember, and what might have alleviated my friend’s rigid anger had he considered it, is that a new audience is perpetually emerging, for whom nothing is stale. This notion may not be comforting to those who prefer their humor to possess a certain antiquity, but it is a reality grounded in nature. Just as I was once told by a solemn nurse that a child is born every moment (she added, with a finger raised for emphasis, that someone also dies in that same instant), so too is an older child reaching an age where they can appreciate and understand humor nearly every moment. To these children, every story feels original. With this fresh audience, eager and appreciative, one wonders why humorists strive so diligently for novelty. Though perhaps they do not.

There are likely various theories regarding the oldest jokes in existence, yet I am unfamiliar with them. What is evident, however, is that people are inherently prepared for every story to be old, as demonstrated by how readily audiences noted when Mark Twain’s “Jumping Frog” was translated into Greek for a school reader. Many remarked that Twain had drawn from ancient literature for his humor. This peculiar tendency reveals our collective desire for stories to possess a sense of familiarity. While we do enjoy a new tale, we often value even more the ability to claim that it resonates with us as something we have encountered before.

Certain stories seem to resurface periodically, particularly those with a military context. For instance, during the Great War, anecdotes featuring such themes reemerged. I recall hearing during the Boer War about a young man who was turned away from enlisting due to dental issues. “But I want to fight the Boers,” he protested, “not eat them.” This clever retort resurfaced between 1914 and 1918, but this time, the young man insisted he did not wish to consume the Germans. I am certain that a similar narrative found its way into the accounts of the Crimean War, where another recruit claimed he did not want to eat the Russians, and a century earlier, someone was asserting the same about the French. In fact, it is likely that this theme could be traced through every conflict in history. Perhaps a young Hittite with poor dental health proclaimed a desire to battle the Amalekites rather than feast upon them. Each iteration of this story remains equally effective, and there has always been a vast new audience ready to appreciate it. As long as warfare persists and humans retain teeth—an assertion I’ve heard might not hold forever—this anecdote will remain popular. Eventually, it may transition into a different narrative phase based on other deficiencies in the recruit's dental health, and this cycle will continue until universal peace reigns or, as some predict, life itself comes to an end with the cooling of the sun.
