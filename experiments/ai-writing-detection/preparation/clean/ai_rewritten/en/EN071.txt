During a recent visit to a village, I found that the quickest route to the inn traversed the churchyard. As I frequently walked through this area, I became somewhat familiar with the deceased residents, perhaps even more so than with the living ones. My understanding, however, was not as profound as that of Edgar Lee Masters in his remarkable work, "Spoon River Anthology." Instead, my acquaintance with these individuals was rather superficial yet tinged with a certain fondness. The fact that they were total strangers, with their stories only accessible to those brave enough to engage in a séance—led by the likes of Sir Oliver and Sir Conan—made my musings about them all the more curious. Their names, in particular, captivated my imagination. 

Many names had succumbed to the ravages of time, rendered illegible by the slow but relentless touch of nature—moss and lichen covering the inscriptions as if they were veils of secrecy. Yet, among those that remained, some were truly remarkable. One has to wonder why names of the deceased often possess a certain charm or peculiarity absent in contemporary monikers. Has anyone ever encountered a Lavender Wiseways in real life? Yet beneath one of those stones lay a Lavender Wiseways, along with her sister Lavinia, who had not married—perhaps to preserve their unique identities. Similarly, one can imagine a time when a gentleman named Paradine Ebb, a farmer near London, might have been greeted warmly in the marketplace. But if I continue down this path, I risk revealing the village’s name, which I prefer to keep to myself in these nosy times.

However, my thoughts were not solely focused on these unusual names when I began writing; rather, I contemplated the epitaphs etched on the tombstones. Some were succinct and unassuming, while others were lengthy and grandiose, yet they all seemed to shy away from conveying anything truly meaningful about the lives of those interred beneath. They often appeared to dismiss the value of earthly existence. Why has no one dared to encourage the mason to carve a genuine tribute to the life that has been left behind? Is that too audacious a request? The inscriptions suggest that none of these souls ever experienced joy.

Consider an epitaph like this:

Here Lies HENRY ROBINSON  
Who lived in the belief—and, with many failures, did his best to act upon it—that if you spend your time striving to make things a little easier and more joyful in this world, the next can take care of itself.

The prevailing sentiment of many graveyards implies that life has been a lengthy ordeal, merely a prelude to a more satisfying afterlife. However, we are aware that this is not the reality for many. Numerous lives, though they may not conform to the somber notions typically associated with cemeteries, are filled with happiness. In the God's Acre I am describing, there exists a cautionary note to the living about the dangers of earthly contentment—an unfair stance for those who have passed and reminiscent of the fable of the fox and his tail. Nearby, an elaborate stone holds dreary couplets cautioning passersby that the next grave might be theirs, urging them to reflect on morbid thoughts instead of enjoying life. The deceased could surely afford to be kinder, more generous, and more benevolent in their reminders.

I envision a gravestone that might read:

Here Lies SARAH DOE  
Who cherished the laughter of children, the warmth of friends, and the beauty of the world; she encouraged others to embrace joy and live fully.

Not only do these inscriptions convey a cautionary tale about life, but they also offer little encouragement regarding what lies beyond. The spirit embodied in these epitaphs is far from uplifting. While nothing can make death appealing, even the most persuasive advocate would struggle against the oppressive dampness, gloom, and decay that permeates the churchyard, along with the insincere attempts at resignation that the stones portray. We ought to inspire more courage and positivity. A rector who permitted only cheerful epitaphs would truly be worth his keep.

Would there be any great impropriety in the following inscription?

Here Lies JOHN SMITH  
Who found earth pleasant and reveled in its beauty; who loved and was loved in return; who desired nothing more than to continue living. He departed this world reluctantly, wishing for happiness to those he left behind. Carpe diem.

Upon reading such an epitaph, I hope the visitor would not be transformed into a hedonistic fiend.

Occasionally, a human flaw could be recorded by the stonemason without threatening the foundations of society. Once our friends have passed away, why not share a few truths? Some secrets are best revealed. For instance:

Here Lies (in no expectation of immortality) THOMAS BROWN  
He was no Friend of the Church but fulfilled his obligations, disturbed no one, and kept his word.

What would occur if Thomas Brown's acquaintances funded such an inscription? Would society falter? Or consider this:

Here Lies MARY JONES  
The wife of William Jones. Remember her fondly, for she was patient when her husband overindulged in drink.

I imagine memorial verses beginning with such honesty and warmth would offer a more authentic reflection of life, capturing the essence of those who have passed and celebrating their humanity rather than merely marking their departure.
