On the infrequent occasions when I dress up in my finest attire and step into the social world, I am invariably taken aback by the sheer number of unfamiliar faces surrounding me. Whether I find myself in a bustling ballroom or at a packed restaurant, I often pause to consider that among the hundreds of individuals present, there is not a single person whose existence I had previously acknowledged. Yet, each one of them leads a life of tremendous significance—not just to themselves, but also to a multitude of friends and family. Each day, they face their own challenges, and there inevitably comes a moment for each individual when the rest of the world, including—much to my chagrin—myself, seems utterly irrelevant. The fact that I have spent all these years blissfully unaware of their lives fills me with a sense of embarrassment.

Today, dressed in my oldest attire, I have perused the index of The Times Literary Supplement, and I find myself grappling with a profound sense of shame over my lack of knowledge regarding numerous books. The sheer number of novels alone appears to hover around 900. To me, the act of penning even a thoroughly inconsequential novel requires remarkable perseverance; yet, in approximately 600 households, this endeavor has been underway, while I (along with you and countless others) have remained completely apathetic. This morning, I have endeavored to compensate for my previous indifference by examining the titles of these works. While merely reading titles pales in comparison to engaging with the books themselves, it does afford me the luxury of claiming acquaintance with various novels, and in some cases, it even provides a faint hint of their content.

In my estimation, the most enjoyable aspect of crafting a novel must be the selection of a title. A title should encapsulate the essence of the work and offer a prospective reader some insight into what they are about to purchase. Opting for a title like Ethnan Frame, Esther Grant, John Temple, or John Merridew (as noted in the index) fails to assist the reader in any meaningful way. Such titles merely indicate that a character within the pages bears that name, which is likely inconsequential to the reader. Conversely, a title like Phyllis does hint at the nature of the narrative. It suggests that no tragic or intensely realistic story would ever bear the name Phyllis. I would surmise that this novel is a delightful exploration of suburban life, predominantly conveyed through dialogue, featuring Phyllis as an endearing character, albeit somewhat unkind regarding that initial box of chocolates he sent her. Nevertheless, they do end up married in the end.

However, if one chooses not to call their book Phyllis, John Temple, or Mrs. Elmsley, what alternatives exist? You might consider the title Kapak, as I have noticed someone has done. The remarkable aspect of Kapak is that whether you approach the book from the front or the back, it remains unchanged. A title that reads the same in either direction provides substantial advantage to the author. Furthermore, in this instance, there is an element of intrigue surrounding Kapak that compels one to dive deeper. Is it a beloved nickname for a bride’s father-in-law, a secret password to a magical realm, a new product for polishing brown boots, or perhaps merely a camera? One cannot help but feel inclined to purchase the book and uncover the truth.

Another enigmatic title is The Man with Thicker Beard, which likely carries some significance. Like Kapak, it is palindromic, maintaining the same appearance in reverse; however, it lacks the subtlety of its predecessor. Still, it would likely entice readers to explore its contents. On the other hand, A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette is precisely the type of title that would not attract our interest. The mere act of asking a shopkeeper, "Do you have A Welsh Nightingale and a Would-be Suffragette?" feels cumbersome. If we were to forget the title and instead inquire about a would-be nightingale and a Welsh suffragette, or perhaps a wood nightingale and a Welsh rabbit, or even the Welsh suffragette's night in jail, we would rapidly regret not opting for something straightforward like Greed, Earth, or Jonah.

This brings me to the realization that employing a French title often proves to be a misstep. Authors must bear in mind that their readers not only need to order the book verbally but also recommend it to their acquaintances. Thus, I believe Mr. Oliver Onions erred in naming his short story collection Pot au Feu. While it is an appealing title, it poses a challenge for anyone attempting to recommend it, often prompting the response, "What?" When faced with such confusion after attempting to pronounce the title in your best Parisian accent, the only recourse is to shift the topic entirely. Yet, it is high time we reached a consensus on the qualities that constitute an ideal title. Kapak, as mentioned, may attract buyers, though some might find it somewhat unfair. While it excels commercially, it does not fulfill our initial criteria; the title must resonate with the book's essence. In one sense, Five Gallons of Gasolene does achieve this, but admittedly, it is not something one would seek in a bookstore.

Now, consider a title that embodies perfection: Their High Adventure. It conveys just enough meaning on its own. When a Man's Married, For Henri and Navarre, and The King Over the Water are slightly more overt, yet still effective. The Love Story of a Mormon is straightforward, lacking any attempt at deception, but it hardly qualifies as a beautiful title. In contrast, Melody in Silver possesses a certain elegance, yet this very beauty can instill hesitation in a potential buyer, for fear of disappointment lurking within its pages. As I scan the index, I find myself relieved by the multitude of novels I have yet to read. The truth is, many of them would likely lead to disillusionment. Nowadays, one reads books primarily to engage in conversation with others during those rare social outings, asking questions like, "HAVE you read The Forged Coupon, and WHAT are your thoughts on The Muck Rake?" For such exchanges, an index suffices quite well.
