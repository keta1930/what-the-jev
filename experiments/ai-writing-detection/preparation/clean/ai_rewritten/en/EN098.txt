The Bar has always struck me as the most esteemed of professions, a field where its practitioners can maintain their dignity, no matter the circumstances. This is not the case with other professions. A teacher can be derisively referred to as an usher, a doctor may be humorously branded a licensed killer, and solicitors often face criminal charges for mishandling trust funds, earning the moniker of mere attorneys. Civil servants, on the other hand, are often seen as lethargic, waking only for their pensions at retirement. However, barristers stand apart from this mockery; the only jest one might make about them is to refer to them as “my learned friend.” Their claim to professionalism carries more weight than that of an actor. The reason behind this perception eludes me—perhaps it is simply because they don their top hats while walking through the Temple.

Many of my acquaintances have dabbled in the legal profession, having “eaten dinners” at the Inns of Court, making it challenging to voice any criticism of their chosen path. Additionally, one can never be too certain when one might find themselves in need of legal representation. Nonetheless, I will take the plunge and put the barrister on trial. "Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, consider this well-tailored individual before you. What can we say about him?"

To begin, let us reflect on our expectations from a profession. First and foremost, we undoubtedly seek a livelihood, yet I believe we desire something beyond mere financial compensation. If offered a thousand pounds a year to walk daily from Charing Cross to Barnet, although poverty might compel acceptance, we would not take pride in such a vocation. We would rather earn that same sum by engaging in work that contributes positively to society. A person of any sensitivity would only find a walking profession bearable if they could convince themselves that their efforts revived the forgotten art of walking or highlighted the overlooked attractions of Barnet. Ideally, after numerous journeys, curious individuals might begin to follow, intrigued by the purpose behind those walks, discovering the old Norman church at the crossroads or, at the very least, developing a healthier appetite for their dinners. In essence, one must believe they are acting not solely for personal gain but for the benefit of the community as well.

Thus, I contend that a profession is deemed noble or ignoble based on whether it allows its practitioners to work for an end greater than their personal advancement. A physician may charge fees, yet their motivation lies in alleviating suffering. A teacher earns a living through instruction, but they see themselves as champions of education. Artists, regardless of their chosen medium, seek to convey a message to the world, sharing their truths—which may benefit them but are not solely for their profit. All these professions, along with countless others, possess an inherent nobility. We embark on these paths filled with aspirations, promising ourselves that we will remain true to our ideals and not compromise our principles for personal interests. Yet, we inevitably stumble. The painter, lured by financial success, churns out repetitive works. The physician prioritizes bedside manner over groundbreaking research. The teacher abandons innovative educational theories to conform to administrative standards. Our failures do not stem from the ignobility of our professions; rather, they arise from our inability to stay true to our aspirations. The guiding light remains for those who seek it; it continues to beckon.

Now, what about the Bar? Does a barrister strive for anything beyond personal advancement? What ideals do they pursue? It matters little whether they succeed more or less frequently than others in reaching these ideals; what I seek to understand is what motivates them. In those quiet moments when we are alone, stripped of external validation, we sometimes confront the unsettling thought of our contributions. If a barrister experiences such an introspective moment, what reassurance can they draw from it? The only solace may lie in the belief that they are contributing to the administration of Justice. To take pride in their profession, to feel justified in their choices during those solitary reflections, they must convince themselves that they play a vital role in upholding legal rights and addressing legal wrongs. 

However, this is not enough. Just as a doctor, through knowledge and experience, becomes more effective in combating illness—benefiting both themselves and the field of medicine—so must the barrister be able to assert that their growing skill as an advocate enhances their capacity to serve the ideal of Justice. But can they genuinely make that claim? I struggle to see how they could. While their increasing expertise may serve their interests and those of their clients, it does not necessarily advance the ideal of Justice itself. Consider a hypothetical case, Smith v. Jones. Counsel represents Smith. After reviewing the facts, he concludes, “Legally speaking, the odds are against us. However, sentiment might sway the jury in our favor. It’s worth a shot.” He proceeds, and if he is sufficiently skilled, he secures a victory. A personal success for him, but what becomes of the ideal? Did he ever contemplate, “I must do my utmost for my client, even if he is in the wrong, and hope for a loss?” I suspect not. The prevailing ethos of the Bar teaches that the focus should be on winning, not on pursuing justice. Ultimately, what is the true pursuit of a barrister? What does the Bar provide beyond the allure of financial rewards?

Earlier, I inquired about the ideals of a barrister. Let’s reframe the question: What does it mean to be an ideal barrister? If we were to casually define an ideal doctor, we wouldn’t necessarily envision a prestigious figure on Harley Street. An ideal teacher does not equate to the Headmaster of Eton or the proprietor of a lucrative preparatory school. Yet, can we conceive of an ideal barrister that is not synonymous with success in terms of wealth and status? The enthusiastic novice writer might aspire to emulate Francis Thompson over Sir Hall Caine, and an aspiring clergyman might find inspiration in the life of Father Damien rather than the Archbishop of Canterbury. But what aspiration can a budding barrister realistically pursue, save for the star of financial success? If they do not envision themselves as Sir Edward Carson, it’s likely because they believe that the more effective path lies in mirroring Sir John Simon’s approach.

There may be alternative responses to the queries I have posed, yet questioning the necessity of barristers in the administration of law does not provide an answer. I cannot fathom how such a system could function without them, just as I find it hard to imagine how roads could be kept clean without someone to do the work. However, this does not prevent me from expressing my belief that road-sweeping is an unappealing profession. Similarly, I feel entitled to share my perspective regarding the Bar. In my view, it offers only tangible victories and lacks spiritual fulfillment. There exists no standard by which its members can be measured except for earthly success, and there is no space within its ranks for altruism or idealism. For these reasons, I assert that the Bar does not belong among the noble professions.
