A few weeks ago, I relocated to a new home, and naturally, my books made the journey alongside me. Strong men, sweaty from exertion, packed them into boxes and lugged them to the moving van, grumbling about Caxton as they worked. Upon reaching my new place, they carried the boxes into the designated room for my library, removed the lids, and awaited further instructions. My immediate concern was to clear out the empty room. In a hurry, we moved the books onto the pristine white shelves that had been prepared for them, prioritizing getting them off the floor over any particular order. We stacked armful after armful hastily, with only a brief pause when the foreman’s eye caught one of my titles. "Did you write this one, sir?" he inquired. I admitted it, and he replied with a noncommittal "H'm," before looking through the remaining stacks and appearing slightly taken aback by the number of titles I had not authored. It seems writing is a rather relaxed profession.

Eventually, the books found their place on the shelves, and there they remain. I told myself that I would organize them properly when a rainy day presented itself. When that rainy day finally arrived, I instead pushed the task to some future morning. As things stand, finding a specific book requires scanning every shelf, and encountering Keats offers no assurance of being close to Shelley. If he hasn’t mysteriously vanished, he’s likely wedged between How to Be a Golfer Though Middle-aged and something else equally perplexing.

Having penned that observation, I felt compelled to check where Shelley actually resided on my shelves. The situation is worse than I had anticipated. He is currently lodged between Geometrical Optics and Studies in New Zealand Scenery. Ella Wheeler Wilcox, whom I had unwittingly invited into my collection, shares a space with Anarchy or Order, a title that was apparently sent in hopes of persuading me to join the Duty and Discipline Movement—a hope that seems futile, as I have yet to fulfill my subscription. Meanwhile, What I Found Out, an account by an English Governess, occupies a corner with The Recreations of a Country Parson, followed closely by Villette and Baedeker's Switzerland. Clearly, I need to take action. Yet, I find myself pondering what that action should be. If I misled you into believing my books were neatly organized in their previous shelves, I apologize. They were arranged in what one might call "all anyhow." Perhaps they were slightly less "anyhow" than they are now, as volumes of the same work were at least grouped together, but that’s about the extent of their order. For years, I avoided the task of tidying them up, just as I’m continuing to do now. It isn’t due to laziness; rather, I am simply at a loss regarding where to start.

Let’s consider the idea of grouping all the poetry together. This seems reasonable at first glance. However, there’s an issue of size: Byron stands a towering eleven inches tall (he is my tallest poet), while Beattie, my shortest, is just over four inches. Placing them side by side would result in a rather absurd sight. You may not be familiar with Beattie, but rest assured, he was indeed a poet. He composed those grand lines about the shepherd:

"The shepherd-swain of whom I mention made  
On Scotia's mountains fed his little flock;  
The sickle, scythe or plough he never swayed—  
An honest heart was almost all his stock."

It’s hardly reasonable to expect a shepherd to wield a plow in the conventional manner, but Beattie appropriately highlighted that Edwin, the shepherd-swain, didn’t either. "And yet poor Edwin was no vulgar boy," we learn later in a line that deserves to endure. Now that I’ve established Beattie’s legitimacy as a poet, I can return to my point: an eleven-inch Byron cannot be positioned alongside a four-inch Beattie and followed by an eight-inch Cowper without creating an awkward shelf display. Yet, how could I possibly discard Beattie—Beattie, who wrote:

"And now the downy cheek and deepened voice  
Gave dignity to Edwin's blooming prime."

This reveals the conundrum. If one arranges books by their contents, the shelf quickly becomes untidy. Conversely, organizing them by size or color might produce an aesthetically pleasing display, but it risks obscuring Beattie from the poetically inclined visitor. Before we make any decisions about the arrangement, we must confront that rather uncomfortable question: "Why do we have books on our shelves at all?" It’s a challenging question to tackle.

You might instinctively assert that the correct response is an indignant claim that you purchased them to read, placing them on your shelves for easy reference. However, upon deeper reflection, one realizes how foolish that answer is. If reading were your sole purpose, why would some books be bound in luxurious morocco or half-calf covers? Why opt for a first edition when a hundredth edition could be acquired for much less? Why do you possess multiple copies of The Rubaiyat? What makes this particular book so precious that you safeguard it so meticulously? Perhaps it’s the fact that its pages remain uncut. If you were to slice the pages open and read it, would that not diminish its value?

Thus, it becomes clear that your library serves a purpose beyond mere reference. You are aware, as much as I am, that it adds character to your room—more so than paint, mahogany furniture, or china ever could. While it is certainly pleasant to have the books accessible for when you need them, such as when you’re writing an article on sea-bathing and need to recall Coleridge’s well-known words, the library is not only functional but also decorative. Let’s not shy away from acknowledging the aesthetic appeal. The more I look at my collection, the more I appreciate its visual contribution. The arrangement as it stands possesses a certain charm that might be lost if I were to reorganize it. For example, Baedeker's Switzerland and Villette are both red, a hue that is beautifully mirrored by a volume of Browning and Jevons' Elementary Logic after a brief interlude of blue. Just yesterday, a visitor remarked, "How lovely your books look," which suggests that perhaps that’s enough. There’s a casual elegance about their current state that I might forfeit if I were to impose a strict arrangement.

Yet, perhaps I could venture to align them all so that their spines face the same direction. Yes, one of these fine days (or perhaps on a rainy night), I shall take my library seriously. There are still a few books that are facing the wrong way, and I will make it a priority to turn them around.
