In a quiet corner of a London club, a small assembly of men, each of whom had achieved remarkable success in their respective fields, gathered around the fireplace after a dinner. This group comprised a distinguished lawyer, a renowned politician, a prominent clergyman, and a seasoned journalist. As their conversation meandered through various topics, it inevitably landed on a familiar question: If given the chance, would you choose to relive your life? With one notable exception, the consensus was a resounding "No." Interestingly, the exception was not the clergyman. He, like the others, felt that a single experience of life was sufficient and had no desire to revisit it.

This inquiry, I suspect, is as ancient as humanity itself. The response is equally timeless, often mirroring that of our gathered company by the fire. This question typically arises in midlife, as youthful exuberance tends to overlook such contemplations. In our younger years, life unfolds like an endless horizon, rendering the notion of its recurrence irrelevant. It is only when we reach the peak of our journey, beginning to descend with the evening shadows casting long over the valley, that this question emerges unbidden. The answer does not imply that the journey was unworthy; rather, it signifies the weariness of the path traveled. We find ourselves exhausted, longing for rest, and it serves as nature’s way of reconciling us with our mortality. Life has shown us its grand spectacle, and now it prepares us for our inevitable return to the earth.

Indeed, we come to accept the finality of life without complaint, even if offered the chance to turn back time, we would likely decline. 

However, this contemplation should not be confused with the more frivolous inquiry: "Is life worth living?" The men in the smoking-room would have responded to that question with an immediate and dismissive "Yes." Each had experienced life as a magnificent adventure, making the most of their time and cherishing every moment of its rich tapestry. This sentiment is true for all who possess a healthy mindset. In our darker moments, we might echo Job and lament our birth, yet such curses quickly fade. Swift, despite his mourning of his own birthday, could not truly despise life, for to loathe humanity would render existence unbearable. The depth of our sympathies directly correlates with our joy in living.

Even those who adopt the bleakest perspectives on life tend to avoid self-destructive actions. A friend recounted a day spent with a renowned author known for his grim philosophical outlook. In the morning, the writer lamented that each day brought a wish to have never been born. Yet in the afternoon, when faced with the opportunity to drown due to a mishap with a sailing boat, he turned down the chance with unexpected urgency. It’s likely he continued to hold onto his belief about not wanting to exist, but his actions betrayed a different truth. We all engage in a form of imaginative play, reminiscent of children who explore a world of "make-believe."

Ultimately, we are grateful for the journey we have taken, but the idea of traversing the same path again is what gives us pause. Occasionally, you may hear individuals say, "Yes, if I could relive my life with the wisdom I have now." This reflects a desire to return while armed with the knowledge of how to navigate life’s challenges more effectively. However, this perspective trivializes the question at hand. It suggests that life should not be a grand enigma, where each day invites us to explore the unexplored, but rather a safe investment that guarantees joy without shadows. Such an existence would be a dull procession devoid of excitement. Strip away the uncertainties of life, and you strip away its enchantment. It would be akin to stepping to the crease in cricket with the assurance of scoring runs at will; no one would find that appealing. The thrill lies in the unpredictability, whether one faces immediate failure or achieves remarkable success. Each dawn carries the same wonder as that first moment of creation, ensuring that life remains a story still unfolding.

Moreover, how could past experiences truly benefit us? It is often said that character shapes destiny. If one were to return with the same flaws—say, a weak chin and a timid demeanor—no amount of accumulated wisdom would spare one from a life lacking purpose.

If we were to relive our existence, it would have to be under the same unknown circumstances, to keep the experience meaningful. We would arrive once more, like wanderers emerging from the eternal, yearning for a fleeting adventure in time. Despite the allure of this journey, our overall sentiment leans towards not repeating it. We understand that everything that makes life precious would disappear along with the familiar faces and cherished connections from our past. The thought of forming new bonds, of forging new paths, feels somewhat disloyal. Holmes once penned a poem about being "Homesick in heaven," but the notion of feeling homesick on earth is far more poignant—wandering among the specters of past memories, attempting to recapture a sense of what once was. We would make new friends; they might even be better than those we lost, but we would not seek improvement. Our hearts would ache for the old companions.

A poignant image from Guido Rey's esteemed book about the Matterhorn comes to mind, encapsulating the essence of our sentiments. As he approached the mountain to climb it, he spotted an elderly man standing alone in the evening light on one of the lower slopes. It was Whymper, the trailblazer of the Matterhorn, now aged and reflecting on the majestic rock he had conquered in his youth. His days of climbing were behind him, and he sought no further triumphs on the peaks. Content with his past, he stood apart, cherishing his memories, leaving the thrill of conquest to the young and eager. Each memory he held was precious, save for that harrowing moment during his victory over the Matterhorn. Yet, had anyone inquired if he still craved those lofty heights and celestial wonders, he would have likely replied, "It is enough."
