I find myself standing in the same spot where countless others have stood, gazing up at the magnificent Belfry Tower of Bruges and contemplating, as many have before me, that its design seems to challenge the principles of architectural propriety. Its construction appears intentionally disproportionate, created to elicit a singular, striking impression of height. It resembles a church raised on stilts. This sort of grand yet awkward structure encapsulates the unique spirit and vigor of these Flemish towns. Flanders boasts some of the flattest and most mundane landscapes, yet its buildings are among the most audacious and extravagant. Nature here is subdued; it is civilization that brims with wild energy. The fields stretch out as flat and uniform as a paved plaza, while the streets and rooftops display a chaotic vivacity that resembles a forest whipped by a tempest. The serene waters meander through the countryside as gently as if they flowed through London’s plumbing, yet even the local parish pump is embellished with intricate carvings of wild creatures. This duality reflects a broader truth about art itself. While we often speak of untamed animals, it is humanity that embodies true wildness. In music, there exist sounds that evoke ancient and profound emotions, far surpassing the cries of the most exotic beasts in the dead of night. Similarly, there are structures whose formless strength seems to rise from the earth like primordial monsters, alongside spires that ascend abruptly, reminiscent of startled birds taking flight.

This primal energy manifested in stone speaks to a distinctive aspect of the human spirit. All the creatures of the field maintain a sense of respectability; only humans have truly broken free from decorum. While animals live within the bounds of domestication, it is humanity that often exhibits wild behavior. Furthermore, while this frantic creativity can be found across all human artistic expressions, it is particularly pronounced in Christian art. This notion often leads people to label Christianity as barbaric or a product of ignorance. However, historically speaking, that’s far from the truth; it emerged during one of the most stable and civilized eras in history.

Yet, there is undeniably something in Christianity that disrupts the framework of conventional beauty, something that infuses passion into the serene features of classical sculptures like the Apollo and impels the horses of the Elgin Marbles into spirited motion. Christianity possesses a rawness that is deeply rooted, echoing a kind of primitive energy, reminiscent of the fervor found in African American spirituals. I recall an engaging discussion in which I had praised the power of militant music in religious settings. Someone posed the question of whether I could envision Christ parading down the street accompanied by a brass band. I responded that I could easily imagine such a scene, as Christ clearly embraced a natural exuberance in significant moments. When children in the streets raised their voices too loudly, some overly proper disciples attempted to silence them in the name of decorum. To which Christ retorted, “If these were silent, the very stones would cry out.” Those words invoked the vast artistic legacy that sprang from this belief. They laid the groundwork for Gothic architecture, a style that seems to have sprouted in towns like Bruges as naturally as trees grow leaves. Here, any random brick or molding can be transformed into a vibrant face, with the facades of grand buildings teeming with open mouths—angels exalting God or devils rebelling against Him. The very rock appears contorted and tortured, as if it is crying out in agony. This miracle has been achieved: the stones themselves do indeed proclaim their existence.

While this fierce creativity may be a hallmark of humanity as a whole and a defining feature of Christian art specifically, it shines most brightly within the artistic expressions of Flanders. All Gothic structures are rich with intricate details, yet the overall design of Bruges’ Belfry is a particularly striking example of this extravagance. It resembles an elongated creature, akin to a giraffe, and this sense of exaggeration is pervasive throughout every corner of a Flemish town. If one were to ask why the inhabitants of such flat terrain instinctively erected these towering, flamboyant monuments, the only answer that comes to mind is, “Because they were the people of these flat countries.” When posed with the question of why the citizens of Bruges sacrificed traditional architecture to embrace dizzying heights, the response can only be, “Because Nature offered them no incentive to do otherwise.”

As I continue to gaze at the Belfry, I can’t help but smile at the memory of friends in London who firmly believe that children will inevitably reflect the qualities of what they refer to as “the right environment.” Environment can be a perplexing factor, producing outcomes that are sometimes beneficial, sometimes detrimental, and often a complex blend of both. A beautiful setting might inspire a child to appreciate beauty, or conversely, it could lead to an indifference towards it; most likely, these conflicting influences will cancel each other out. In reality, the impact of one’s environment may be negligible.

In the realm of historical analysis, which once enjoyed popularity and remains conventional today, we often encounter lists attributing the characteristics of nations to their geographic conditions. It has been suggested, for instance, that the Spaniards are passionate due to their warm climate, while Scandinavians are adventurous because of their cold surroundings. Englishmen are said to be seafaring due to their island status, and the Swiss are considered free-spirited thanks to their mountainous terrain. While such observations are appealing, I am equally convinced that I could compile a list that directly counters these claims, demonstrating the opposite influence of geography. For example, Spaniards have charted more continents than their Scandinavian counterparts, despite the former's climate potentially discouraging effort. Likewise, the Dutch have fought valiantly for their freedom, much like the Swiss, even though they lack mountains. Furthermore, many Mediterranean cultures, including Pagan Greece and Rome, have developed a disdain for the sea, despite having the most navigable waters at their disposal. The list could easily go on indefinitely. However, two examples stand out as particularly significant and indisputable. First, the Swiss, who reside beneath awe-inspiring cliffs and glaciers, have produced virtually no art or literature, instead embodying a pragmatic, sensible, and business-oriented ethos. Second, the people of Belgium, who inhabit a landscape as flat as a carpet, have, through an inner drive, aspired to elevate their towers toward the heavens.

Given the uncertainty surrounding the extent to which individuals align with or diverge from their environments, I find little solace in believing that contemporary discussions about environment hold much practical significance. Instead of continuing to dwell on these modern theories, I choose to return my focus to the Belfry of Bruges. I would give these theories more consideration if I were not convinced that they would fade into obscurity long before the Belfry itself ever does.
