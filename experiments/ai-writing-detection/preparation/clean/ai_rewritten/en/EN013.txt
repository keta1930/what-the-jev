It’s likely that many who have never run a shop have, at some point, entertained the notion that owning one could be enjoyable—naturally, in a manner of their choosing, offering only items they appreciate and to customers they favor. The popularity of the childhood game of “keeping shop” speaks volumes about this appeal. Among children, especially in France, the game of market takes precedence, where each child oversees a unique stall, filled with cleverly crafted replicas of fruits and vegetables made from wildflowers and leaves, all animatedly vying for attention in lively French banter, sprinkled with wit.

While most of us never venture into the reality of shop ownership, the idea often lingers in the back of our minds. A friend of mine has long dreamed of establishing a London hub for the most captivating and vivid European pottery. I’m convinced that, if she could gather the pieces and ensure a steady supply, she would find great success. Unfortunately, the likelihood of this dream coming to fruition is slim, as individuals who take action are few and far between. Another acquaintance is currently buzzing with plans for a restaurant in an area that seems particularly in need of such a venue, especially since it is mainly inhabited by flat dwellers. The catchy motto she’s considering is “Where to dine when the cook goes out,” but, like many ideas, it may ultimately remain just that—an idea.

At first glance, it seems that a shop launched in an area lacking that type of establishment would stand a better chance than one opening next door to a similar shop—barring any potential rivalry. Yet the intricacies of business are perplexing, revealing numerous, often contradictory, paths to success. One might initially assume that the principle of scientific selection and segregation is the most effective approach; however, the idea of congregation appears equally valid. For instance, a tailor may thrive as the sole provider on a street, while another can prosper even more by being one of many tailors in a tailor-heavy area. Additionally, the contrasting strategies of visibility versus discretion each have their merits. One hat maker may flourish within a grand, brightly lit store, while another thrives behind the grimy windows of an outdated shopfront. The dichotomy extends to the concepts of singularity and plurality as well, where one shopkeeper can achieve success with a single location, while another might build an empire with multiple outlets simultaneously.

Then there are those business owners who succeed despite seemingly operating without any customers. We’re all aware of shops that appear perpetually empty, while at the other end of the spectrum, there are successful businesses—such as Altman and Tiffany—who maintain their anonymity, confident in their established reputations.

But how does one choose the right line of business?

Once, a man with no formal training decided to pursue a career in London. He arrived in the city to explore his options, his mind open and ready to absorb possibilities. As he wandered, he noticed the abundance of antique shops near the British Museum and the South Kensington Museum. This led him to the revelation, previously unknown to him but recognized by dealers, that the atmosphere in these areas is charged with excitement, enticing visitors to take home a piece of the oddities they encounter. Unfortunately, his aspirations to set up a shop in London never materialized, as he was unable to secure a location for a monumental mason’s yard opposite Westminster Abbey.

If I were to ever delve into the world of shopkeeping, my primary desire would be to operate within a field that resonates with me. Some items are genuinely captivating to sell, whereas others are decidedly not. For instance, selling old books would seem ideal, yet I find that I would much prefer to keep them for myself. The same goes for artwork—how could I bear to part with a beautiful piece? Equally, allowing a customer to make the mistake of purchasing a poor-quality piece would be unbearable. A fruit shop might offer a pleasant environment, yet I firmly believe that fruit should be distributed freely rather than sold for profit. The tobacconist’s trade once offered a sophisticated and enjoyable experience, but that has changed. Today, the tobacconist is merely a cog in a colossal Trust, far removed from the intimate atmosphere of the past, where aficionados of tobacco could gather, discuss, and savor their craft—so different from the modern chemist’s shop, filled with seasonal gifts and photographs.

If that enterprising tobacconist, Mr. Godall, were to return to a London he once knew—one reminiscent of Baghdad—he would likely need to find a new kind of establishment. Perhaps he would opt for an oyster bar, reminiscent of the one near Leicester Square where a young man had once offered cream tarts. More likely, he would choose to open an American bar. However, if his true aim were to observe human nature at its most raw and impulsive—particularly at night—he might consider running a coffee stall. After 10 PM, the coffee stall owners become the most genuine companions to those wandering the streets late at night. There’s a coffee stall just a stone’s throw from my home, and no matter the hour I return, its owner is always lively and cheerful, serving the hottest beverages to restless patrons. The stall draws in a crowd of hungry revelers, often accompanied by soldiers and their partners. Everyone greets him by name, and the atmosphere is filled with laughter and conversation. Regardless of how late I wake from a troubling dream, the sound of voices and merriment from the coffee stall reassures me that the familiar world remains intact and that I haven’t drifted into some other realm. “Good,” I think to myself, “I am still here.” To be able to serve these night owls joyfully, providing them with comfort and stability in those late hours, would undoubtedly make one’s existence feel meaningful.
