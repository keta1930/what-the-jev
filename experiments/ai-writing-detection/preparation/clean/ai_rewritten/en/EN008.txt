A sharp, urgent knock resonates on my outer door, carrying a hint of timid hesitation, as if the person behind it were slightly apprehensive and ready to flee at any moment. I turn to glance at the clock. It reads four-forty. One of the odd side effects of my long tenure as a senior assistant at sea is the tendency to awaken around four in the morning. This gives me several hours, while on land, to reflect on my shortcomings, weaknesses, and, less frequently, my achievements and strengths. A man who rises at such an hour is often met with disdain on land. Family members express their sentiments with excessive vigor, and I must remain still to ponder my existence, or risk the disgrace of being asked when my next departure will be.

However, this morning, in these ancient chambers of a historic inn nestled in London's core, I have resolved to rise and venture out. The catalyst for this significant departure from my otherwise leisurely routine is a woman. "Cherchez la femme," as the French say with their characteristic logic. Fortunately, she is not difficult to find, as she stands just outside my solid oak door, tapping insistently with that same delicate urgency. Responding to this romantic call, I let out a low growl of agreement and begin my preparations. To ease the concerns of any reader worried about an impending elopement, I assure you that we are not embarking on any such reckless escapade. Our destination is merely a few blocks up the Strand, to Covent Garden Market, to witness the arrival of the city’s fresh produce.

Once I've hastily dressed, almost as rudely as a sailor preparing for watch, and having appeased the repeated tapping with brief acknowledgments of my progress, I grab my hat and cane. After unfastening the imposing antique bolts of my door, I find a young woman poised by the window, gazing out at the courtyard of the old inn. She is quite the assertive young lady, always devising what she calls "stunts" for her articles. That is her profession—writing for the press. Her other profession involves selling manuscripts, which creates the bond between us. For the standard agent's commission, she is currently representing one of my works. Being an unattached and somewhat unprotected male, she organizes little outings around London to keep me informed and entertained. Dressed in the vibrant attire of a London flower girl, she is here to gather material for a special article in a morning newspaper. With a spark of excitement in her bright black Irish eyes, she appears wholly focused on her task. As we comment on the beauty of the early summer morning in the city, we make our way down the stairs and out through the grand, ancient archway, proceeding leisurely west along the Strand.

To those who appreciate and understand the unique charm of London, the city always possesses a beauty all its own; however, at five o'clock on a summer morning, it exudes a youthful fragrance and fresh vitality that touches the soul. The newly washed streets gleam in the sunlight, resembling a path of "patines of bright gold." Early buses rumble by, having returned from their overnight stays. As we approach the new Gaiety Theatre, which boldly juts into the bustling traffic like a prominent Byzantine promontory, we observe Waterloo Bridge crowded with wagons heavily laden. From all directions, the great carts are arriving, passing Charing Cross, as they come from Paddington Terminal and the market-garden regions of Middlesex and Surrey. Down Wellington Street, carts filled with vegetables from Brentwood and Coggeshall roll by, alongside neat vans packed with crates of watercress harvested from the lush lowlands of Suffolk and Cambridgeshire. Behind us thunder large four-horse vehicles from the docks, carrying peaches from South Africa, potatoes from the Canary Islands, onions from France, apples from California, oranges from the West Indies, pineapples from Central America, grapes from Spain, and bananas from Colombia.

We turn through an archway behind a theatre, adjacent to the stage door of the Opera House. As we enter, the booths are quickly filling with fresh produce. Men in long alpaca coats, clutching substantial marbled notebooks, walk about with an air of importance. A towering pile of pumpkins rises behind a hill of cabbages. Strings of onions dangle from rails, and the heads of barrels are being knocked in, revealing purple grapes buried in corkdust. Pears and figs, cultivated under glass for affluent customers, rest in boxes lined with soft tissue. A broken crate of tangerine oranges spills its vibrant contents, creating a splash of ruddy gold on the wooden walkway. A wagon laden with beets rolls in, its broad wheels crushing through the soft fruit, filling the air with a heavy, sweet aroma.

We navigate through the stalls until we reach the flowers. A throng of ladies—some young, some middle-aged, all adorned in the same flamboyant attire I mentioned earlier—surrounds an almost overwhelming display of blooms. Roses currently dominate the scene, presenting a satisfying robustness and a glorious abundance that hardly seems credible in a commodity so easily enjoyed without ownership. I feel no urge to possess these vast collections of fragrant beauty; it would be akin to owning a harem, I imagine. Vivid patches of blue violets in round baskets and delicate eglantine in dainty boxes provide a striking contrast to the grand display of roses and the dew-kissed maiden-hair ferns nearby.

"And what are those things, then?" my companion inquires, momentarily distracted from the flowers. She gestures toward a pile of dull-green items stacked on mats or being unloaded from large vans. As a Cockney, she expresses surprise when I explain that those are bananas. She shrugs and turns back to the musk-roses, quickly forgetting the bananas. For me, however, the sharp, penetrating scent of the green fruit cuts through the heavy perfume of the flowers and evokes vivid memories. I envision farms in distant Colombia or possibly Costa Rica. There's nothing quite like a smell to awaken the past. I picture the wooden pier and the line of rickety open-sided cars rattling into the dim shed, pushed by a noisy, squealing locomotive. I see boys napping between shifts, their large straw hats shielding their faces as they sprawl on the ground. In the distance, the blue mountains rise, and behind them lies the still blue sea. I hear the whine of the elevators, the monotonous click of counters, and the loud arguments of the locals. I can feel the heat of the tropical day and see the glimmer of white waves crashing upon yellow sands beneath tall palms. I remember the mysterious, impenetrable solitude of the jungle, alive with a never-ending battle of winged and crawling creatures, if one knows how to perceive it. While my companion diligently collects material for her article about the Market, I deftly step aside to avoid a swarthy gentleman from Calabria, who, with his two-wheeled barrow, serves as the final link in the vast transportation chain connecting the remote farmer in tropical lands to the Cockney pedestrian pausing on the sidewalk to purchase a banana for a few pence.
