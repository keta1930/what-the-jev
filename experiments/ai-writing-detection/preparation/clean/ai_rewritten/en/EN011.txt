Mr. Asquith remarked recently, "I have devoted a significant part of my life to advising businessmen on how to extricate themselves from their troubles." This comment was prompted by a group of individuals who were presenting him with the usual complaints about lawyers and the supposed necessity for "businessmen" to take charge of governance. Throughout this war, one of the most tiresome assertions has been the notion of a "business government" and the incessant criticism directed at lawyers.

Personally, I am not a lawyer and do not hold any particular fondness for the profession. I tend to steer clear of their services whenever possible. However, to dismiss lawyers outright as a group is as misguided as claiming that a successful grocer or tailor possesses the qualities of a great statesman simply because of their commercial success. The responsibilities of managing an empire are far more complex than running a grocery store, and it is naive to think that someone who has thrived in the business of selling provisions can automatically govern a nation with insight and wisdom. Take, for example, the most notable grocers of our time—Lord Devonport and Sir Thomas Lipton. Both are undoubtedly commendable individuals, yet would we genuinely consider either of them suitable for the role of Prime Minister? I doubt it.

A great statesman must demonstrate his capabilities through the application of statesmanship, especially under the intense scrutiny of the public eye. While a grocer might make a significant error in his trade without widespread notice, a statesman’s missteps are immediately apparent to all. A statesman must rise to the occasion in the most transparent of conflicts, and those who achieve prominence have invariably proven their mettle through rigorous challenges. This scrutiny does not spare lawyers; Mr. Asquith faced the same trials as Mr. Chamberlain, who was a nail maker, or Mr. Balfour, a landowner. No one derided Mr. Chamberlain as merely a "nailmaker" or Mr. Balfour as simply a "landlord" without adequately addressing their qualifications. So why assume that throwing the term "lawyer" at Mr. Asquith or Mr. Lloyd George suffices to dismiss their abilities?

Is there a belief that lawyers possess a greater self-interest than people in other professions, such as brewers, soap manufacturers, or bankers? I find that hard to believe. Lawyers are representative of the general populace, comprising both virtuous and unscrupulous individuals. Judge Jeffreys was indeed a notorious figure, yet it was the lawyers of the seventeenth century who played a crucial role in preserving the liberties we enjoy today. Think of Abraham Lincoln—a more altruistic and wise individual may never have existed, and he was, after all, a lawyer. Furthermore, it’s worth noting that Mr. Asquith himself has made significant personal sacrifices in politics. He relinquished a legal practice that earned him approximately £10,000 annually, a sum that would have increased had he committed fully to it. Instead, he chose a role that has consistently been more burdensome and never provided him more than £5,000. He could have aspired to the position of Lord Chancellor, enjoying a comfortable life on the Woolsack, yet he opted to face the daily challenges in the House of Commons, where he became the target of every disgruntled office-seeker. Some might argue that he sought glory, but I would argue otherwise. As Danton once expressed, I would prefer to tend sheep on a hillside rather than involve myself in the governance of men, which can be an incredibly thankless profession. Despite any flaws one might attribute to Mr. Asquith, an insatiable desire for glory is certainly not among them. You could observe a far greater thirst for recognition in Mr. Churchill within a mere few moments than you would find in Mr. Asquith over five years, and Churchill is not a lawyer.

Nonetheless, the underlying disdain for lawyers does have historical roots. This is not a new phenomenon. Recall Samuel Johnson’s quip when asked about a departed individual: "I dislike making disparaging remarks about someone who isn’t present; however, I believe he is an attorney." Similarly, Carlyle was not much kinder, characterizing barristers as “loaded blunderbusses”—if you paid him, he would obliterate your opponent; if your opponent paid him, he would turn against you. A lawyer’s weapon may be the law, yet their objective is not necessarily justice. Often, the more adept a lawyer is, the more adept they become at circumventing justice. This detachment from the substance of a case and the conscious rejection of moral principles in their professional dealings creates a lingering suspicion around lawyers. They might argue that it is their duty to advocate for their client, while their opponent is tasked with doing the same for theirs, leaving the judge and jury to ensure justice prevails. While that is indeed true, it doesn’t eliminate the skepticism surrounding someone who passionately represents both the guilty and the innocent.

Moreover, lawyers operate in a field that can be quite convoluted. Consider Sancho Panza, who administered justice as Governor of the Island of Barataria; had he been Governor of Britain, he would have enforced the law, and his judgments would have varied significantly. The relationship between law and justice is akin to that between grammar and Shakespeare. If Shakespeare were judged by grammarians, he would likely face condemnation as a rogue and vagabond; similarly, justice frequently suffers at the hands of lawyers. While we recognize the necessity of law, much like grammar, we do not hold an inherent affection for either. They are often perceived as sterile, lifeless disciplines, and those who engage with them can appear suspect. One could be the most unscrupulous individual of their era, yet if one adheres strictly to the law, the lance of justice may never touch them. Law, which ought to serve justice, can often betray its intended purpose.

However, it would be unfair to overlook the progress we have made. Today, the law serves as a more effective instrument of justice than it has in the past, and this improvement is largely attributable to the efforts of distinguished lawyers. While there may be figures reminiscent of Dodson and Fogg in the legal field, there also exist honorable individuals like Pyms and Pratts, who have valiantly defended the liberties of our nation against tyrannical rulers and subservient parliaments.
