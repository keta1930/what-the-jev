The newly created chair focusing on the history, literature, and institutions of the United States, which will be shared among various universities in Great Britain, marks a significant departure from the previous exchange professorships that often yielded mixed results. The intent is not to send an American professor annually to Britain to instill them with the “true” culture from its source. Instead, the individual holding this chair will likely be British as often as they are American, and may very well be a public figure rather than an academic. The primary goal is to foster a deeper understanding of the United States in England, and such an aspiration is indeed noble. The ongoing peace and prosperity in the world hinges significantly on how well England comprehends America.

It is a misconception to believe that American understanding of British culture is on par with British understanding of American life. The British Empire represents a substantial and relatively straightforward reality, one that has been in the global spotlight for an extended period. Conversely, the United States, until quite recently, was viewed by the British as a minor entity, albeit a far more intricate one than they assumed. Both sides are acutely aware of each other’s shortcomings, judged through a distinctly familial lens. Americans tend to boast in a nasal tone, while Britons often adopt a condescending manner. Regardless of which nation might emerge victorious in the tumultuous world stage, England ensured its own survival; the American, on the other hand, often settled for the less dignified victories found in commerce, willing to compromise quality of life for financial gain. Nonetheless, Americans have long acknowledged and appreciated the strengths of English political systems, the allure of English living, and the Empire's significant role in fostering freedom and civilization globally. This acknowledgment, however, appears to be reciprocated only by a select few notable British individuals who demonstrate a genuine admiration for America.

To a considerable degree, this lack of mutual appreciation can be attributed to America’s own shortcomings. The nation does not present a narrative that is easily deciphered. It is evident—and so plainly that it is often overlooked—that America is bound to an English heritage. English law forms the foundation of American jurisprudence. The English language, while distinct, is fundamentally similar, as even philologists acknowledge the relatively minor differences. The literary canon of America is deeply rooted in English tradition—Chaucer and Shakespeare are as much part of America’s literary fabric as they are of England's, for the shared ancestry flows through the veins of both. The turmoil of the thirteenth century that birthed law and parliament in England is mirrored in America’s own historical struggles, providing a sense of pride in the realization that certain foundational achievements need not be repeated, even as we recognize the potential for ongoing improvement. Americans, like the British, acknowledge that their independence stemmed largely from a war fueled by misguided economic theories, yet the resolution of this conflict imparted invaluable lessons in empire management—lessons that the British have adeptly learned for the collective benefit of all. The establishment of American independence, once achieved, paved the way for a democratic freedom that England was quick to embrace. We are aware of these connections, yet—

Yet, somehow, we allow these fundamental truths to become obscured. We permit the failure of England to resolve the seemingly insurmountable Irish question to foster attitudes among some Americans that are understandable for certain Irish individuals but wholly unjustifiable for any American. We allow ourselves to be swayed by an emotional attachment to an immigrant from Eastern Europe, who arrives with a worldview shaped by circumstances long since passed in English-speaking nations, leading us to mistakenly perceive them as the embodiment of America’s foundational ideals. We too readily accept the rationale of “He is not pro-German; he simply opposes British policies.” However, we must question: why is there such anti-British sentiment? Why should we allow it to appear as though recognizing the English roots of America equates to endorsing every action taken by England throughout its history? Why should dissent against specific British actions lead to a wholesale rejection of the core principles that define who we are? From the very beginning, America has been an integral part of the grand English experiment—one that is significant for its capacity to learn through experience.

The international community has invested heavily, in terms of both blood and resources, in the education of England. The world’s collective response to Germany’s audacious challenge demonstrates that this investment has been well-placed. England stands as the nation best equipped to exercise the power it possesses. This assertion does not dismiss the unique strengths of other nations; indeed, these distinct qualities have played a vital role in shaping England’s journey. Italy, France, the United States, and even Germany have all contributed to this educational endeavor. While England may not be flawless—her own citizens would likely be the first to acknowledge this—she has gleaned much from her experiences and maintained her steadiness throughout the process. Educating another nation to such a degree is a daunting task, one that the world may hesitate to undertake again. The likelihood of achieving similar results elsewhere is slim, as what England offers the globe extends beyond her institutions; it encompasses the English freeman, who can come from Canada, Australia, Africa, or the farthest reaches of the ocean.

This freeman is remarkably adaptable, excelling in various roles across the globe and exhibiting an extraordinary ability to assimilate. Reflect on his origins: he began by fully integrating the Danes into his identity while retaining the essence of an English man. He subsequently accepted a Norman king, just as he had with his Danish predecessor, with the hope that this ruler would not impose too many burdens upon him. However, when the uninformed Norman William exterminated many of his natural leaders and subjugated the remainder, how did this Englishman manage, within a span of two centuries, to elevate himself and his fellow adventurers into the status of English gentlemen? How did he transform a motley group of exiles fleeing ecclesiastical oppression, broken younger sons, unemployed artisans, speculators, and assorted immigrants into the foundation of America? Is this individual likely to lose his sense of purpose when faced with the age-old dilemmas that resurface? This is a challenge he has been navigating successfully for over a millennium. Allow him the opportunity to rediscover his identity in the context of these challenges. Do not expect him to pause and articulate a coherent rationale for his actions; he would not be a true adherent of the English tradition if he could do so. Perhaps those who take on the responsibilities of the new chair can assist him in articulating this journey.
