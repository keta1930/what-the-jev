A distinguished naval officer once posed a question to me regarding the origins of Davy Jones, leaving me speechless and embarrassed. The embarrassment should have belonged to him, given his position within the Admiralty, where knowledge of maritime lore is expected. However, the whims of circumstance found me, a mere literary figure, feeling the weight of that moment.

With my head bowed, I resolved that upon my return to London and access to books, I would uncover the truth. But have I truly discovered it? That is for you to determine.

I commenced my inquiry with a "Glossary of Sea Terms," which offered a slick explanation regarding Davy Jones's locker but failed to address its etymology. I then progressed to "The Oxford Dictionary," where the definition was presented with greater clarity, including guidance on the pronunciation of Davy’s name. Many might presume that it is pronounced as it appears—simply Davy—but the late Dr. Murray had a different perspective. The correct pronunciation is Dē.vi. After navigating this confusion, the Dictionary elaborated on nautical slang, defining Davy Jones as “the spirit of the sea, the sailor's devil,” and described Davy Jones’s locker as the ocean, particularly as a grave for those who meet their demise at sea. Among the references was Smollett’s "Peregrine Pickle," along with one J. Willock, whom I will revisit later.

Determined to trace the origin of Davy Jones, I turned to "The Dictionary of National Biography," which, despite its limitations in being unavailable for casual reading ashore, remains an invaluable resource—arguably more essential than the Bible or works by Boswell, Plato, and others, should one find themselves stranded on a desert island. To my surprise, I discovered eight individuals named David Jones, all of Welsh descent; yet none could be directly linked to the legendary figure in question. Among them were hymn-writers, a revivalist, a soldier and translator, a barrister, a missionary to Madagascar (the only one with any maritime experience), and a fervent preacher whose life challenges turned his hair from coal-black to white overnight—an eventuality mine seemed destined to follow. 

Reflecting on my approach, it occurred to me that I should have consulted "Notes and Queries" first, where I was promptly rewarded. This publication had addressed the very question back in 1851. On June 14, that year, a Mr. Henry Campkin sought assistance from the periodical, which, given its nautical motto courtesy of Captain Cuttle, should have welcomed inquiries of this nature. Unlike my Admiralty acquaintance, Mr. Campkin did not bluntly inquire, "Who the devil was Davy Jones?" Instead, he posed the question in a gentlemanly fashion, asking, "Who was the significant individual whose name has become such a powerful myth? And what led to the ocean being identified with the locker of this enigmatic figure?"

Mr. Campkin's request was answered remarkably quickly—much faster than one might expect today. By June 21, Mr. Pemberton, the only reader of "Notes and Queries" to rise to the occasion, provided an answer that would serve as a cornerstone for our understanding. As a seafarer himself, Mr. Pemberton had pondered the question at length and proposed that the name Davy Jones might have originated from the biblical prophet Jonah (who, notably, was not Welsh but an Israelite). While Jonah was not precisely a sailor, his own seafaring exploits are well-documented; in his prayer, he reflects upon his experiences with the ocean: "The waters compassed me about... the depth closed me round about; the weeds were wrapped about my head," and so forth. Thus, Pemberton posited, the sea might be fittingly referred to as Jonah's locker by an unrefined mariner, and the name Jonah could naturally have morphed into Jones. Given that many Welshmen (including Mr. Lloyd George, from whom we derive stirring oratory) are often named Davy, it follows that "Davy Jones" would eventually emerge. This theory from Mr. Pemberton appears to be the sole explanation I have encountered, though I suspect Mrs. Gamp might lend her support—albeit with a humorous twist, preferring a different term for "locker" that comic military poets often rhyme with "réveillé."

The more one contemplates this theory, the more plausible it becomes. As Mr. Pemberton might have elaborated, there is further evidence linking Jonah and Jones through the fish that swallowed the prophet but subsequently did not retain him. For a skilled dialectician, the connection between whales and Wales would present no challenge. Later, I discovered that Dr. Brewer, in "The Dictionary of Phrase and Fable," fervently endorses the Jonah connection, but he unnecessarily posits that "Davy" derives from "duffy," a spirit from the West Indies. Thus, according to his interpretation, Davy Jones's locker becomes Duffy Jonah's locker—essentially the seabed, or the place where sailors intended to send Jonah. The resulting confusion is rather comical: first, a man of God cast overboard by the crew; second, a divine fish sent to rescue him; and third, the misuse of the prophet’s name to denote the sailor’s devil, with Jonah becoming an unwitting ruler of the very element he had reason to despise. This is how myths take shape.

And so much for Davy Jones. Yet, J. Willock, another author referenced by "The Oxford Dictionary," leads us into yet another enigma. In one of his voyages, he mentions: "The great bugbear of the ocean is Davie Jones. At the crossing of the line, they shout that Davie Jones and his wife are coming aboard...."

"And his wife!"

As for the identity of Mrs. Davy Jones, I shall refrain from delving into that mystery—regardless of any orders from the Board of Admiralty.
