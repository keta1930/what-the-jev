Not long ago, I had the peculiar fortune of encountering once more the renowned prophet known as Old Moore, whose cautious predictions can be found in the most unexpected places, including the streets. To my surprise, he did not recognize me. While it is not uncommon for people to forget others, it struck me as particularly odd that someone with such extraordinary foresight could fail to recall me, especially since I recognized him immediately. Perhaps the years spent peering intently into the murky future had dulled his ability to look back. There we stood, with me urging him to remember our past connection, while he remained oblivious. This fleeting moment of superiority over a man who, despite the continual failure of events to conform to his predictions, foresees so much, filled me with a sense of triumph. Yet, the fickleness of memory is a constant reminder of how foolish it is to boast about such moments.

Memory, indeed, is one of the most perplexing marvels of our human condition. The sheer existence of memory—a miracle of the brain—is far beyond our limited understanding, much like the vast expanse of the cosmos. Contemplating this is a daunting task. However, the whimsical quirks of memory are worth our attention. The irrationality of our memory system, with its arbitrary selection of what becomes permanent, raises questions. Why must our memories be subject to a downward pull, urging us to battle for the preservation of our finest recollections? It seems that at the inception of our memories, it would have been equally feasible to instill an upward momentum. Yet, rather than retaining our best memories, we find that as we age, our recollections become increasingly inaccurate, and we often lose far more than we retain.

Take my own experience as an example. Once, I could effortlessly recite lengthy excerpts from esteemed poets, as well as those less celebrated but deeply cherished, like Matthew Arnold and William Cory. Now, I find solace in reciting a Bab Ballad to lull myself to sleep. That whimsical piece never fails me, yet I struggle to remember the correct order of even two stanzas from Omar Khayyám's "Rubáiyát," a poem I once knew entirely. I would much prefer to have retained the wistful verses of the Persian poet over Gilbert's light-hearted "Etiquette," but alas, the whims of memory had different plans.

Any prose that I had previously committed to memory has gradually slipped away, lacking the rhyme or rhythm that aids retention. Yet, one particular sentence, which was never entirely mine to begin with, frequently dances before my inner eye. It comes from a story by Mr. Kipling, about a rebellious elephant who refuses to work due to the prolonged absence of his master. This master, Dheesa, having taken leave for a trip, overstays his welcome. The phrase that persistently echoes in my mind is: "Dheesa had vagabonded along the roads till he met a marriage procession of his own caste, and drinking, dancing, and tippling, had drifted past all knowledge of the lapse of time." Out of the countless books I have read and can only vaguely recall, it is remarkable that this single sentence is so vividly etched in my memory.

In my past, I was well-versed in numerous psalms; however, those have faded. Curiously, I have held onto a rather ludicrous exchange from a book titled "The World of Wit and Humour," which I studied many years ago:

"Father, I have spilt the butter. What shall I do?"
"Rub it briskly with a woollen fabric."
"Why?"
"Because friction generates caloric, which volatises the oleaginous particles of the stearine matter."

—And yet, I once knew many psalms.

One of the strange aspects of what we refer to as memory loss is its contagious nature. How often does one person forget a name that is well-known to him, only to have his companion, equally familiar with it, also draw a blank? This odd phenomenon was highlighted recently when I needed to remember the name of a not-so-famous but talented repertory actor in order to send him a letter. Although I could not recall his name, I managed to describe him and his work well enough for my companions to recognize him. We could even discuss the roles we had seen him play and praise his skills, yet none of us could retrieve his name in that moment. What was at play here? We all knew it, yet we collectively failed to remember it.

We left, determined to uncover this elusive information, and my last wise contribution was that I believed his first name was Joseph and his last name started with a P. When we reconvened the following day, each of us had suddenly remembered the name. It seemed to have dawned on us during the night. Knowing that the actor's name was Michael Sherbrooke made the realization particularly satisfying for me. If I am not recognized by my companions as Mrs. Nickleby, it is merely because of their kindness.

The greatest enigma lies in the question: where do the forgotten items reside when they slip from our minds, only to resurface unexpectedly? Where do they hide? This concern about their elusive presence weighs on me far more than the inability to summon them from the depths of my brain. Are they not actually there? Do they perhaps take breaks, stepping out for lunches or evenings off, which causes us to miss them? Is there a vast, external area of the mind from which we must retrieve facts, much like waiting for old newspapers to be brought from Hendon at the British Museum? The fact that these memories often return, eventually and without fail, lends some credence to these speculations.
