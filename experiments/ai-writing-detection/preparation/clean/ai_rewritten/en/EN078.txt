Those who dismiss the existence of deities often claim that a profound love for humanity would suffice in place of faith. In theory, this sentiment might hold water, but it seems elusive for those who espouse it. The genuine affection for humankind is a tangible emotion, predominantly found among what society tends to label as the uneducated. Conversely, it appears conspicuously absent in the very circles that discuss it most fervently.

For instance, one can observe a striking joy in simply being with others during public holidays, particularly among the masses. This communal spirit draws them closer to a state of bliss, despite the chaotic scenes they often present.

I recall a vivid scene at a rural train station where a group of factory girls boarded an empty train. About twenty of them chose to crowd into a single carriage, leaving the rest of the train entirely vacant. This act exemplified a genuine love for humanity—a tangible joy found in simply being near one’s peers. The intense, visceral connection they shared starkly contrasts with the sterile, theoretical love proposed by so-called rationalistic idealists.

The sheer exuberance that erupted when that train departed remains etched in my memory; countless factory girls who couldn’t find seats expressed their delight by jumping and cheering. Such unrestrained joy is something I have never witnessed among modern philosophers or idealists. You wouldn’t catch a group of rational thinkers cramming themselves into a third-class carriage just for the thrill of shared company, nor would you find them bouncing up and down in celebration.

Some express concerns that tourists will overwhelm the serene beauty of locations like Hampstead or Burnham Beeches. However, this fear seems unfounded; tourists tend to favor the company of their own. They have a fervent need to gather in groups, reveling in their collective experiences.

Turning to a more mundane yet equally telling aspect of this principle, I feel compelled to address the role of the barber. Before any contemporary figure claims to love humanity genuinely, I contend that he should first demonstrate a genuine pleasure when his barber engages him in conversation. This barber epitomizes humanity; one must learn to appreciate that connection. If a person cannot foster affection for the barber—someone he sees regularly—how can he possibly extend that love to those he has never met, such as individuals from Japan?

Critics often point to the barber’s tendency to initiate discussions about the weather. However, this is a common conversational starter among all social classes, including dukes and diplomats, albeit with a tone of feigned boredom. The barber, on the other hand, speaks on such topics with an infectious enthusiasm that is hard to replicate. He may point out to a client that they are thinning on top, but this very honesty should be regarded as a virtue rather than a flaw. His candidness arises from his expertise; though he earns a living through his trade, he remains a sincere specialist.

The only true measure of these interactions is through experience. To illustrate this, I’ll recount a conversation I had with a barber not long ago—I assure you, it was quite real, even if I can’t recall the exact words exchanged.

I had received an invitation to a gathering intended for Colonial Premiers and, in order to avoid any misconceptions about my appearance, I decided to stop at a barber shop in the Strand for a shave. While enduring the somewhat uncomfortable process, the barber remarked:

“There’s quite a bit of buzz in the papers about this new shaving technique, sir. They say you can shave yourself with anything—from a stick or a stone to a poker.” At that moment, I sensed a hint of sarcasm in his tone. “You could even use a shovel or a—”

He faltered, searching for more creative suggestions, and despite my ignorance of the topic, I eagerly jumped in.

“Or a button-hook,” I suggested, “or perhaps a blunderbuss or a battering-ram or even a piston-rod—”

He picked up where I left off, his enthusiasm reignited. “Or a curtain rod, or a candlestick, or—”

“Cow-catcher!” I chimed in, and we continued this lively exchange for quite some time. Eventually, I inquired about the whole idea, and he explained it to me in an eloquent and detailed manner.

“The amusing part,” he noted, “is that this concept isn’t actually new. People have pondered alternatives to razors for ages. There’s always been speculation about how to shave without a razor, but none of those ideas ever materialized, and I doubt this one will either.”

“Well,” I responded, slowly rising from the chair while trying to put my coat on inside out, “I can’t speak to the validity of your new shaving method. Shaving, while significant, is a rather trivial matter in the grand scheme of things. Yet, your comments resonate with me on a deeper level. They remind me of something more profound. You see, the human race continuously seeks shortcuts to simplify life, but what they gain in ease they often relinquish in other areas.”

I continued, “If one person manages to avoid the labor of preparing for a shave, then surely another must bear the burden of creating an alternative for that process. It would be fantastic if we could achieve a clean shave without inconveniencing anyone, and even better if we could skip shaving altogether without causing discomfort to others.”

“‘But, dear barber of the Strand, neither you nor I have crafted the world we inhabit,'” I mused.

“Whoever designed it—presumably someone wiser and better than ourselves—did so under peculiar constraints and with a blend of pleasure and pain.

“In its earliest texts, it’s unmistakably stated that one cannot both consume and retain one’s cake. Regardless of how much discourse occurs, it remains true that a man who has discarded his razor cannot utilize it. Periodically, people rise with notions of new solutions and proclaim that everything can be achieved without sacrifice, that what is deemed bad is actually good if only one is enlightened, and that the distinction between being shaved and unshaved is merely a matter of perspective. They argue that the essence of shaving exists within all men, and that every basic tool is a potential razor. Those superstitious individuals of the past may have viewed facial hair as a definitive trait, but enlightened thinkers now argue that it is merely an absence of something else. Bristles are seen as a void where a clean shave ought to be.

“Life continues on its course, and I’m sure there’s meaning in it all. But a child embodies the Kingdom of God, and the moment you attempt to kiss a baby, they will know whether or not you are clean-shaven. Perhaps I’m confusing the concepts of being shaved with being saved; my democratic inclinations often lead me to drop my ‘h’s.’ Before long, I may even assert that goats symbolize the lost due to their long beards. This is becoming overly metaphorical.

“Nevertheless,” I concluded as I settled the bill, “I found our discussion about the New Shaving incredibly intriguing. Have you ever come across the concept of New theology?”

The barber smiled, admitting that he had yet to encounter such a notion.
