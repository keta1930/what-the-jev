The rain fell steadily as Victor Crummles stepped out onto the pavement. However, the weather was the least of his concerns. He raised his umbrella instinctively, not truly registering the action. His thoughts were consumed by the daunting task ahead. “I’m really in a bind,” he reminded himself, glancing at the piece of paper in his pocket that indicated where and when he was due for a medical examination. Reassuring himself, he confirmed its presence: Kilburn. Twelve o’clock.

Indeed, he found himself in quite a predicament. But he quickly reassured himself that he didn’t mind it at all. His patriotism ran deep, and he felt a strong desire to contribute to the war effort. He longed to take on the Germans and show them what he was made of. If only he had been younger, he thought; he would have enlisted without hesitation. But at thirty-eight, he felt the weight of his age.

As he approached the “Golden Crown,” he considered stopping for “just one.” A drink would bolster his spirits and prepare him for the doctors. After all, he believed he ought to give them a fair chance, regardless of the potential ramifications for himself. A whisky-and-soda would surely help him feel "in the pink."

With the drink consumed, he felt a surge of confidence. It was time to make his way to Kilburn. He contemplated his travel options. It was at least a two-mile journey—plenty of distance to cover. He could catch the bus, or take the Tube, but walking seemed the best choice in the end. There was time to spare. Besides, he needed to consider his varicose vein. The doctors should be informed; it wouldn’t be right to conceal such information from them. Varicose veins were serious conditions, and he had done his research. He could recall the definition clearly:

“The dilation and thickening of veins, accompanied by lengthening and tortuosity, leading to the formation of knots or knobs, where blood coagulates, fibrin deposits, and sometimes even bone matter develops; additionally, the vein walls are diseased.”

This was no trivial matter. Many men had been disqualified due to similar issues, and it was only fair the doctors were made aware of his condition. He certainly noticed those knots when he exerted himself. Who could say if there was “fibrin” or “osseous matter” involved? Regardless, it was imperative that the doctors see his leg in its true condition.

He believed it was wrong to let patriotism cloud his honesty. He felt a moral duty to avoid deceiving his country, especially considering the financial resources that would be wasted on someone who might falter early in service. He wanted to fight for his nation as much as anyone else, but he also understood the responsibility he bore. It was essential to prioritize the country’s interests over his own desires. 

Yes, he decided, it was his obligation to walk. It was crucial that he disclose the details of his varicose vein. He hoped it wouldn’t be a disqualifying factor, but he was committed to being forthright no matter the outcome. He wouldn’t misrepresent himself; he would ensure that the doctor fully understood the state of his leg.

When Victor finally entered the examination room, fatigue washed over him, but he steeled himself. He had indulged in another drink at the “Spread Eagle” and felt ready to face whatever awaited him. The room was occupied by two doctors—one seated at a desk, the other standing near a window.

“Anything bothering you?” the seated doctor inquired.

“Not that I’m aware of,” Victor replied, adopting a serious demeanor. However, he begrudgingly added, “I do have a bit of a varicose vein, but it’s hardly worth mentioning.”

“Oh, don’t worry about that,” the doctor assured him. “We’ve moved past that point. Now, strip.”

“Don’t worry about that? Moved past that point?” Victor pondered the implications. He had done his duty by mentioning the condition. If there was indeed fibrin or osseous matter in his veins, he had provided ample warning. It was the nation that would bear the consequences. Doctors could be so dismissive sometimes.

“Stripped? Alright, let’s take a look at you,” the doctor commanded.

The examination commenced, and Victor wondered if his varicose vein might actually astonish the doctor. He had walked two miles, and it should be noticeable—though he didn’t necessarily wish for it to be. If his condition was significant, it was only fair that the doctors were informed.

“What did you say your age was?” the doctor asked.

“Thirty-eight, sir,” Victor replied.

“Thirty-eight! Thirty-eight… um… Jeffkins!” the doctor called to his colleague, who joined him in assessing Victor. They appeared to disregard his leg entirely. If they overlooked it, he wouldn’t be responsible if something went awry.

“You can get dressed,” the first doctor announced before stepping aside with Jeffkins to whisper to each other.

After a moment, the first doctor returned. “Well, my man, it won’t do,” he declared. “We admire your spirit… very commendable, indeed. But (laughing) thirty-eight? Come now.”

A realization struck Victor. Was he truly being rejected? And for being too old? The thought filled him with shame and frustration, especially since he was eager to fight the enemy.

“But upon my word…” he began earnestly.

“There, there, we understand,” the doctor replied. “You’ve done your best, and it’s commendable. But thirty-eight! Really now… Good morning.”

Once outside, Victor was engulfed by a tumult of emotions that he could not endure alone. He turned back toward the “Spread Eagle,” seeking solace in the familiar surroundings as he grappled with the bitter disappointment of his rejection.
