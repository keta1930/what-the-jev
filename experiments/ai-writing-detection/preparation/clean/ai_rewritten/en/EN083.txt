Having secured a sleeping compartment, I promptly made my way to the conductor with coins in hand, as one does in such situations—though often to one’s own dismay—to inquire if I might have a compartment to myself, thus avoiding any unwelcome company.

Unfortunately, my request was denied.

Next, I proceeded to my allocated compartment to assess the situation—specifically, to determine whether I would be positioned above or below the person who would be sharing the space with me, inevitably some rather unpleasant individual.

Upon arrival, I found my companion already present, and we exchanged the stony, unyielding look that English travelers often reserve for each other in a sleeper car.

It was a relief to discover that he had drawn the dreaded upper berth, which allowed me to ease up a bit. Eventually, we engaged in polite exchanges—discussing the use of hat racks and storage areas, and making amicable agreements regarding the lighting, which I could control from my lower berth. By the time morning arrived, we had warmed to each other enough that he felt comfortable sharing his significant grievance about Paris.

This grievance, which he believed everyone ought to hear, revolved around the rampant distribution of counterfeit coins in the so-called City of Light and the locals’ propensity to foist them upon unsuspecting tourists. According to him, it was simply unimaginable that foreigners, particularly the English, should face such a lack of hospitality. Yet it was the English who often fell prey to those holding outdated francs, five-franc pieces lacking their rightful silver, and ten-franc coins devoid of gold.

He had been in Paris for only a short while when, as he recounted his tale, I realized I should narrate it as he described it to me.

“I’m sure you have your own stories about Paris,” he began, “but I’ve found myself victimized at every turn.”

As he rose to prepare himself, I relaxed in my berth, observing his struggles in the cramped space and feeling a twinge of disdain for his undershirt.

“Just a few hours after arriving in Paris,” he continued, “I needed to pay a cab driver. I offered him a franc. He scrutinized it, laughed, and handed it back. I tried again with another coin, only to receive the same response. After finally locating some legitimate currency to dismiss him, I sat down at a café, trying to trace back where I had received these worthless coins. Much of my week in Paris involved this sort of endeavor.”

He sighed as he pulled on his trousers, revealing red suspenders.

“I showed some of the bad francs to a waiter,” he recounted. “He reacted just like the cab driver—he laughed. In fact, few things provoke Parisian amusement quite like a counterfeit coin. Everyone I showed my collection to found it hilarious.” His expression darkened with anger. “This indifferent attitude towards what is, in essence, a complete lack of integrity in dealings between people,” he asserted, “shows just how far the Parisians have descended into cynicism.”

I found myself agreeing with his sentiment.

“The waiter sifted through my money, identifying which coins were valid and which were either counterfeit or no longer in circulation. He even called over other waiters to share in the joke. In the span of just four hours, I had accumulated three counterfeit francs, one fake two-franc piece, and two phony five-francs. I tucked them away into another pocket and obtained fresh change from him, only to find that it contained one obsolete five-franc piece and two more discredited francs. The cycle continued, and I became a perpetual target.”

At this point, he began to wash up, interrupting the flow of his narrative.

When he returned, I inquired why he didn’t always check his change.

“It’s tough to remember to do that,” he explained, “and besides, I’m no expert. Things only got worse, and when I received a counterfeit gold piece, I realized I had to take action. So I wrote to the Chief of Police.”

“In French?” I asked, intrigued.

“No, in English—the language of integrity. I detailed my experiences and mentioned that other English travelers had faced similar troubles. I urged him, as a matter of civic pride— esprit de pays —to do everything in his power to rid Paris of this problem. I noted that the waiters seemed to be the biggest culprits.”

“Did you receive a response?” I probed further.

“Not yet,” he said, having finished his grooming and allowing me space to sit up.

Later, while I encountered him in the dining car, I asked if he could show me his collection of counterfeit coins. I wanted to see firsthand what these coins looked like.

“I don’t have them any longer,” he replied.

“Oh, you must have sent them to the Chief of Police with your letter?” I suggested.

“No, actually, I didn’t,” he admitted. “The truth is—well—let's just say I managed to pass them all off again.”
