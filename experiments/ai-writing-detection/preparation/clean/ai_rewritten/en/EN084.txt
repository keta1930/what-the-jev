As I strolled down the Strand, a sudden downpour caught me off guard, yet I chose not to open my umbrella. In truth, I found myself unable to do so. The mechanism was faulty, and even if it had been functional, I would have still refrained from using it; I wouldn’t be caught under such a ridiculous excuse for an umbrella any more than Falstaff would parade through Coventry leading his ragtag band of misfits. The irony is that this umbrella doesn’t belong to me; it belongs to someone else, and I can only hope they read this account. They've taken my silk umbrella, and in return, I have their cotton one. I can picture them swaggering along the Strand beneath my umbrella, casting a disdainful look at the poor soul stuck with their pitiful excuse for a shield against the rain. I imagine them chuckling to themselves, thinking, “I really got the better of you this time. That thing of yours? It won’t open at all. And it collapses like a sack. But this umbrella…”

Yet, I digress from his unseemly ruminations. He is the type of person I would classify as having an “umbrella conscience.” You know the sort I refer to—individuals who would never dream of dipping their hands into someone else’s pocket, forging checks, or robbing a cash register, even if the opportunity arose. However, they might casually swap umbrellas, neglect to return borrowed books, or take advantage of the railway company. Ultimately, they consider themselves thoroughly honest, albeit with a certain flexibility regarding their moral code. Perhaps they pick up a random umbrella from a barber's stand, convinced that it can’t possibly be worse than their own; they might even find it to be an improvement. They won’t scrutinize it closely until they’ve gotten quite far along, at which point they’ll exclaim, “Goodness! I’ve taken the wrong umbrella,” feigning surprise, as if they genuinely believe they’ve made an innocent mistake. “Well, there’s no point in going back now. That person is long gone, and I’ve left them my own!”

This reflects how we engage in a game of hide-and-seek with our conscience. Simply avoiding detection by others isn’t enough; we also evade self-recognition. Even those who appear to be paragons of virtue can be afflicted by what I term “umbrella morals.” A well-known preacher, for example, was discovered deceased in a first-class railway carriage, his pocket containing a third-class ticket.

When it comes to books, the moral landscape is even murkier. I recall a notable library belonging to a deceased divine and literary critic, whose collection was auctioned off. It was an impressive assortment of rare volumes primarily focused on seventeenth-century authors, an area in which he was considered an expert. Many of those books bore the markings of libraries from across the nation. He had borrowed them and never found the right moment to return them. They clung to him like legal precedents. Yet, he was a pious man, renowned for his exceptional sermons, which I can personally attest to. If pressed, I would concede that it is indeed challenging to part with a beloved book.

In fact, the only reliable principle concerning books seems to be that espoused by a gentleman who was once asked by a friend to lend a particular volume. “I’m sorry,” he replied, “but I simply cannot.” “Don’t you have it?” inquired the friend. “Yes, I possess it,” he affirmed, “but I adhere to a strict rule of never lending books. You see, no one ever returns them. I know this from personal experience. Come, let me show you.” He led the way to his library, declaring, “There are four thousand volumes here. Every—single—one—of them—borrowed.” Hence, never lend books. You cannot trust even your closest friend in this regard. I know this all too well. Where on earth did that copy of Gil Blas go? And Silvio Pellico? And… why continue the enumeration? He knows. HE KNOWS.

Then there are hats. Some individuals willingly exchange hats, which I find utterly inexcusable. This act crosses the fragile boundary of conscience where honesty and dishonesty disguise themselves. It is impossible to don a stranger’s hat without being acutely aware of the fact. Yet, it happens. I once placed a silk hat on a rack in the smoking room of the House of Commons, only to discover later that it had vanished, leaving no silk hat in its stead. I was forced to walk bareheaded through Palace Yard and Whitehall to purchase a replacement. I’ve often pondered the identity of the man who took my hat while carrying his own. Was he a Tory? A Radical? Surely, he couldn't have been a Labour supporter, as no Labour man could don a silk hat without feeling it scorch his brow. The idea of Will Crooks in a silk hat is almost as absurd as envisioning the Archbishop of Canterbury in a bowler; it feels almost sacrilegious. 

It is conceivable that the gentleman who absconded with my silk umbrella genuinely made an honest mistake. If he were aware of its true owner, perhaps he would return it along with his compliments. Such instances have occurred before. I, too, have exchanged umbrellas on occasion. I hope I did so with integrity, but one can never be entirely certain. Reflecting on it now, I realize that even that silk umbrella wasn’t originally mine. It was part of a lengthy series of exchanges in which I alternately gained and lost. My most striking exchange transpired at the residence of a wealthy individual where I had been invited for dinner with some politicians. During the summer, the weather was dry, and for several days, I had no need for an umbrella. Then one day, a sensation swept through my household. An umbrella adorned with a gold band and a tassel, inscribed with the name of a prominent statesman, was discovered in the stand. Never had such a magnificent umbrella graced our home. We were immediately struck with a mix of awe and dread—humbled by its splendor and terrified by its presence. I felt as if I had been caught red-handed in an act of theft against the British Empire. In a flurry, I composed a letter to the owner, expressing my admiration for his political views but clarifying that I had no intention of stealing his umbrella. I then hailed a cab and delivered both the umbrella and my note to the nearest dispatch office.

The owner responded graciously, accepting full responsibility for the mix-up when he returned my own umbrella. “What can I say?” he remarked, “with the distinguished gentleman who donned the hat I had on, the second distinguished gentleman who handed me a coat, the third who placed an umbrella in my hand, and the fourth who ushered me into a carriage, I was too confused by all the esteemed flunkeys to refuse anything offered to me.”

It is worth noting that it was the name embossed on the umbrella that ultimately saved the day in this scenario. That’s the way to outsmart the individual with an umbrella conscience. I can picture him examining his newfound treasure with secret delight, only to then notice the name and address, at which point his unwavering belief in his own honesty would take over. After my experience today, I’m considering engraving my name on my umbrella. But certainly not on that dilapidated thing languishing in the corner. I have no qualms about letting anyone take that. It’s truly up for grabs.
