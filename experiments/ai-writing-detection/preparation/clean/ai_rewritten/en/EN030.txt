The future is uncertain, and while we cannot predict how we will react in dire situations, we are often acutely aware of our limitations. For instance, one might find themselves at a loss in a crisis, such as a fire on a ship or in a building, unsure how to respond amid the chaos. The shock and terror of such emergencies could freeze our instincts or even unleash our primal urges for self-preservation. Although true emotional crises are infrequent, and no amount of preparation can truly equip us for them, we can only hope that we would act appropriately in those moments. Personally, I recognize a fundamental limitation of mine: I am utterly incapable of reciting poetry or performing in front of an audience, regardless of the circumstances. Facing a crowd, even one made up of friends, fills me with a dread that surpasses any physical danger I might encounter. The thought of standing before spectators to express dramatic verses through gestures and expressions sends shivers down my spine.

Watching a skilled reciter perform, no matter how composed they appear, often overwhelms me with embarrassment. Throughout my limited experiences with reciters, I have felt more acutely ashamed for them than they could ever feel for themselves. Even the esteemed actor Brandram, when he adopted a falsetto voice for Shakespeare's female characters, made my face flush with discomfort, compelling me to shut my eyes during his rendition of "Boots at the Holly Tree Inn." However, Brandram was not merely a reciter in the traditional sense; he was more of a solo performer who presented an entire play, sans costumes or sets. The reciters I refer to typically focus on individual pieces and often consist of enthusiastic amateurs who remain undeterred by the scathing critiques in Mr. Anstey's "Burglar Bill," entertaining at various gatherings or smoking concerts. In my youth, the most prominent reciter was the multifaceted Dagonet, who had a notably effective counterpart in the late Mr. Eaton, known for "The Fireman's Wedding." Their era also introduced "Kissing Cup's Race," a piece that Mr. Lewis Sydney and "Q" worked tirelessly to render obsolete. Today, I find myself at a loss regarding the current favorites in recitation, as I have not encountered any in quite some time.

Reflecting on the past, I recall both the best and the worst recitations I have encountered. Among the worst was "Papa's Letter," a poem steeped in cloying sentimentality that could evoke tears from the audience in droves. The story, narrated by a young widow, centers around her golden-haired son who wishes to write to his father. This was prior to Sir Oliver Lodge's advancements in wireless communication between the living and the dead. As the child couldn't write, the mother whimsically transforms him into a letter by placing a stamp on his forehead. He then dashes outside to play but is tragically struck by a runaway horse, and—

The identity of the author of this overly sentimental piece eludes me, although I once spotted W. S. Gilbert's name in a program associated with it. Could it really have been his creation? The psychology behind humor is indeed perplexing.

Turning to the best recitation I remember, I must mention an experience from twenty-five years ago that has only recently been traced back to print. It was performed at a Bohemian gathering I attended in a Fleet Street tavern, where a large Scottish painter, with a visage reminiscent of Falstaff, took the stage. His jovial, ruddy face and exuberantly white hair and beard made quite an impression. I had never crossed paths with him before or after that evening, yet the memory of his presence amidst the swirling tobacco smoke remains vivid. When called upon to share something, the towering figure claimed he would recite James Boswell's authentic opinion of Dr. Johnson. An electric anticipation filled the room, as the artist was renowned for this performance. For someone like me, unfamiliar with the work, the title alone was enticing enough. With a depth of humor that seemed to require crossing borders, he presented the piece while perched tipsily on the edge of an imaginary bed. Each word resonated, and by the conclusion, the grandiose image of the Great Cham was utterly dismantled. For years, I sought this poem, yet no one could provide information about it. Occasionally, I encountered someone who had heard it, but the authorship remained a mystery. It was only last year that I learned the name of the author: Godfrey Turner, a prominent figure in Fleet Street during the 'sixties and 'seventies. A chance meeting with his son led me to various humorous anthologies, one of which, H. S. Leigh's "Jeux d'Esprit," contained the poem. Like many exceptional recitations, it loses its charm when merely read, yet as delivered by the Scottish painter, it came to life in a remarkable way. Here it is; though I suspect there is a mistake at the start of the third stanza unless it reflects Boswell's befuddlement.

While the piece was splendid in the hands of that Scottish artist, I find no desire to hear it again. In fact, I would only be inclined to listen to recitations that mimic others. Under those circumstances, I could appreciate anything, as the allure of mimicry is profound. Perhaps part of this enchantment stems from my own inability to imitate; regardless, a capable mimic never fails to astonish me, filling me with admiration. However, I am aware that many impersonators who grace the stage are nothing more than opportunists, too lethargic and lacking the creativity to develop original material. Instead, they pilfer snippets of popular songs, attempting to parody the original artists. Yet, despite this, I often find myself inexplicably pleased to see them included in the lineup.

What stands out about talented mimics is their ability to not only replicate vocal tones but also to capture the essence of a person's conversational style. I recall an instance where a particularly skilled performer gave a spontaneous impression of a renowned scholar he had just met. Interestingly, during that impersonation, the mimic, despite being somewhat uneducated, seemed to adopt vocabulary and phrasing that I am certain he did not possess in his everyday speech. It was as if, for a brief moment, the mimic transformed into the scholar, albeit with an underlying touch of humor or mischief. It raises the intriguing question of whether, in such intense moments of impersonation, the original person's essence diminishes—if, for that fleeting time, they are, in some way, less themselves.
