The challenge presented by cities was one of illumination. How could one guide the residents through the labyrinthine streets once the heavens had withdrawn their celestial lantern? Or, in a more scientific sense, how could they navigate after the Earth had turned away from the sun? The moon, occasionally, provided some assistance; the stars, too, twinkled cheerfully above the rooftops. A beacon here and there on a church or fortress created a picturesque scene and, in the uneven terrain, offered a guiding light for those lost in the darkness. Yet, when the sun, moon, and stars were obscured or absent, the nighttime traveler had to rely on—according to old illustrations—tall, stable lanterns, two stories high. These makeshift lighthouses had numerous holes drilled into their conical roofs, allowing beams of light to shoot forth into the eyes of the bearer. As he ventured into the eerie night, carrying his own source of illumination by a ring on his finger, he experienced the cyclical nature of day and night around him. Shadows loomed over his path, and he felt as if he were being haunted by specters; when the curfew struck, he found himself alone, relying solely on the light he carried in a town enveloped in darkness.

Following the era of portable lanterns in a world of obscurity, a new phase emerged characterized by oil lamps that were notoriously difficult to light and easily extinguished. Their flickering glow was pale and unsteady. The winds of the heavens would blow boisterously, and mischievous children would snuff out the flames, plunging the city back into darkness. Navigating the streets became a challenge, with citizens groping along walls, dining without comfort, and stumbling through muddy gutters. As if the playful winds and rambunctious youths weren’t enough, these fragile lights were often strung between houses, swinging above the thoroughfare. If a gallant general happened to ride by on horseback, passionately pursuing the fate of nations, there was a high likelihood of shattered glass and spilled blood, leaving the street once again in its original state of darkness—untraveled and uncharted, resembling a desolate wasteland.

The conservative, reflecting on the past and the future, finds satisfaction in the progress achieved. Looking back at the dim, flickering lights of his predecessors, he feels uplifted by the advancements that have been made. The advent of gas lamps marked a transformative period for social gatherings and communal enjoyment, heralding a new era. This innovation was a significant step forward, allowing humanity to escape the constraints of fog and darkness. Evening strolls were no longer a thing of the past; the promenade remained lively well after sunset, extending the day's pleasures for everyone. The urban populace had their own stars—tame and obedient.

It must be acknowledged that these gas lamps did not shine as steadily or brightly as their celestial counterparts, nor did they possess the elegance of fine wax candles. However, their proximity rendered them far more practical than the distant planets. It is also worth noting that they did not emerge in a synchronized manner like the heavenly bodies, which appear in the sky as needed. Instead, the lamplighters would dash through the streets each evening, their enthusiasm evident. It was a sight to behold, as man sought to imitate the reliability of the cosmos, and despite the imperfections—occasional mishaps resulting in unfortunate accidents—the public praised the lamplighter’s dedication. Children were taught to express their admiration, not in exact words, which might be deemed inappropriate, but in a way that was suitable for young mouths.

Indeed, we should commend him! Yet, the time for his diligent work is drawing to a close, and soon we will no longer see him racing down the street, punctuating the twilight with each illuminated lamp as he goes. The Greeks would have crafted a grand myth around him, imagining how he distributed starlight and collected it once the need had passed. His lantern, capable of igniting a whole neighborhood, would have been immortalized in tales. Now, as with all heroic endeavors, his efforts are approaching an end. A new milestone has been reached. The stars that grace our streets in the future will not be lit one by one; rather, they will all ignite simultaneously. An electrician in a remote office flicks a switch—and suddenly, the entire city is awash in light! Fiat Lux, the electrician declares. What a sight it is, on a clear evening from the heights of Hampstead Hill, when the sprawling urban landscape is revealed in an instant—a vast, shimmering design that stretches for miles. On that night, all the street lamps burst into illumination together, creating a symphony of light! This is the dawn of a new era, heralded recently by an experiment in Pall Mall. The rise of electric light represents one of civilization's most romantic advancements, a silver lining amid a multitude of factories and clerical offices. For those artists who ponder the beauty of places like Thirlmere, this might offer a sliver of solace; at least for those who appreciate beauty and are willing to accept it wherever it appears.

Nevertheless, while the conservative celebrates progress, he remains cautious of change; he advocates for restraint and careful consideration. The mere mention of electricity now evokes a sense of foreboding. In Paris, at the entrance of the Passage des Princes, near the Opera House, and along Rue Drouot at the Figaro office, a new and disturbing form of urban illumination now flickers ominously at night—an unsettling sight, almost grotesque. Such a light seems fit only for scenes of violence or the corridors of asylums, enhancing dread rather than dispelling it. Just a glance is enough to rekindle an appreciation for gas lighting, which casts a warm, inviting glow. One might think humanity would be satisfied with what Prometheus had gifted them, rather than seeking to capture the wild lightning from the stormy skies above. Yet here we find ourselves confronted with this blinding light, and the suggestion is made that we should venture out under the glare of this terrifying brilliance. It wouldn’t take a superstitious person to hesitate about indulging in leisure under such an unsettling illumination, nor would it be unreasonable to prefer a more gentle light that reveals beauty more gracefully. While the harsh glare may illuminate the domain of the unsavory Figaro, which feels more at home in the depths of hell, where joy and laughter prevail, where the philosopher observes quietly, where love and merriment flourish, let us at least allow the old, soft glow to illuminate our paths.
