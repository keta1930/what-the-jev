In August, there's a certain pleasure in relaxing in Fleet Street or wherever the demands of work may confine you, while reminiscing about the sea. I find no envy for the throngs flocking to Margate and Blackpool, Salcombe and Minehead, for I've convinced myself that the sea has lost its charm since my youth. Back then, the tidal pools brimmed with starfish, sizable crabs roamed the empty shores, and anemones danced with their tentacles from every crevice.

While poets may claim the sea remains unchanged (and they might be correct regarding the water itself), I suspect the beaches are deteriorating. Over the past decade, I doubt I’ve encountered more than five starfish, despite numerous walks along the shoreline—not solely in pursuit of lost golf balls. Occasionally, I might spot a small crab hastily retreating to safety, but the formidable creatures that once sparked both fear and excitement during our childhood escapades are conspicuously absent. I cannot accept the rationale that my finger has simply grown larger; I would prefer to believe that today's children discover crabs and starfish in the same abundance and size I once did. Yet, we cannot ignore the fact that their numbers are dwindling. It’s clear that repeatedly collecting starfish to display as barometers in our homes detracts from the future generations' experience.

Another pastime of our youth, I suppose, has become inaccessible to children today. We would wait for the tide to recede just enough before daring to traverse the base of the cliffs, moving from one sandy cove to another. The waves would lap at the rocks, and a single misstep could send us tumbling into the water. It was thrilling to feel the peril of being caught by the rising tide without facing actual danger. On rare occasions, we were fortunate enough to see frantic figures at the cliff's summit, ignorant of the true nature of our adventure, believing we were in dire straits. Those moments were particularly exhilarating.

Since those carefree days, I have engaged in a bit of serious climbing, though none has quite matched that thrill. The sea was never more than a foot below us, and the water only two feet deep, yet the shock of an unexpected fall would be just as jolting as plunging down a cliff. Thus, we enjoyed the dual pleasures of climbing—the physical satisfaction of the ascent and the exhilarating relief when your heart settled back into its normal rhythm. Moreover, you couldn't meet your demise, and if you faced an impassable obstacle, you could simply wait for the tide to drop and reveal a new foothold.

However, I fear such joys are now lost on today's youth. The tides may not retreat as reliably as they once did, or perhaps the cliffs have altered in form and quality. It’s also possible that people have become more informed, no longer mistaking the nature of our activities. For whatever reason, I seem to fare better in Fleet Street, content to envision myself by the sea rather than risk disappointment with reality.

In my mind's eye, I escape the noise of bands and piers; a band playing by a moonlit shore evokes a sense of adulthood, while the beach and the remaining crabs summon the spirit of childhood, creating a conflict that can easily lead to discontent. I can picture myself with a shovel and bucket, reveling in simple happiness. Recently, I encountered a fortunate young boy with a pile of sand in his garden, and I was delighted to receive a request for a tunnel. The tunnel I built was quite satisfactory, yet not so flawless that I couldn't envision improving it with practice. I left with aspirations of becoming an architect; if I return to the sea, I will construct a proper tunnel, and then—who knows what else? Presently, I feel invigorated, as if I could create a cathedral.

Nevertheless, there remains one aspect of childhood joy that is forever elusive: the sheer delight of getting soaked in the sea. Near Fleet Street stands a statue honoring the man who pioneered Sunday schools in England, but the individual who truly deserves commemoration in stone is the doctor who first suggested that saltwater couldn't give you a cold. Whether this assertion holds any truth is uncertain, but it provided a marvelous and steadfast rebuttal to concerned adults, enriching the joys of seaside excursions. Unfortunately, such joys are no longer accessible to those who have reached adulthood. I, for instance, can choose to soak my feet in fresh water at will, but finding that same thrill in saltwater is no longer a privilege.

Given my sentiments and reflections, it saddens me to acknowledge that if I were to visit the sea this August, it would not be with a spade and bucket in hand but rather with a set of golf clubs. Even my evenings would be spent cycling to the nearest town to fetch a newspaper, rather than enjoying the tranquility of the beach. Yet, you cannot convince me that my affection for the sea has waned or that I no longer find joy in the innocent pleasures of childhood. I firmly believe that it is the sea that has changed, and I take comfort in imagining it as it once was, all while remaining happily ensconced in Fleet Street.
