Two soldiers, clearly siblings, were positioned at the entrance of a railway carriage—one seated within the compartment, the other standing on the platform.

"Promise me you won't forget to write, Bill," the one on the platform urged.

"I won’t," replied Bill. "I’ll be back at—tonight, and I’ll make sure to write everyone tomorrow. But, goodness, what a task! There's Mother, the missus, Bob and Sarah, Aunt Jane, Uncle Jim, and—well, you know the whole crowd. You’ve had to do it, Sam."

"Indeed," Sam said with a hint of regret; “it’s quite a puzzle.”

"And if you write to one and overlook another, they all get upset," Bill continued. "But I make sure to mention everyone. I’ll write 'love to Sarah' and 'hope Aunt Jane’s cold is better,' and that sort of thing—it helps fill the page. But I’m stumped if I can think of anything else. I just start with 'I hope this finds you well, as it leaves me at present,' and then I’m out of steam. What else can I say?"

"Nothing," Sam said with a sigh. "I just sit there staring at the paper, but nothing comes to me. It feels like my mind is as empty as a drum."

"Same here. It’s not like writing love letters. Back when I was doing that, it was easy. If I hit a wall, I’d just fill half a page with doodles, and it looked just fine. But writing to Mother, the missus, Sarah, Jim, and the others is a whole different ballgame. You can’t just fill it with doodles; that would look ridiculous."

"Quite right," Sam agreed.

As the train began to pull away, the soldier inside settled back into his seat, pulled out a cigarette, and lit it. I learned that he had been at the front twice and was now on sick leave. He had participated in the battle of Mons, experienced the retreat to the Marne, advanced to the Aisne, fought in the first battle of Ypres, and engaged in the fighting at Festubert. He had been present for some of the defining events of history, yet when it came to writing a letter, even to his wife, he found himself at a loss for words. He was much like a woman mentioned by Horace Walpole, whose correspondence began and ended with the statement: "I write to you because I have nothing to do: I finish because I have nothing to say."

It seems that never before has there been such a surge in letter-writing as there is today, and much of it is well-crafted, as evidenced in newspapers. However, the experience of my companion on the train reflects the struggles of countless young men who, for the first time, are eager to express themselves in writing but find that they lack the ability to convey their thoughts. This is not due to a lack of intelligence; rather, the act of putting pen to paper leaves them paralyzed. They struggle to translate the vibrant atmosphere surrounding them into concrete words. They require encouragement to articulate their feelings. Once prompted, they can articulate their thoughts perfectly well, but without that nudge, they remain silent.

In a broader context, it can be argued that letter-writing is a dying art. The introduction of the penny post and the fast pace of modern life have contributed to its decline. When renowned writers such as Madame de Sévigny, Cowper, Horace Walpole, Byron, Lamb, and the Carlyles penned their memorable letters, society was much more leisurely, allowing for the indulgence of writing to friends. The expense of mailing a letter made it a significant endeavor. If you could only send a letter once a month or even every six months, and at a high cost, it became a matter of great importance. The less fortunate, of course, could hardly partake in the luxury of letter-writing. De Quincey recounts how a century ago, the rural families of Lakeland would avoid postal fees. A letter arriving by stagecoach would be received by a mother who, upon glancing at the address and recognizing a certain mark indicating her son was well, would return it unopened to the carrier. In those days, receiving a letter was an event.

Today, however, with the ability to send a letter across the globe for a mere penny and postal services delivering multiple times a day, many of us do not take letter-writing seriously. Carlyle foresaw that the introduction of the penny post would diminish the art of letter-writing by making it too affordable. "I shall send a penny letter next time," he wrote to his mother as cheap postage was on the horizon, predicting that people would no longer feel compelled to write meaningful letters when they could send them for next to nothing. He was correct; the telegraph, the telephone, and the postcard have further contributed to the decline of this once revered form of communication. The rarity or difficulty of obtaining something often elevates its value. If diamonds were as common as pebbles, we wouldn’t bother to pick them up.

Yet, the situation for Bill, Sam, and countless others today diverges from this trend. They aren’t aiming to craft literary masterpieces; rather, they wish to share their experiences with loved ones back home, to convey something about their lives and the monumental events they are part of. However, the enormity of those experiences often eludes their expression. They should not feel pressured to articulate the grand narratives; instead, the essence of letter-writing resides in the small, intimate details. Bill might not be able to narrate the retreat from Mons, but he could share, as he did with me, the story of the blister on his heel, his yearning for a cigarette, the endless marches that left him exhausted, losing his friend at Le Cateau, or how his boot sole fell off at Meaux. Through such mundane details, he would paint a vivid picture of the great retreat.

To craft an effective letter, one must approach the task with a light and casual attitude. It is essential to be personal rather than abstract. One should not dismiss the significance of everyday occurrences as too trivial to mention. Instead, one should recognize that these small moments are precisely what we discuss at home. By sharing these, the recipient gains a sense of presence; they can envision the writer, hear their voice, and understand their circumstances. This is the true purpose of a letter. Keats encapsulated this concept beautifully in one of his extensive letters addressed to his brother George and his wife in America, where he expressed the depth of familial affection that characterized his nature. He described a visit to his mother, the laughter over his poor jokes, their outing for tea at Mrs. Millar’s, and how the interplay of light and shadow struck them as they passed through the Horse Guards. He continued, "I intend to write you such volumes that it will be impossible for me to keep any order or method in what I write; that will come first which is uppermost in my mind, not that which is uppermost in my heart—besides I should wish to give you a picture of our lives here whenever by a touch I can do it; even as you must see by the last sentence our walk past Whitehall all in good health and spirits—this I am certain of because I felt so much pleasure from the simple idea of your playing a game of cricket."

This illustrates the recipe from one of the masters of the craft. A letter written in such a manner bridges the gap of distance; it sustains the personal exchanges and intimate connections that separation interrupts, ensuring one’s presence is felt even in their absence. It should never be too simple, too ordinary, or too conversational. Its familiarity is not a weakness but rather its greatest strength. Should it strive for order, grandeur, or elaboration, it may turn into a fine essay yet will undoubtedly fail as a letter.
