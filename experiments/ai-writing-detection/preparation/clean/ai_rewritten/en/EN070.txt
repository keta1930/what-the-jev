On my final morning along the Flemish coastline, with the knowledge that I would soon be crossing over to England, my attention was caught by a piece of Gothic carving, a common sight in Flanders. Its age was uncertain, as it bore the marks of wear and was somewhat illegible, yet it undeniably reflected the style of the early Middle Ages. The sculpture seemed to depict individuals engaged in various primary activities: some appeared to be sailors tugging on ropes, others were reaping crops, and a few were energetically pouring substances into each other. This portrayal is emblematic of the early thirteenth century, a period renowned for its vigorous spirit. In contrast, the ancient Greeks tended to immortalize their gods and heroes in moments of stillness. Their art, while splendid and philosophical in its poise, often conveyed a sense of detachment, as if they were masters presiding over a multitude of laborers. In stark contrast, the early medieval artists relished the representation of people in action—be it hunting, boating, grape treading, shoemaking, or cooking. The essence of life was captured in their works, showcasing the vibrancy of human endeavor. The Middle Ages, a time of burgeoning social order, was marked by strong religious convictions that had yet to become burdensome. This distinct contrast between Greek and Gothic art is palpable; the figures of the Elgin marbles appear eternally frozen in a moment of perfection, while medieval carvings emanate a sense of movement and activity, akin to the buzz of a beehive surrounding a grand cathedral.

However, there was something peculiar about these particular figures that left me uncertain. Those that had heads bore oddly shaped features, and it seemed to me that their mouths were open. I couldn’t tell if this was a deliberate artistic choice or merely an artifact of nascent creativity. As I pondered this, I recalled how singing was often intertwined with many of the tasks depicted—there are songs for reapers and for sailors hauling ropes. My thoughts continued to wander while I strolled along the pier at Ostend, where I heard sailors shouting in rhythm as they worked. This reminded me that sailors still chant in unison while laboring, often changing their songs based on the specific tasks at hand. Later, after my sea journey concluded, the sight of men toiling in English fields brought to mind the traditional songs associated with harvesting and various agricultural activities. It struck me as curious that, despite this rich history, no modern trades seem to possess a ritualistic form of poetry. How did it come to be that people sang simple verses while performing specific tasks like pulling ropes or gathering fruit? Why is it that no one engages in similar practices while creating modern products? It is unusual that a contemporary newspaper is never printed with the accompaniment of a chorus, nor do shopkeepers ever seem to break into song.

If reapers can sing while they reap, why can’t auditors sing while they audit, or bankers while they bank? If there are songs dedicated to the various duties aboard a ship, then surely there should be songs for the myriad tasks performed in a bank. As the train sped from Dover through the lush Kentish gardens, I found myself attempting to compose a few verses that might suit the work of bank clerks. Perhaps the task of tallying figures could begin with a resounding chorus extolling the virtues of simple addition.

“Up my lads and lift the ledgers, sleep and ease are o’er. Hear the Stars of Morning shouting: ‘Two and Two are four.’ Though the creeds and realms are reeling, though the sophists roar, Though we weep and pawn our watches, Two and Two are Four.”

“There’s a run upon the Bank—Stand away! For the Manager’s a crank and the Secretary drank, and the Upper Tooting Bank Turns to bay! Stand close: there is a run On the Bank. Of our ship, our royal one, let the ringing legend run, That she fired with every gun Ere she sank.”

Upon arriving in London, enveloped by its familiar haze, I encountered a friend who works in a bank and shared my lyrical suggestions with him for potential use among his colleagues. However, he was not particularly optimistic about their reception. He clarified that his reluctance didn't stem from any judgment about the quality of the verses or their polish. Rather, he sensed an indefinable atmosphere within our society that makes it inherently difficult to sing in such environments. I found his insight compelling, although it left me pondering the underlying mystery of it all. It also made me reflect on the views of Socialists, who often attribute societal distress not to moral issues but to the chaos inherent in private enterprise. Given that banks are private entities, I had expected the post office, which operates on a collectivist model, to embrace the concept of choral singing. To my surprise, when I encouraged a lady at my local post office to sing, she met the idea with a far colder reception than the bank clerk had. In fact, she appeared to be in a considerably more somber state than he.

Some might argue that my verses were to blame for her demeanor, yet it is only fair to mention that the sample verse from the hypothetical Post-Office Hymn read:

“O’er London our letters are shaken like snow, Our wires o’er the world like the thunderbolts go. The news that may marry a maiden in Sark, Or kill an old lady in Finsbury Park.”

Chorus (with a swing of joy and energy):

“Or kill an old lady in Finsbury Park.”

The more I contemplated the situation, the more it became evident that many of the most significant and characteristic modern activities seemed devoid of a choral spirit. For instance, one cannot be a successful financier and sing simultaneously; the essence of financial success lies in maintaining silence. In many contemporary circles, being a public figure also necessitates a certain reticence, as nearly all actions are performed discreetly. The notion of a chorus of money-lenders is almost laughable. The well-known story of the solicitors’ corps, who, upon hearing “Charge!” from their Colonel, collectively responded with “Six-and-eightpence,” illustrates this stark contrast. In military settings, men can sing while charging, yet the same cannot be said for legal proceedings. Ultimately, my reflections led me to a disheartening realization akin to my bank clerk friend’s subconscious feeling—that there is something spiritually stifling about our modern existence. Bank clerks lack songs, not due to their financial circumstances, but rather because of a deeper sorrow. Sailors, despite their poverty, still manage to sing. As I made my way home, I passed by a small tin building of a religious nature, resonating with exuberant shouts akin to a trumpet filled with sound. They were singing wholeheartedly, and for a fleeting moment, I entertained a recurring thought: in our society, the superhuman realm is often the only place where one can encounter genuine humanity. Human nature feels pursued and has sought refuge in sacred spaces.
