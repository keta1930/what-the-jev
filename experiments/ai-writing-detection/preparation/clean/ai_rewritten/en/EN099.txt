What is it that we all seek? I suspect that the answer is surprisingly close. As a child, I imagined that Heaven or Fairyland—whatever I chose to call it—lay just behind me, which is why I could never quite glimpse it, no matter how many times I twisted and turned in an attempt to catch it off guard. I envisioned a person perpetually spinning around on one foot, like a top, in an effort to discover that hidden world that perpetually eluded him. Perhaps this is the reason the world spins. It is as if the world is forever attempting to glance over its shoulder in pursuit of the elusive realm that it cannot exist without.

In any event, as I mentioned earlier, I believe we must always envision the ultimate aim of our endeavors as something curiously close at hand. While science takes pride in discussing the vast distances of its celestial bodies and the daunting remoteness of the phenomena it examines, poetry and religion insist on the immediacy of their subjects, often highlighting their unnerving nearness. The Kingdom of Heaven is always described as being "At Hand," while Looking-glass Land is merely a reflection through the glass. Thus, it wouldn’t astonish me if the next turn down a street led me to the very center of that labyrinth where all the mystics seem to be lost. I wouldn’t even be surprised if, upon rounding a corner in Fleet Street, I stumbled upon an even stranger lamp; nor would I be shocked if I found myself in Elfland after the next turn.

However, what did catch me off guard recently was something far more unexpected. I ventured out of Fleet Street and unexpectedly found myself in England.

The peculiar shock I felt likely merits some explanation. Even in the bleakest or most disappointing moments of English life, there is one fundamental truth about our nation that must not be overlooked: England is not nearly as foolish as it appears. The various representations of England, the external symbols of the country, often mislead the observer. England operates as an oligarchy, deliberately presenting an elite that seems inferior to the general populace.

Consider the discourse in the House of Commons; it is not only poorer than the speaking found in many smaller debate clubs or informal dinners, but it also falls short of what is deemed acceptable in almost every other venue. It seems that my countrymen prefer this solemn absurdity at the higher echelons of our national life. It may be a curious sight to witness the blind leading the blind, but England offers a stranger spectacle: the blind attempting to guide those who can see. This, in fact, is an understatement. The ignorance displayed by political leaders is often a manufactured and deliberate facade. If one is fortunate enough to engage in genuine conversation with a politician, they may find themselves astonished by the intelligent insights that emerge. Initially, it can be unsettling. I have never been close enough to one of these individuals to question why they choose to feign ignorance while in Parliament.

The same holds true for the electorate. The average voter seems to cast their ballot beneath their own potential; they engage in the act of voting with only a fraction of their mind. Voting should encompass the entirety of an individual—just as one should worship or marry with their whole being. When casting a vote, one ought to engage their intellect and emotions, their spirit and physical instincts, their appreciation for beauty and their love for music. If they have ever witnessed a breathtaking sunset, the vibrant hues should influence their choice at the polls. If they have been moved by beautiful melodies, those songs should echo in their ears as they make that sacred mark. Yet, the challenge confronting English democracy at every election is that it often represents something less than its true potential. The issue is not merely that a minority of the electorate participates; rather, it is that a minority of those who vote truly engage with the process.

This is the tragedy of England: one cannot accurately judge the nation based on its most visible leaders. The stereotypes we encounter do not encapsulate the reality. On the occasion I reference, I discovered this to be particularly true of a once-vibrant middle class, which I assumed had nearly disappeared from existence. It appeared to me that the primary representatives of this class had all drifted in different directions; they had either pursued the allure of the Smart Set or sought the simplicity of the Simple Life. Personally, I find it difficult to favor one over the other; those individuals may keep either—or, more likely, both—alternatively succumbing to the sickness of each. Yet, all the notable figures who seem to embody the middle class have either adopted the monocle of Mr. Chamberlain or the singular vision of Mr. Bernard Shaw.

This old class I speak of lacks a true representative. Its sustenance was ample but unpretentious; its meals were simple yet devoid of trends. It took politics seriously, and when it engaged in public discourse, it committed the faux pas of attempting to articulate well. I believed that this earnest political England had all but vanished. Yet, as I said, I made a turn away from Fleet Street and discovered a room filled with that very essence.

At the front of the room stood a chair once occupied by Johnson. This was a club where Wilkes had addressed the masses in a time when even the most disreputable among us exuded a sense of vigor. However, one could argue that these historical references might merely denote a bygone era. The most remarkable aspect was that the hall resonated with the passion, sincerity, fervor, and oratory characteristic of the eighteenth century. The club members represented a spectrum of opinions, yet not a single speech struck me with the dissonance of unreality that often accompanies hearing skilled speakers express sentiments I share. The Toryism present in this club mirrored that of Johnson: a Toryism that embraced humor and appealed to humanity. The democracy found here resembled that of Wilkes: a democracy capable of crafting epigrams and engaging in duels, a democracy that could confront challenges head-on and withstand slander; it was the democracy of Wilkes—or, perhaps more accurately, that of Fox.

One element in particular stirred my soul, connecting me to my ancestors. Each speaker, regardless of their eloquence, rose to address the audience with raw passion, intent on dismantling their opponent—not through mockery, but via genuine eloquence. This marks a significant decline in our modern political discourse. Today, a speaker does not become more oratorical as their sincerity deepens. An eighteenth-century orator, when genuinely enraged, would search for grand words to obliterate their adversary. In contrast, the contemporary speaker resorts to petty language to undermine their opponent. The rhetoric now resides in the formalities that go unheard. When a politician—be it Mr. Chamberlain, a moderate figure, or a more radical socialist—becomes truly sincere, their discourse degenerates into something coarse, punctuated by phrases like, “Where do we come in?” or “It’s your money they want.”

The individuals in this eighteenth-century club were entirely of that bygone era; each one stood up visibly trembling with emotion, striving to vanquish their counterpart through the power of articulate expression. I found myself debating with them on the topic of Home Rule, and ultimately expressed my belief that the English aristocracy held disdain for the idea of an Irish Parliament because it would resemble their own club.

As I exited the club and stepped back into Fleet Street under the dim glow of a streetlamp, I stumbled upon a crude poster about Wastrels, claiming that London was rising against something hardly anyone had heard of. Suddenly, it struck me, almost like a vivid tableau: the modern world resembles a vast and tempestuous ocean, teeming with monstrous, living entities. And upon this turbulent sea rests a fragile, thin layer of ice, composed of deceitful wealth and duplicitous journalism.

Standing there in the shadows, I could almost hear that ice beginning to crack.
