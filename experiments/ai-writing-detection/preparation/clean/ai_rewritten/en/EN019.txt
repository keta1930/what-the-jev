He was a reserved individual, clad in somber attire and sporting a wide-brimmed straw hat. His moustache and whiskers had a distinctly military air, yet his posture was uncharacteristically slouched, and his dreamy gaze seemed lost in thought. As our modest pleasure boat approached Yarmouth Harbour, he cast a gloomy yet curious glance at the chaotic array of small vessels that filled the waters. It is well known that entering this harbour is not a straightforward affair; instead of approaching the town directly like a visitor, boats must navigate around the back as if sneaking in like a traitor. The river passage appears almost too constricted for substantial traffic, making the larger ships loom like giants against the sky. As we drifted beneath a timber-laden Norwegian ship that appeared to block the heavens like a grand cathedral, the man in the straw hat pointed at a peculiar wooden figurehead, resembling a woman, and remarked, as if continuing a prior discussion, “Why have they stopped making these? They never harmed anyone.”

I responded with a lighthearted quip about the captain's wife being envious, yet deep down, I recognized that he had touched upon a profound truth. There is something in our contemporary civilization that seems antagonistic toward such wholesome and beautiful symbols.

“They despise anything that is human and lovely,” he continued, precisely voicing my unspoken thoughts. “I suspect they destroyed all the cheerful old figureheads with hatchets and took pleasure in it.”

“Like Mr. Quilp,” I replied, “when he smashed the wooden Admiral with a poker.”

In an instant, his demeanor transformed; he straightened and looked me in the eye, fully engaged for the first time.

“Do you come to Yarmouth for that?” he inquired.

“For what?” I asked, puzzled.

“For Dickens,” he clarified, tapping his foot rhythmically on the deck.

“No,” I replied, “I come here for enjoyment, though that isn’t entirely different.”

“I always come,” he stated with a quiet resolution, “to find Peggotty’s boat. It isn’t here.”

At that moment, I fully grasped his sentiment.

Yarmouth exists in two distinct forms; I imagine there are countless renditions in the eyes of its residents. For myself, I have yet to exhaust the list of Batterseas. Yet for visitors and tourists, there are two predominant versions: the impoverished, dignified part and the thriving, garish section. My new companion seemed to haunt the former like a spectral presence, while he only briefly mentioned the latter.

“The place has changed significantly... due to the holidaymakers, you know,” he expressed, not with disdain but with a palpable sadness. This was the closest he would come to acknowledging the overwhelming seaside resort that lined the front in a cacophony of sound and color, dazzling in the sunlight and louder than the ocean itself. But beyond this hubbub—out of earshot—were narrow lanes that felt like secret pathways leading to hidden sanctuaries of tranquility. We wandered through squares filled with such profound silence that entering one felt akin to diving into a serene pool. In these peaceful spots, we strolled and discussed Dickens, or more accurately, engaged in the ritual of reciting lengthy passages that we both knew by heart. In those moments, we were enveloped in the essence of an older England. Fishermen strolled by who could have easily stepped out of a Dickens novel; we visited a dusty curiosity shop and purchased pipe stoppers carved into figures from *The Pickwick Papers*. As evening descended, the slow golden light seemed to seep into every crevice as we made our way into the church.

Inside the dim church, my gaze was drawn to the stained glass windows, which on that clear golden evening, blazed with the fiery heraldry characteristic of the most passionate and ecstatic expressions of Christian art. Finally, I turned to my companion and said:

“Do you see that angel over there? It seems it might represent the angel at the sepulchre.”

Noticing my unusual emotional response, he raised his eyebrows.

“I suppose,” he replied. “What’s strange about that?”

After a moment of contemplation, I asked, “Do you recall what the angel at the sepulchre said?”

“Not particularly,” he admitted, “but why are you in such a hurry?”

I briskly led him out of the tranquil square, past the fishermen’s almshouses, heading toward the coast, while he continued to question my abrupt departure.

“I am going,” I explained, “to feed coins into automatic machines on the beach. I want to listen to the music from the performers. I intend to have my photograph taken. I plan to drink ginger beer from its original bottle. I will buy some postcards, and I am eager for a boat ride. I’m ready to listen to a concertina, and were it not for my lack of musical training, I’d be willing to play it. I am even prepared to ride a donkey, that is, if the donkey is amenable. I’m ready to be a donkey myself; for all of this was commanded by the angel in that stained-glass window.”

“I really think,” he said thoughtfully, “it would be best to put you in the care of your relatives.”

“Sir,” I responded, “there are certain authors to whom humanity owes a great deal, whose talents are so shy, delicate, or reflective that we ought to associate them with particular charming locations or fading memories. It wouldn’t be unusual to seek the essence of Horace Walpole at Strawberry Hill or to find Thackeray’s spirit lingering in Old Kensington. However, let us not indulge in antiquarianism where Dickens is concerned, for he is no relic. Dickens doesn’t look back; he gazes forward. He might observe our modern crowds with either satire or indignation, but he would fundamentally love to engage with them. He may critique our democracy, but it’s because he expects much from it. We shouldn’t confine his works under the title of ‘The Old Curiosity Shop’; rather, let us bind them under ‘Great Expectations.’ Wherever humanity exists, he would urge us to confront it, to embrace it with a fervent passion, and to digest it with the appetite of a giant. We must accept these holidaymakers as he would and extract from them both their tragedy and their comedy. Do you now remember what the angel proclaimed at the sepulchre? ‘Why seek ye the living among the dead? He is not here; he has risen.’”

In that moment, we emerged onto the expansive stretch of beach, bustling with the lively, chaotic energy of our laughter-filled, yet somewhat desperate democracy. The sunset, now reaching its peak glory, cast a vibrant red hue over everyone, shimmering like the colossal firelight synonymous with Dickens’ narratives. In that peculiar evening glow, every figure seemed both absurd and enchanting, as if each held a story waiting to be told. I overheard a little girl, caught in a playful scuffle with another, proudly declare, “My sister-in-law has got four rings beside her wedding ring!”

I paused, eager to hear more, but my friend had already wandered off.
