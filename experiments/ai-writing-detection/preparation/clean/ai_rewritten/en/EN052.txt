Please do not assume that this title suggests an intriguing personal story waiting to be shared. "Story! God bless you, I have none to tell, sir." It has been quite some time since I experienced the thrill of falling in love, and as I gaze into the mirror, noting what Holmes refers to as "Time's visiting cards" adorning my face and hair, I come to the realization that I may never again partake in such an experience. To echo Mr. Kipling's soldier:

That's all shuv be'ind me Long ago and fur away.

Yet, as Wordsworth suggested, poetry is emotion recollected in tranquility, and it is only when you have moved past the experience of love that you can genuinely articulate it or discuss it with the necessary philosophical objectivity.

Falling in love is, of course, not the challenging part; anyone can do that. The real challenge lies in discerning whether the feelings you are experiencing are authentic or merely a facade. Many individuals misinterpret their emotions and only realize, when it is far too late, that they have never truly experienced genuine love. This misunderstanding contributes to the backlog in Divorce Courts. It also highlights the significance of "calf love," which serves as a formative experience, helping one to differentiate between the essence of love and its mere semblance.

When I refer to "calf love," I am not including the vague inklings of early childhood, reminiscent of those immortalized in "Annabel Lee":

I was a child and she was a child In that kingdom by the sea.

* * * * *

Yet we loved with a love that was more than love, I and my Annabel Lee.

I recall that love vividly; it blossomed when I was merely eight years old. "She" was also eight, having just returned from India. Admittedly, she was not conventionally attractive, but—well, she had come from India. The very essence of that distant land's coral beaches captivated me, and it was that allure that drew me to her. Additionally, she was the daughter of a soldier, and in my young mind, being a soldier's daughter was second only to being a soldier himself. This was around the time when, inspired by "The Story of the Hundred Days," I had set off with a satchel containing only a nightshirt and a toothbrush, hoping to enlist in the Black Watch. Alas, my quest ended rather abruptly at the train station. Ultimately, she bestowed upon me, as a symbol of her affection, "Poor Little Gaspard's Drum," a book that introduced me to Napoleon and the Egyptian desert, and most thrilling of all, the Mamelukes. Oh, how that word sent shivers down my spine! "The Mamelukes!" What could one do but fall for a girl who invoked such enchanting phrases?

However, this early affection does not truly represent "calf love." That true experience arrives with the beginnings of adolescence, marked by the first down on one’s lip. People may dismiss "calf love," but such dismissal is akin to mocking the beauty of dawn or the arrival of spring. When David Copperfield found himself enamored with the eldest Miss Larkins, he was genuinely in love with the vast, unfolding universe, and she merely served as the available conduit for his burgeoning emotions.

It is crucial to experience "calf love" while still young. Much like measles, it is generally innocuous during childhood but can become perilous in later years. The "calf love" of an older individual often leads to folly. Hence the adage, "There's no fool like an old fool." An older man should not simply fall into love; rather, he should approach it with caution, much like Mr. Barkis did. That commendable man took the process of love seriously:

"'So she makes,' said Mr. Barkis, after a long interval of reflection, 'all the apple parsties, and does all the cooking, do she?' 

"I confirmed that this was indeed the case.

"'Well, I'll tell you what,' said Mr. Barkis. 'P'raps you might be writin' to her?'

"'I shall certainly write to her,' I responded.

"'Ah!' he said, slowly turning his gaze toward me. 'Well! If you was writin' to her, p'raps you'd recollect to say that Barkis was willin', would you?'"

This exchange exemplifies the careful approach that should accompany middle-aged courtship. The error made by the "Northern Farmer" was that he applied the same cautiousness of age to the youthful heart. "Doänt thou marry for munny; but goä wheer munny is," he advised his son Sammy, who wished to marry the humble parson's daughter. He presented his own matrimonial experience as a model for Sammy:

And I went wheer munny wor, and thy moother coom to and Wi' lots o' munny laäid by, and a nicetish bit o' land. Maybe she worn'd a beauty: I nivver giv' it a thowt; But worn'd she as good to cuddle and kiss as a lass as an't nowt?

I have always held onto the hope that Sammy rejected his father's advice and chose instead the parson's daughter.

There is, of course, nothing inherently wrong with marrying for financial reasons. George Borrow once stated that there are worse avenues to wealth than marrying into it, and perhaps this holds some truth, though I find Borrow’s own experiences less than convincing. I have encountered individuals who pursued wealth and, in doing so, fell genuinely and passionately in love. However, it is essential to ensure that monetary gain is not the primary motive; otherwise, regret is sure to follow. Mr. Bumble married Mrs. Corney for "six teaspoons, a pair of sugar tongs, and a milk-pot, with a small quantity of secondhand furniture and twenty pounds in money." Just two months later, he lamented his decision, admitting he had gone "dirt cheap." "Only two months to-morrow," he mused, "It seems an age."

Those who subscribe to the notion of "love at first sight" believe that marriages are divinely orchestrated and that our earthly existence is merely about fulfilling that destiny. Johnson, who was a devoted husband to the elderly Mrs. Porter, scoffed at this belief, asserting that love is merely a product of circumstances. While this practical viewpoint is sensible, there are notable exceptions, such as the tales of Dante and Beatrice or Abelard and Heloise, where passion seems to ascend to the heavens. However, such relationships rarely conclude in happiness. A more mundane approach to love appears more suited to earthly realities. 

Sir Thomas More's methods of courting were perhaps the most unusual on record. He had developed a preference for the second of three sisters and was poised to marry her when he experienced a moment of realization—But let me share the words of Roper, his son-in-law, who documented the event:

"And all beit his mynde most served him to the seconde daughter, for that he thoughte her the fayrest and best favoured, yet when he considered that it woulde be bothe great griefe and some shame alsoe to the eldest to see her yonger sister in mariage preferred before her, he then of a certeyn pittye framed his fancye towardes her, and soon after maryed her."

This illustrates a calculated form of love, yet it resulted in one of the most beautiful family lives imaginable, centered around this remarkable figure of English heritage.

In conclusion, there exists no universal formula for falling in love. Each individual embarks on that journey guided by their own spirit and circumstances.
