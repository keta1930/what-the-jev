The conclusion that we had to relinquish the governess-cart signified that a new home for Polly needed to be secured.

Polly is a roan pony, notably plump, and particularly so in recent weeks due to the limited diet of meadow grass. She had been a part of our lives for so long—this is a tale relayed to me by my amiable neighbor during the War, a delightful person known for her patience, especially in croquet—that Polly had become, in the way that ponies often do, a cherished family member. Thus, the thought of parting with her felt like an act of betrayal. However, necessity is a compelling force that disregards both sentiment and nostalgia, prompting us to start the uncomfortable process of preparing for her sale. The first step involved crafting an advertisement, a task that proved to be more complex than it initially appeared. The choice of words was crucial; we needed a description that would both entice potential buyers and remain honest. 

We pondered what traits are most sought after in a pony. Speed? Polly was quite adamant about adhering to her own leisurely pace, particularly on trips out. Willingness? She had a tendency to be quite obstinate. Strength? Yes, she was robust. Youth? Well, she had joined our family a decade ago and was no longer a young foal. After much serious contemplation, which could be likened to the deliberations at Versailles, we settled on describing Polly as either a "strong useful pony" or a "useful strong pony." Eventually, we agreed on the phrasing "Pony, strong, useful" and sent the advertisement off to the local paper, often humorously referred to as the county chronicle.

The next pressing matter was determining an appropriate price. To navigate this, we sought the counsel of Mr. Edmead, the local butcher, a man whose expertise with ponies is unrivaled. 

"Considering all factors," he stated, "I believe twenty-five pounds would be a fair asking price."

We exchanged glances, astonished. Twenty-five pounds! We had initially purchased her for just nine pounds all those years ago. Why had we not taken up pony breeding as a hobby? Mr. Edmead elaborated that the War had inflated the value of ponies. Yes, given the circumstances, he affirmed that twenty-five pounds was indeed a fair price, and he would have been willing to pay that amount himself if he needed a pony at the time; however, he was currently well-equipped and thought Polly might not be fast enough for his needs. With that, he bid us good day.

Those seeking to buy a pony share a striking similarity. They are clean-shaven, donned in hard round hats, and their overcoat collars often hang haphazardly, half up and half down. They carry sticks, yet despite their desire for a pony, they often wish for one at a lower price. Each man who came to inspect Polly echoed a similar sentiment: while they recognized her as a strong useful pony, she ultimately did not meet their needs. Day after day, Polly was scrutinized. Buyers examined her mouth, shook their heads, felt her knees and hocks, scrutinized her from various distances, rattled their sticks in their hats, and prodded her unexpectedly, yet none made a purchase.

As time wore on, desperation began to set in. While we were fond of Polly, the urgency of selling her grew stronger. We longed for a swift resolution. Then, a market gardener named Fox, a jovial and portly man, arrived. Polly was presented once more, subjected to the extensive evaluation rituals of pony buyers. Everything seemed to be progressing smoothly, until an unexpected interruption occurred.

You might wonder about this newcomer, Vivian. We could have anticipated the arrival of new characters, but this tale is not solely mine; it belongs to my delightful neighbor.

Vivian is a small boy who had known Polly throughout his life. By some unfortunate twist of fate, he wandered out from his lessons at the precise moment when Mr. Fox, clearly interested in Polly, was weighing the decision to go through with the purchase. I should clarify that Vivian possesses a charm that leads him to view the world as a playground designed for making friends, drawing him to laborers who work outdoors. The moment he spotted Mr. Fox's kind face, he eagerly added him to his list of acquaintances.

"Run along, Vivian," I instructed. "It isn’t playtime yet, and we have important matters to attend to."

"Are you going to buy Polly?" he boldly asked Mr. Fox, countering my request to leave.

"I was considering it," Mr. Fox replied, turning to me. "How old do you estimate she is? She appears to be around twelve."

That estimate felt too low, yet I nodded in agreement. However, Vivian blurted out, "Oh, mother, Mr. Brooks insists she’s seventeen if she’s a day, and I’m sure she’s at least that!"

Mr. Fox's demeanor shifted to one of contemplation. "Mr. Brooks said that, did he?" he inquired.

I hesitated to instruct Vivian to return inside, fearing it would show my anxiety over his honesty; truthfully, I was quite apprehensive. All I could do was hope for a favorable outcome.

"She’s quite docile; accustomed to traffic and so forth?" Mr. Fox probed.

Vivian then began to chuckle. This nostalgic laughter, reflecting on past events—he often revisits old stories, and his giggles are quite endearing; however, in this moment, I felt a knot tighten in my stomach.

"Don't you remember, mother?" he started, "that time she was startled by the traction engine and dashed into the grocer's shop?"

Mr. Fox, whose large hand now held my son's tiny one, adopted a serious expression.

"That would be a liability in my line of work," he commented.

I quickly interjected, "Oh, that was ages ago! She’s quite steady now. Remember, Vivian, it was on your fifth birthday when that happened?"

"No," Vivian insisted, "that was my seventh birthday—something amusing always occurs on my birthdays," he explained to Mr. Fox. "It was on my fifth birthday that Polly fell down."

"She’s fallen, has she?" Mr. Fox said with an ominous tone.

The ensuing exchanges grew increasingly disheartening. It was never my intention to withhold any details; Mr. Edmead was fully aware of Polly’s history when he provided his valuation. Yet, Vivian’s presence made me acutely self-conscious, and I felt the heat rise to my face, fearful of inadvertently suggesting any form of deceit.

Mr. Fox, to his credit, navigated the situation skillfully. He refrained from pressing Vivian for more information and diverted the conversation to other topics. Still, I could sense his mind calculating the diminishing value of Polly with each passing moment.

In the end, Mr. Fox departed about ten minutes later, leading Polly away and leaving a cheque in her place. However, it was not for the twenty-five pounds we had anticipated—Vivian had ensured that.

What is the lesson to take away from this experience? The takeaway is this: when your spouse is serving in Mesopotamia and the time comes to sell the pony, it’s best to keep your cherubic child securely locked away in the nursery.
