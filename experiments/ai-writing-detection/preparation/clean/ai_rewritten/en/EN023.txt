Recently, while contemplating the motion of trees swaying and the unseen force of the wind, I found myself in a reflective mood, considering how the visible world can often be influenced by that which is not seen. This metaphor arose quite naturally as I penned my thoughts in a wooded area. However, upon returning to Fleet Street, a place I find infinitely more vivid and poetic than any wilderness, I was unexpectedly struck by the significance of that metaphor. The figures of people around me took on the appearance of a forest, with their souls resembling the gentle breezes that navigate through it. Each individual I encountered seemed like a tree, uniquely expressive. The man engaged in conversation with me appeared as an articulate tree, while the van driver, animatedly gesturing for me to move aside, resembled branches dancing in a gust of wind, a natural spectacle I could observe with ease. The policeman, raising his hand to caution approaching buses of the danger posed by my presence, seemed like a shrub momentarily disturbed by a strong current of human law, an authority stronger than the chaos of anarchy. 

Eventually, this woodland imagery faded, but the stark dichotomy between the tangible and the intangible lingered in my mind. Just when Fleet Street, with its familiar yet bewildering brightness, became mundane, a striking violet poster caught my eye. Bold black letters proclaimed an intriguing question: “Should Shop Assistants Marry?”

The moment I read those words, it felt as if the world had been turned upside down. The figures of Fleet Street appeared as if they were walking on their hands, and the iconic cross of St. Paul’s seemed suspended in the air in an unnatural position. I realized that I had entered a realm where the belief prevailed that the visible world, represented by the swaying of trees, dictated the existence of the wind. In essence, material conditions, no matter how twisted or bleak, were regarded as more significant than the underlying spiritual truths, regardless of their purity and strength. 

What would various historical periods or philosophical schools have made of such a question? The ascetics from Eastern traditions or early Christian eras might have interpreted it to mean that shop assistants were too elevated in spirit to engage with romantic feelings. Yet, I doubt that’s the intention of the purple poster. In certain ancient societies, it could have posed a question about whether individuals of such lowly status as shop assistants should even be allowed to propagate their lineage. However, I fear we must confront the disturbing reality of what the poster implies. This inquiry genuinely reflects a segment of society questioning whether fundamental human relationships between the sexes contribute positively to the modern commercial environment. It invites us to consider whether the archetypal figures of Adam and Eve are well-suited for the setting of Marshall and Snelgrove. If this perspective isn’t absurd, I struggle to define what is. We are contemplating whether an age-old institution could enhance our (hopefully transient) institutional framework. I have encountered similar inquiries in my experiences; for instance, a man once asked, “Does Democracy benefit the Empire?” This question parallels saying, “Is art advantageous to frescoes?”

I assert that numerous such inquiries exist. However, if the world were to run short of them, I could easily propose a host of comparable questions, grounded in the same rationale. 

“Do Feet Enhance Boots?”—“Is Bread More Delicious When Eaten?”—“Should Hats Contain Heads?”—“Do People Deteriorate a Town?”—“Do Walls Damage Wall-papers?”—“Should Neckties Secure Necks?”—“Do Hands Impede Walking-sticks?”—“Does Burning Consume Firewood?”—“Is Cleanliness Beneficial for Soap?”—“Can Cricket Truly Improve Cricket-bats?”—“Should We Include Brides with Our Wedding Rings?” and many others.

None of these inquiries differ in intellectual substance or merit from the question posed by the violet poster, nor do they stray from the nature of typical questions put forth by many contemporary economists. Each inquiry reflects this same foundational absurdity. They don’t ask whether the means align with the end; instead, they display a deep-seated skepticism by inquiring if the end is compatible with the means. They never consider whether the tail suits the dog; rather, they ponder if a dog is the most aesthetically pleasing accessory to attach to a tail. Ultimately, instead of examining whether our modern structures—streets, trades, agreements, laws, and concrete institutions—are appropriate for a wholesome human existence, the discourse rarely includes the concept of healthy human life. When it does, it is only as an afterthought, merely questioning whether that thriving existence is appropriate for our streets and businesses. 

Perfection, as a goal, may or may not be achievable. It could be feasible to discuss imperfection as a step towards perfection. However, it seems beyond reason to suggest that perfection serves as a pathway to imperfection. The New Jerusalem may be either a tangible reality or a mere fantasy, but it strikes me as utterly outrageous to claim that the New Jerusalem is a genuine destination on the road to Birmingham.

This situation encapsulates one of the most profound yet concealed tyrannies of modern materialism. The concept should, in theory, be straightforward. A genuinely human individual would instinctively prioritize spiritual matters. At a specific moment, a person—an embodiment of divine potential—might find himself working as a shop assistant. Within him lies a capacity for profound love, an innate desire for fatherhood, and a quest for loyalty that unifies his life. Typically, he would consider how the current conditions of shop work align with his epic destiny concerning love and marriage. Yet, as I’ve indicated, modern materialism exerts its quiet yet suffocating influence. It stifles his impulse to rebel. By continually emphasizing the environment and visible realities, discussing economics and physical constraints, and creating an incessant narrative filled with iron machinery and relentless engines, modern materialism ultimately shapes a distorted perception in which the truth becomes inverted. 

In the end, the individual does not ask, as he rightly should, “Should married men tolerate being shop assistants?” Instead, he poses, “Should shop assistants marry?” Materialism achieves its deceptive triumph. The enslaved individual no longer questions, “Are these chains deserving of me?” Instead, they contentedly and scientifically inquire, “Am I even worthy of these chains?”
