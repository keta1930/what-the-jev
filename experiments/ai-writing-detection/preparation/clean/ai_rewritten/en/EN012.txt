At times, especially when a deadline looms and an article that should have been submitted the previous day is still unwritten, I find myself at my desk pondering whether there exists any conceivable topic I could articulate something about. On one such occasion, I relinquished the decision to chance, and Fate, through a randomly opened dictionary, directed me to contemplate goldfish. (You will discover that piece later in this collection.) However, today, I find myself free from such dilemmas. Today, I am untroubled. The reason for my contentment? I have a new nib in my pen.

Typically, when a playwright like Shakespeare crafts a tragedy or when Mr. Blank pens one of his delightful essays, a considerable amount of contemplation precedes the actual writing. One cannot simply produce lines such as "Scene I. An Open Place. Thunder and Lightning. Enter Three Witches," or "As I look up from my window, the nodding daffodils beckon to me to take the morning," spontaneously. At least, that is the case for most writers. Yet, with a fresh nib in my possession, I can transition effortlessly from breakfast to the blotting paper, as if a new sheet of foolscap is transformed by an enchanting flow of blue-black ink. When poets and dreamers extol the joy of writing, they refer to the satisfaction of sharing their thoughts with the world; with an old nib, this can be a laborious task. They do not comprehend (as I do) the delight an artist experiences when witnessing elegantly formed "k's" and graceful "s's" emerge under their steel tip. While others might ponder, "Will my audience appreciate this?" I find myself thinking, "How will the typesetters delight in this?"

Nevertheless, it is possible that the typesetters will not be as enamored as I hope. Perhaps my work may be somewhat beyond their comprehension. I recall receiving an anonymous postcard on a previous New Year's Day. It wished me well and encouraged me to extend my generosity to the typesetters by writing with more abundance. At that time, I managed to fit a thousand words onto a sheet measuring 8 by 5 inches. While I took the advice to heart, it was a significant adjustment—akin to an artist of miniatures being compelled to spend their life painting the Town Council of Boffington in the style of Herkomer. My canvases may be larger now, yet they remain impressionistic. "Pretty, but what exactly is it?" is the inevitable reaction; one steps back to assess the piece and gestures with a hand, saying to a companion, "You see it better from this distance, my dear." However, should there be a typesetter who, amidst the chaotic pace of life, takes a leisurely moment—perhaps during lunch—to examine the exquisite words with an artist's eye rather than a laborer's perspective, I believe they will find satisfaction. They will share in my joy over my new nib. So, does it truly matter what those who only glimpse the printed text think of it?

A woman once approached me, claiming she could discern my character through my handwriting, a discipline she referred to as the science of calligraphy. I prepared a special sample, filling it with thoughtful phrases like "To be good is to be happy," "Faith is the guiding star of life," and "We should always show kindness to animals." I wanted to impress her. After dedicating her morning to this task, she informed me at lunch that I was "synthetic." You might assume the compositor misprinted "synthetic" when I had intended to convey "sympathetic." Initially, I misunderstood her, striving to appear as sympathetic as possible. Yet, she reiterated "synthetic," leaving no room for confusion. I urged her to elaborate, expecting that each letter would unveil a deeper truth, but all she offered was "and not analytic." For the remainder of the day, I reveled in the idea, repeating to myself, "I am synthetic! I am synthetic! I am synthetic!" while lamenting, "Alas, I am not analytic!" I had no understanding of what that meant.

What led her to deduce my purported synthetic nature? Merely the fact that, in a bid to save time, I tend to join some of my words. This act does not indicate synthetic behavior; rather, it signifies a busy individual. What she should have articulated was, "You are a person of action, constantly in motion; undoubtedly, you possess a commendable character and are kind to animals." Then, one would feel their writing was not in vain.

As my pen begins to tire and loses some of its initial vibrancy, I find I can still continue. During my school days, I had a friend whose uncle manufactured nibs. If you perceive any scholarly insight within this essay, it is thanks to that boy. He once recounted how many nibs his uncle produced annually; thankfully, I have forgotten the exact number. It must have been thousands. Each term, he arrived back at school with a hundred of these nibs; one would expect him to be quite industrious. After all, if one lacks the intellect or inclination to engage in serious work, possessing nibs is certainly something. However, those nibs were ultimately utilized for more playful purposes. There existed a game whereby you flicked your nib against another boy's nib, and if luck was on your side, you could capsize his nib, adding it to your own collection. The game involved a fair amount of strategy, the details of which have since faded from my memory. I suspect it could even be played at the Admiralty during the off-season. Another playful activity involved inserting a clean nib into your pen, lightly touching it to the cheek of an unsuspecting boy with his back turned, then calling out to him suddenly. As Kipling noted, we are indeed the only truly humorous race. Tragically, that boy's uncle passed away a couple of years later, leaving behind around £80,000, but none of it went to his nephew. Of course, he had received the nibs regularly each term, so we mustn’t overlook that.

The nib I am currently using is dubbed the "Canadian Quill," likely crafted from some steel goose that roams the distant North, a fact that Canadian housewives must explain to their husbands every Michaelmas. This nib has now carried me through everything I intended to express today—if indeed there was anything I felt compelled to communicate. In truth, this morning, the sheer act of writing was sufficient for me; with the spring breeze flowing through the open windows and my trusty Canadian quill in hand, I could have transcribed a directory. This encapsulates the true joy of writing.
