It seems that the confession-book has vanished from existence. It has been two decades since I last encountered one. In my youth, I would share with curious owners my favorite foods—porridge, if I recall correctly—my admired heroes from both reality and fiction, my preferred virtues in women, and various other trivialities. At that age, these preferences seemed inconsequential, as I was destined to outgrow them. However, for the journalist of that era, a commitment to such definitive opinions could be perilous! If a writer were to declare in print, as I am about to do, that the daffodil is his flower of choice, merely because he spotted a lovely bowl of them on his desk while seeking inspiration, it would be unfortunate for him if a confession-album owner later revealed that he had previously sworn allegiance to the violet. Such a predicament could stifle imaginative expression. Luckily for me, I face no such constraints, and I can boldly state that the daffodil is, and has always been, my favorite flower. While many might argue in favor of the rose, I contend that the joy the daffodil brings is unparalleled, much like the pleasure a thousand pounds might provide to Rockefeller compared to the delight it offers to you or me. The daffodil arrives not only before the swallow, which is of little consequence since no one holds the swallow in disdain for that, but it also precedes the myriad flowers of summer, emerging right after a barren winter. In this way, it surpasses the rose, akin to the way an oasis in the Sahara eclipses champagne at a wedding.

Indeed, a favorite flower must undoubtedly be one that blooms in spring. This leaves us with a selection of the daffodil, violet, primrose, and crocus. The bluebell appears too late, and the cowslip is merely a lackluster version of the primrose; camellias, anemones, and other blooms belong to a separate category altogether. So, will you opt for the violet or the crocus? Or perhaps you are inclined to follow the legendary Disraeli and prefer primroses for your statue?

I write this as someone who spends the majority of his life in London, and for me, the violet, primrose, and crocus are all deficient in a vital quality—they don't display well. My choice of a favorite flower must include the ability to enhance my home; it needs to showcase its beauty effectively indoors, which necessitates a long stalk. Of all flowers, crocuses are least suited for picking. I acknowledge their charm as the first indication of spring that graces our parks, but I desire them to be closer than that. It is impossible to pluck a crocus and place it in water; it would be cruel to remove the primrose and violet from their natural habitat. In contrast, the daffodil eagerly invites being picked. That is precisely what it awaits.

"Long stalks, please." Who among us, when tasked by a loved one to gather flowers for the home, has not been given such a directive? And is there any stalk that can rival the daffodil’s in terms of length, sturdiness, and beauty? Other flowers require foliage to enhance their appearance, but daffodils can stand alone in a vase, their green and yellow attire bringing the essence of spring into any room. A house adorned with daffodils feels alive, regardless of whether the sun shines outside. Daffodils in a green bowl—let the snow fall, if it must.

Wordsworth composed a poem dedicated to daffodils. In fact, he wrote verses about most flowers. To be unique, a plant must surely be one that has never inspired his pen. However, he did not write about daffodils in a bowl. The daffodils I cherish are stationary, while those celebrated by Wordsworth danced along the banks of Ullswater, swaying and fluttering in the breeze. He suggests that even he might have felt jovial in their presence—a daunting thought, which makes me relieved to have my daffodils safely indoors. When he first encountered them, he gazed with wonder, unaware of the wealth that such a sight would bestow upon him. Technically, he hadn’t gained anything in that moment, but he must have recognized from past experiences with daisies and celandines that it was good for a certain amount of inspiration.

To him, a simple daffodil constituted enough material for a slender volume priced at two shillings and fourpence. 

You might argue that I am in no better position, but unlike him, I have never criticized others for merely perceiving a primrose as just a primrose.

Regardless of whether you prefer them as I do—indoors—or as Wordsworth did—outdoors—the name of the flower merits attention. Is there a more beautiful name in existence than daffodil? Say it aloud, then compare it to "agapanthus" or "chrysanthemum" or any other name you choose, and see if the daffodil does not come out on top.

Pansies, lilies, kingcups, daisies,  
Let them bask in their praise;  
As long as the sun sets,  
Primroses will enjoy their glory;  
While violets retain their place in history;  
But for filling my bowls with flowers,  
I only want the daffodil.  

As Wordsworth should have articulated.
