A newspaper has recently expressed concern over the decline of diary-keeping, prompting several readers to respond by affirming their long-standing dedication to the practice. It’s likely that many of these diaries now contain a note along the lines of “Wrote to the Daily —— to counter the claim that the diary-keeping tradition is fading.” Such minor occurrences are indeed the essence of diary entries.

This widespread observation might explain why keeping a diary has become increasingly rare; it seems that nothing significant happens in most people's lives. A diary would become more appealing if one could craft entries like these:

MONDAY.—"What an exhilarating day! Encountered a couple of hooligans on my commute and had to present my card to the police. When I arrived at the office, I was astonished to find the building ablaze, but I managed to save the confidential treaty between England and Switzerland just in time. Had the public discovered this, it surely would have led to war. At lunch, I witnessed a runaway elephant parading through the Strand. I didn’t think much of it then, but later mentioned it to my wife, who agreed it was worth jotting down."

TUESDAY.—"Received a letter from my solicitor informing me that I have inherited £1,000,000 from the will of an Australian gold-digger named Tomkins. Looking back at my diary, I see that I saved his life two years ago by diving into the Serpentine. This is quite satisfying. I was late getting to the office because I had to stop by the Palace to receive my knighthood, yet I still managed to complete a fair amount of work before being interrupted by a madman wielding a razor, who demanded £100. After a fierce struggle, I shot him. Had tea at an ABC where I ran into the Duke of —-. On my way home, I fell into the Thames, but swam to safety without any trouble."

Unfortunately, our entries rarely read like that. Instead, they tend to be rather mundane and lackluster:

MONDAY.—"This morning, I felt like staying in bed and sending an excuse to work, but after a bath and breakfast, I was fine. Worked until 1:30 and then had lunch. Afterward, I continued working until five, and on my way home, I got my hair cut. In the evening, I read A Man's Passion by Theodora Popgood. It was disappointing. Went to bed at eleven."

TUESDAY.—"Received a letter from Jane. Had a productive morning at work, and during lunch, I met Henry, who invited me to play golf with him on Saturday. I told him I was already scheduled to play with Peter but expressed interest in playing with him the Saturday after. However, it turned out that he was already playing with William then, so we couldn’t arrange anything. On my way home, I bought a pair of shoes, but they might be too tight. The salesperson assured me they would stretch."

WEDNESDAY.—"Played dominoes at lunch and won five pence."

If this type of diary is indeed falling out of favor, the world may not be missing out on much. Nonetheless, it remains a harmless pleasure for some to document their daily experiences each evening, and it might even be of interest to the diarist in the future to recall that on Monday, April 27th, they had their hair cut. Additionally, if questions later arise regarding the exact date of Henry’s passing, the diary might serve as evidence that he was alive at least until Tuesday, April 28th. While this may not be significant, it holds potential value. Yet, there exists another category of diary that carries little importance at all. I feel no need to apologize for sharing another set of entries.

MONDAY.—"Woke up at nine and found a letter from Mary waiting for me. How little we truly understand our friends! Beneath the facade of affection, there may lurk the hidden sting of jealousy. Mary informs me that she cannot contribute to my stall at the bazaar because she has her own to manage. I ate breakfast mechanically, my mind elsewhere. What is the meaning of life after all? I spent lunch contemplating the depths of existence. Afterward, I lay down for an hour to collect my thoughts. I felt anger towards Mary this morning. Ah, how trivial! Will I ever escape the confines of my own nature? Will my better self ever rise to the sublime heights of selflessness it is capable of? I got up at four and wrote to Mary, forgiving her. Today has been a wonderful day for the spirit."

It seems that many diaries capture the adventures of the mind and spirit, perhaps out of a lack of physical excitement. If one cannot recount, “Attacked by a lion in Bond Street today,” they can at least write, “Attacked by doubt at St. Paul’s Cathedral.” Most people might prefer to remain silent in the absence of a lion, choosing instead to document trivialities like “Attacked by the hairdresser with a hard brush.” Yet, there are others compelled to put pen to paper, finding that their unique experiences are often confined to their emotions.

Of course, there exists within every diarist the hope that their writings may one day be unveiled to the world. Future generations might stumble upon them, astonished by the mundane lives of the twentieth century, or their diaries might be sought after by those eager to understand the inner workings of a recently passed famous figure. Ideally, they might even share their thoughts publicly in their autobiographies.

Indeed, a diarist often keeps in mind the prospect of a future autobiography. “I remember,” they may write in that esteemed account, having completely forgotten the details, “I distinctly recall”—and at this point, they will refer back to their diary—“meeting X for lunch one Sunday and saying to him…”

What follows may not hold much significance, but it will certainly illustrate the impressive memory that the distinguished author maintains in their twilight years.
