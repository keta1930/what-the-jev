In his insightful poem, Mr. Kipling categorizes individuals into the Sons of Martha and the Sons of Mary, where the former represents those who labor tirelessly and create mechanisms for movement, while the latter embodies those who benefit from such efforts. Kipling’s emphasis leans towards the industrious, highly skilled workers rather than the quieter contributors to society. However, it is worth noting that a significant portion of Mary’s Sons—perhaps even the majority—remain at home, depending on their industrious relatives for support.

A notable subset of Martha's Sons is composed of waiters, a group that has often been overlooked by literary figures. There is a pressing need for a dedicated account of waiters, akin to the works celebrating doctors and lawyers by Cordy Jeaffreson and Dr. Doran. A compilation focusing on seasoned waiters would be ideal. These are individuals who have honed their craft over time, experienced a variety of wines, and observed the intricacies of human nature during meals. Such a volume should feature waiters from various cultures, including the seasoned professionals from England and the well-versed waiters of France, whose stories should be rich enough for any clubman to savor. Furthermore, it is essential to include the old waiters of Ireland, whose numbers are sadly dwindling. An elderly Irish man I recently conversed with nostalgically recounted the loss of these once vibrant characters, lamenting the disappearance of the humorous, independent waiters he once knew. He expressed a deep sense of loss over their absence, noting that these figures, though perhaps slow or imperfect, provided a sense of familiarity and comfort to travelers. They extended hospitality, offered sustenance (albeit sometimes tardily), quenched thirst (often more swiftly), and shared amusing banter that balanced their shortcomings.

I would like to share two anecdotes from my friend that illustrate this point. The first involves an old waiter named Dennis from Mallow, who responded to a request for brighter light in the coffee room with a humorous retort that captured the essence of his character. "Could it be made brighter?" my friend asked, to which Dennis replied, "It cannot." When pressed further about the usual lighting conditions, he wittily remarked, "It is often worse." While not a monumental tale, it highlights the delightful unpredictability of conversation one encounters in such establishments, a charm that is often absent in English service. The second story revolves around Florence, the head waiter at a yacht club in Ireland. When some newly honored honorary members invited American friends to dine with them, they realized mid-journey that this was against club rules. Upon consulting Florence, he confirmed the rule but reassured them, "There would not, sorr. The only rule in this club that is never broken, sorr, is the one which forbids gratuities to be given to the waiters." Such anecdotes capture the unique character of these waiters, who are irreplaceable in their roles.

For those Sons of Martha who thrive in the service of others, catering to the needs of the hungry, there is little reason for pity. In fact, many of them enjoy greater wealth and happiness than their patrons. When they retire, it is often into comfortable homes—sometimes even entire rows of houses they own—with a sense of financial security, spending their later years contentedly with friends, family, and a drink in hand, moving gradually towards their final days. There is no cause for sorrow here; however, my heart does go out to another group of Martha's Sons: the railway porters. These individuals help facilitate our travel and escape from home yet rarely experience such freedom themselves, constantly carrying heavy luggage or searching for elusive cabs.

The mere absence of holidays does not inherently equate to misery. Indeed, vacations can often be overrated, marked by discomfort and the need for recuperation afterward. Therefore, those who forgo holidays need not be viewed as pitiable figures. Nonetheless, I find myself feeling a twinge of sympathy for those diligent workers who seem to spend their entire lives assisting others in their departures, never enjoying such escapes themselves.

One might argue that a bathing-machine attendant, who never enters the sea, might still find satisfaction in the joy their clients derive from swimming, and their own lack of participation does not sour their enjoyment of the experience. Similarly, a waiter, who is either about to enjoy a meal or has just finished one, should not evoke pity for the consistent service they provide. However, the weary porters observed at busy stations like Victoria, especially during the summer when Londoners flock to the coast, seem to bear a burden of unfulfilled desire. I can’t help but think that some of these men might long to join the throngs on Brighton beach, yet their reality confines them to the station, facilitating others’ journeys to leisure while remaining stranded in the hustle of travel.

Like Moses, who was denied entry to the Promised Land, these porters are forever tasked with the labor of helping others reach their destinations while being unable to partake in the journey themselves. The engine drivers, stokers, and guards get to enjoy the thrill of travel, but porters are relegated to the periphery, lingering only at the edges of burgeoning adventures. Furthermore, imagine the plight of porters at the major train stations throughout London and beyond as they handle luggage labeled for exotic destinations—places steeped in allure like those associated with King Arthur, or the scenic beauty of the north, or even the enchanting landscapes of Kerry, Galway, and Connemara.

In conclusion, the intricacies of service roles reveal a profound truth about the nature of work and fulfillment. While the waiters and porters of the world provide essential functions in society, their own experiences often remain unrecognized. They embody a unique blend of humanity and labor, shaping the experiences of others while sometimes sacrificing their own desires and dreams in the process.
