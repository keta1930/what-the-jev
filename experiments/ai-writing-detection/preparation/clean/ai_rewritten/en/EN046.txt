John Stuart Mill's concern that the sounds of the piano might eventually fade away is trivial compared to my anxiety that a day will arrive when whimsical literature will vanish from the quaint old bookshops, leaving no treasures for my eyes to discover. In an effort to restore my faith in the existence of such literary gems, a friend has sent me a copy of "The Compleat English Physician, or The Druggist's Shop Opened (the like not hitherto extant)" by William Salmon, who dated his preface "From my house at the Blew Ball by the Ditch-side near Holborn Bridge, London, May 5, 1693." This comprehensive volume draws from all realms of creation—animal, vegetable, and mineral—to offer remedies for human afflictions, and in the dedication, the author extends his services to the Most Serene and Illustrious Princess Mary II., should she require his medicinal expertise.

The "Dictionary of National Biography," which omits mention of this particular work, notes that William Salmon was born in 1644 and received his education under a mountebank. After a period of travel, he established himself in London as an unconventional practitioner, dealing in pills for every ailment, complete with horoscopes. There was speculation during his lifetime that he may not have been the original source of the extensive knowledge reflected in his numerous volumes, but rather an amanuensis. The "Dictionary" corroborates this theory; however, in the preface to "The Compleat English Physician," Salmon vehemently defends himself against one critic, whom he describes as "the nasty author of an impertinent and scurrilous pamphlet," asserting that he has accumulated thirty years of practical pharmacy experience. Yet, it seems improbable that even three decades of diligent work could suffice to gather the vast array of insights found within this remarkable tome.

Despite its primary focus on medicine, Salmon inadvertently crafts an exceptionally caustic form of anti-social satire, surpassing even Jonathan Swift. Not all the bitterness found in "Gulliver's Travels" can equal the effect of this empiric's unassuming classification of humanity alongside the animal kingdom. In Book V, titled "Of Man and Beasts," he begins with the statement: "Chapter 1. Homo, Man & Woman.... They are the general inhabitants of the Universal Globe of the Earth and their food is made of Grain, Pulse, Fruits, Flowers, Roots, Herbs, and the flesh of Beasts, Fowl, Fishes, Insects, etc." He then enumerates the ailments that various parts of the human body can remedy. For instance, when reduced to powder, a person's hair can treat the Green Sickness and other basic disorders. It can also be transformed into oil to alleviate cold-related pains and stimulate hair growth in bald areas. The rest of the human body, whether in the form of powder, volatile oil, spirit, essence, salt, magistry, or balsam, is similarly beneficial for a myriad of ailments. It is both an ironic and frustrating reality that we possess within ourselves the cures for all the afflictions our bodies endure.

In most scientific fields, contemporary scholars possess more knowledge than those who came before them. Knowledge tends to build upon itself over time. However, after perusing Salmon's twelve hundred pages, one cannot help but feel relieved that the practice of medicine has become significantly more straightforward since 1693. The next time we call upon a physician, we should express gratitude for their modern incompleteness. In Salmon's era, a doctor, brimming with confidence in their comprehensive understanding, might have prescribed a treatment derived from our recently deceased neighbor.

After detailing the restorative properties of the human body, Salmon shifts his focus to Alces, the Elk; Antilopus, the Antelope; and Asinus, the Ass. Every creature serves a therapeutic purpose for humanity, but few are as valuable as the Ass. While a living donkey has its merits, it pales in comparison to the extensive benefits derived from a deceased donkey transformed into medicinal compounds. Equus, the Horse; Capra, the Goat; and Cercopithecus, the Monkey, each serve as well-stocked apothecaries in their own right. Indeed, anything that moves, whether on two legs or four, yields potent elixirs, yet it is the inclusion of humanity in this mix that is jarring. It seems appropriate for the Lord of Creation to extract remedies from his soulless inferiors, but to do so from himself is a disheartening notion.

The avian world also contributes to this pharmacopoeia. For example, the flesh of Alauda, the Lark, can alleviate colicky pains—a thought to keep in mind when visiting Ye Old Cheshire Cheese. Alcedo, the Kingfisher, when reduced to powder and combined with ground human skull and a hint of amber salt, proves effective against epilepsy. A number of swallows, when pulverized in a mortar (a grim thought indeed!), yield a substance that can prevent seizures. To restore lost memory, one must combine the heart of Hirundo, the Swallow, with filings of a human skull (Mr. Pelman's preferred choice) and dried peony roots for a powerful remedy. Even the nest of Hirundo, when fashioned into a poultice, can both soothe a quinsy and heal a serpent's bite. Likewise, the delicate systems of Rubecula, the Robin Red-breast, and Regulus, the Wren, also hold medicinal value. The flesh of Lucinia, the Nightingale, is said to help those suffering from consumption, while its gall mixed with honey creates an excellent eye wash. However, it seems unjust that songbirds should be subjected to such exploitation. "Yet," one might argue, "if the Nightingale could cure consumption, it might have saved Keats." While that's a valid point, had Keats accepted such a remedy, he may never have become the poet we cherish.

In his discussion of Lucinia, the Nightingale, Salmon makes an observation—entirely unnecessary—that sets him apart among authors. He creates a literary oddity: the most unpoetic statement ever penned. While the phrase "A Mr. Wilkinson, a clergyman," may stand as the most uninspired line in poetry, the assertion that Lucinia, the Nightingale, "grows fat in autumn," completely erases the enchantment associated with this iconic bird.
