As I navigated the winding lane that ascends the hillside towards the cottage nestled beneath the towering beech trees, a sense of gentle anticipation enveloped me, though I couldn’t quite pinpoint its source. The evening was drawing to a close, and while Venus had already faded from the sky, the moon had yet to make its appearance. The air was thick with those rich fragrances that seem to intensify under the cloak of night—those scents of summer evenings immortalized by Keats in our collective imagination.

In my mind echoed lines that capture the essence of these moments: 

I cannot see what flowers are at my feet. Nor what soft incense hangs upon the boughs; 

Suddenly, I was reminded of my last nighttime journey up this lane, two weeks prior, when the dazzling sight of white hawthorn blooms illuminated by starlight had struck me with unexpected beauty. I recognized the location well—it was just ahead, where the tall hedgerow leans over the grassy pathway, creating a verdant archway. I anticipated reaching that familiar spot soon, eager to once again experience the thrill of spring’s arrival.

However, upon arriving at the designated place, I found the white hawthorn had disappeared. The arched entrance remained, but its former splendor had vanished. My two weeks spent in the hustle and bustle of Fleet Street had stripped it of its vibrant charm, and I realized I would have to wait for the full cycle of the seasons before I could once again witness the hedgerows erupting in a frothy display. Admittedly, I continued my ascent up the lane feeling less exhilarated than before. This was partly due to the steep climb ahead, which felt particularly laborious at the end of a long workday, especially while carrying a bag.

Yet, the deeper reason for the cloud over my spirits was the absence of the hawthorn. You might call me a sentimental fool for lamenting such trivial things in this harsh world filled with pain and sorrow. Perhaps it is precisely this unforgiving reality that lends such depth to these seemingly trivial thoughts. It is through these reflections that we become acutely aware of the fleeting nature of all things. The cycles of nature intertwine comings and goings so beautifully that one cannot easily discern whether the joy of arrival outweighs the sorrow of departure. It is a continuous cycle of “Hail” and “Farewell” in one breath. Today, I heard the cuckoo calling across the meadows, and I could already detect a wavering in its second note. Soon, that second note would fade entirely, leaving the solitary call to resonate over the valley like a bell tolling the end of spring.

I pondered who among us wouldn’t want to capture those ephemeral moments of beauty if given the chance? Who wouldn’t wish to preserve the cuckoo’s twin calls, echoing eternally across summer fields, or to see the blackbird perpetually singing its joyous song after summer showers? Who can observe the daffodils swaying in cheerful dance without echoing Herrick’s timeless lament about the brevity of that very dance:

Fair daffodils, we weep to see You haste away so soon; As yet the early-rising sun Has not attain'd its noon. Stay, stay. Until the hasting day Has run But to the evensong; And, having prayed together, we Will go with you along.

Indeed, I believe Herrick would have understood my momentary sadness regarding the white hawthorn. His empathy would be clear if you recalled the second stanza, which, if you are of my kindred spirit, you can conjure without needing to consult a text.

This same awareness of the transient nature of beauty inspired John Keats’ “Ode to a Grecian Urn,” where pastoral beauty is captured in an eternal moment: 

Ah, happy, happy boughs I that cannot shed Your leaves, nor ever bid the spring adieu.

And here lies the paradox of our existence. We yearn to hold on to the fleeting beauty of nature, yet we also recognize the importance of its transience. The idea of trees that never lose their leaves and an eternal spring we never bid farewell to is alluring, but ultimately, we wouldn’t truly want it that way. The thought of little Johnny remaining perpetually at the age of ten may seem appealing, and it is natural to look back fondly at the childhood of our children, wishing to relive those moments. Yet, in reality, you wouldn’t desire for Johnny to remain ten forever—after five years of that stasis, you would wish for growth and change. For life and its beauty are dynamic, not mere images etched on a Grecian urn.

The same holds true for the spectacle of nature. If the seasons were to halt, so too would the wonder surrounding them. The thrill of hearing the cuckoo’s first call in spring or witnessing the hawthorn hedge burst into bloom would vanish. I would no longer relish the cheerful chatter of rooks in the trees come February, which heralds the arrival of spring, nor would I eagerly anticipate the sighting of the first primrose or swallow. While I would lose the pangs of “Farewell,” I would also forfeit the ecstasy of “Hail.” I would possess my Grecian urn, but at the cost of losing the magic of the vibrant, living world.

As I approached the gate, I had managed to bury my regrets over the absent hawthorn. I knew that when tomorrow arrived, new wonders would greet me in the hedgerows—the wild rose and honeysuckle, followed by the blackberries and later the bright-hued hips and haws. Even if the cuckoo’s call should fade, the thrush would take its place, and after the thrush, that steadfast little creature in the red waistcoat would continue the melody through the dark days of winter.
