Years ago, I found myself aboard one of David MacBrayne's steamers en route to a Scottish island. Among the sparse group of travelers was a fascinating individual with whom I soon struck up a conversation. He was a robust, tall figure, boasting a pointed grey beard and a voluminous head of grey hair topped with a Panama hat. He shared that he was headed to a renowned fishing lodge, a destination he frequented every August. A seasoned traveler, he had extensive knowledge of Persia and had even served in Parliament; one of his sons had participated in the siege of Mafeking. While I retained these details about his life, I never learned his name. Our discussion veered towards literature, during which I introduced him to Doughty's "Arabia Deserta."

Since that encounter, I have often thought about him, pondering his identity. Whenever I came across fishermen or individuals who might have known this remarkable character, I made it a point to inquire about him, but to no avail. Recently, however, I felt I was finally on the right path when I met someone at a club who also made it a tradition to spend a couple of weeks each year on that same Scottish island. He claimed to be familiar with everyone who had ever visited that secluded location.

Here’s how the conversation unfolded.

"If you've been visiting the island for as long as you say," I remarked, "then you might just be the person to help me solve a mystery that’s been on my mind for nearly five years. There’s a man who regularly fishes there." I proceeded to describe my fellow passenger in detail. "Can you tell me who he is?"

He took a moment to think, knitting his brows in concentration.

"Are you certain he was particularly tall?" he finally asked.

"Definitely," I confirmed.

"That’s unfortunate," he replied, "because otherwise, he might have been Sir Gerald Orpington. However, Orpington is short. He was indeed in Parliament, but a tall man wouldn’t be him."

He contemplated my description for a while longer.

"You mentioned he had been to Persia? There’s old Jack Beresford, who is tall and has a lot of hair, but I can assure you he has never set foot in Persia, and he doesn’t have a son at all. This is quite puzzling. Can you describe him once more?"

I reiterated my description, and he counted off the details on his fingers.

"Well," he said, "I would have sworn I knew every angler who ever fished at Blank, but this gentleman—oh, wait! You say he’s tall and well-built and has traveled? It must be old Carstairs, but then again, it can’t be him. Carstairs was never married and never served in Parliament."

He pondered again, furrowing his brow.

"Are you sure he wasn’t a clean-shaven, bald man with a single eyeglass?"

"Not at all," I responded.

"Because if he had been, it would have been old Peterson without a doubt."

"He was neither bald nor clean-shaven," I clarified.

"Are you absolutely certain he said Blank?" he queried after a lengthy pause.

"I am," I assured him.

"Please, describe him again. I know everyone up there; I must know him."

"He was a vigorous, bulky, very tall man," I explained, "with a pointed beard and a head of grey hair under a Panama; he frequented Blank every August. He had traveled extensively and was knowledgeable about Persia; he served in Parliament, and one of his sons was a part of the siege of Mafeking."

"I don’t know him," he concluded.

It had been established by those around me that there was a striking resemblance between my rather ordinary features and those of a visitor from the previous year—Dr. Sullivan of Harley Street. This notion had solidified into an undeniable truth, as irrefutable as a mathematical theorem. One of my acquaintances, who was also a friend of the Harley Street doctor, made this comparison a regular topic of his conversations. "Isn't this gentleman," he would say to various guests in the smoking room as they returned from nearby farms in the evenings, "the very image of Dr. Sullivan of Harley Street, who was here last year?" They would all scrutinize my face and agree that I bore a remarkable likeness. Perhaps my nose was a tad larger or there was a minor difference in our chins (he, presumably, having only two—how irritating!), but overall, the resemblance was striking.

This comparison continued for quite some time, and while I was not entirely accustomed to it, I had grown somewhat excited about the prospect of returning to London and concocting a suitable ailment that would compel me to visit my doppelgänger in Harley Street, allowing me to examine him firsthand. However, last night, my friend experienced a bit of a setback, which I hoped might redirect his conversations and provide me with some relief.

The incident transpired as usual; we were seated in the smoking room, he and I, when another local acquaintance entered—someone who had been away for a few weeks and, therefore, had not yet crossed paths with me. This detail was crucial to my friend.

Eventually, the inevitable happened. "Don't you think," my friend inquired, "that this gentleman is the very image of Dr. Sullivan of Harley Street, who was here last summer?"

"What Dr. Sullivan is that?" the newcomer asked.

"Dr. Sullivan of Harley Street, who fished here last summer. Don’t you remember him? He looks just like this gentleman."

"The only Dr. Sullivan I know," the newcomer replied, "is Dr. Sullivan of Newcastle. He’s a very old man now, and quite learned. He has a remarkable private museum. He—"

"No, no, the Dr. Sullivan I mean is from Harley Street—a specialist—who stayed at the Manor last summer and went fishing. He was a clean-shaven man, exactly like this gentleman here. Everyone has remarked on the resemblance."

"Dr. Sullivan of Newcastle has a beard," insisted the newcomer. "And he’s quite elderly by now. A fascinating individual indeed. He is a leading authority on South Sea Islanders. You should see his collection of Fiji war clubs."

"But that’s not the Dr. Sullivan I'm referring to. Surely you remember him," my friend pressed. "We all used to gather every evening, just like we are now—Dr. Sullivan of Harley Street, the specialist, who looks exactly like this gentleman. The likeness has been noted by everyone."

"Dr. Sullivan of Newcastle has a beard," the newcomer reiterated. "And he is quite old by now. A treasure trove of eclectic knowledge. He once showed me his collection of coins and medals. He has coins that date back to the Roman Emperors and fascinating stories about each one. His collection—"

"Yes, but—"

"—of idols is astonishing. You’ve never seen such comical figures worshipped by those natives. His collecting knows no bounds. He even possesses a mummy adorned with blue beads. He has skulls from various parts of the world, showcasing different formations. It has been some years since I last saw him, and he may—"

"Yes, but—"

"—be deceased. If he isn’t, he’s certainly a man worth meeting. If you ever find yourself in Newcastle, sir,"—this was directed at me—"don’t miss the opportunity to visit him. Though, he must be very old by now. He—"

At that point, I finished my drink and slipped away to bed. As I consulted the mirror while undressing, I chuckled at my reflection. "You can rest easy tonight," I remarked to myself, "for there are signs you are about to have a reprieve."
