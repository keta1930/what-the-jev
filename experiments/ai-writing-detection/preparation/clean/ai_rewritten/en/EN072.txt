Among the various columns in newspapers, one that consistently captures my interest—always excluding those I contribute to myself—is the section called "The World's Press." This feature offers a glimpse into the perspectives of publications that often fly under the radar for most readers. Recently, I stumbled upon The Shoe Manufacturers' Monthly, a periodical that the discerning crowd eagerly anticipates each month. Its former competitor, The Footwear Fortnightly, seems to have faded from relevance. One amusing tidbit from the latest edition notes that Kaffirs have a peculiar preference for boots that make noise. I mention this as a pretext to delve into the age-old conundrum of squeaky boots and collars, essentially highlighting the inherent unfairness of life.

Those well-versed in fashion—especially the majors and clubmen who regularly dispense advice on attire—have attempted to elucidate the reasons behind a squeaky collar. However, their explanations rarely satisfy anyone with a modicum of intelligence. They attribute the issue to a collar being either too large or too small, excessively dirty or overly clean. They contend that if one has collars tailored to fit (like a true gentleman), all will be well, whereas purchasing cheap, off-the-rack options is the root of the problem. They propose using a bit of soap on the outside of the shirt or suggesting various remedies, but they are fundamentally mistaken.

The straightforward reality, which most people recognize, is that some individuals are simply more prone to having squeaky collars than others. A collar that squeaks on a man is less a commentary on the collar itself and more a reflection of the man wearing it. He is, in essence, unlucky. Despite whatever advantages nature may have bestowed upon him—handsome looks or a noble character—he finds himself at odds with the inanimate objects around him.

We all know that one individual who naturally attracts the affection of children and dogs. This rare ability to inspire such fondness is a sign of good fortune. However, to gain the favor of inanimate objects is an even greater feat. A man whose collar or window sash seems to take to him instinctively is truly someone favored by luck. Imagine him for a moment: his collar remains silent, his clothes fit him perfectly, and at dinner parties, he glides directly to his seat while the rest of us awkwardly shuffle around searching for our place cards. Even the windows of taxis open effortlessly for him. When he travels by train, his luggage is always the first to emerge at Paddington. String untangles itself upon his approach, and he is the only one capable of making a lasting impression with sealing wax. If he’s asked by a hostess to ring the bell in a crowded room, the bell miraculously appears from behind the sofa, readily accessible to him. Vegetables somehow seem to arrange themselves perfectly on his plate.

It is important to note that I am not merely addressing the typical fortunate individual. Such a person might suffer losses on the Stock Exchange or experience heartbreak; they may find themselves in unfortunate situations, like a railway accident. This type of misfortune is bearable—albeit with a bit of grumbling—because it elicits sympathy from others. In contrast, the individual who suffers at the hands of inanimate objects garners no sympathy. We could be on a bus that tips over, and no one would blame us for it. Yet if our collar begins to audibly protest, we collectively assume it’s our fault for not purchasing better collars. If our dinner cards elude us or if the string of our package becomes impossibly knotted, we are labeled as clumsy. Our struggles with asparagus or macaroni earn us a reputation for poor manners, and our luggage gives us a reputation for being slow.

I believe we, the others, have every right to voice our grievances. Regardless of our successes in other arenas, the absence of affinity with inanimate objects gives us a valid reason to complain. While it may be gratifying to win £500 on the Stock Exchange purely by chance, there is always a shadow over this joy. The next £500 earned through clever investment will surely be attributed to luck. Luck receives the credit for our achievements, while the truly fortunate individual is credited solely for their good fortune. Therefore, we are justified in our complaints.

I cannot comprehend why inanimate objects seem to conspire against certain individuals. Perhaps there exists a semblance of justice in this phenomenon. It might be the case—indeed, it’s likely—that those favored by objects are despised by animals, children, and even their fellow humans. In fact, I find myself harboring dislike for such individuals. The more I ponder their existence, the more I realize that they often lack admirable qualities. They are, in essence, unremarkable. It is no wonder that they are compensated with the ability to harmonize with inanimate things, which grants them, in the eyes of the unthinking, a reputation for skill and agility. When you encounter a person known for such attributes, you may come to understand their true nature.

This morning, circumstances dictated that I write this piece while clad in a dressing gown. Now, I intend to don a collar. My hope is that it will produce a squeak.
