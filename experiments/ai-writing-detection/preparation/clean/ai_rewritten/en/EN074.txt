We’ve all encountered individuals who recount strange occurrences with a skeptical tone, insisting that despite the oddities, they do not believe in the supernatural. In stark contrast, I find myself firmly grounded in the belief of the supernatural, rooted in intellect and reason rather than personal encounters. I don’t perceive ghosts; instead, I recognize their intrinsic likelihood. My understanding is purely cerebral; my physical being is firmly tethered to this world. Yet, it is often the most peculiar experiences that leave a lasting impression on those of my mindset. The most bizarre event I have ever encountered unfolded recently, centering around my ability to engage in a game—one that I played surprisingly well for a solid seventeen minutes. I daresay, even the ghost of my grandfather would have evoked less astonishment.

On one of those brilliant, scorching afternoons, I found myself, to my utter disbelief, participating in a game of croquet. I had always assumed this pastime was a relic of the days of Leach and Anthony Trollope, and thus I had neglected to don the extravagant side whiskers that seem essential for such an occasion. I played alongside a man I shall refer to as Parkinson; our match was accompanied by a semi-philosophical debate that persisted throughout the duration of the game. I am convinced that I held the upper hand in our discussions, yet it is undeniable that I was thoroughly outmatched in the game itself.

“Oh, Parkinson, Parkinson!” I exclaimed, affectionately tapping him on the head with my mallet. “You are so far removed from the pure love of the sport! You, who can actually play! It is we, the inept, who find joy in the essence of the game. You crave glory; you seek the thunderous applause of victory; you do not truly love croquet. You cannot profess your love for the game until you have embraced the possibility of defeat in croquet. It is we, the bunglers, who worship the sport for its own sake. If we could glimpse the face of Croquet herself—if I may put it that way—we would be content to see her expression of displeasure. Our play is labeled as amateurish, yet we wear that title with pride, for 'amateur' derives from the French word for 'lover.' We accept all the encounters she offers, whether they lead to disaster or monotony. We linger outside her iron gates—those hoops, if you will—striving to gain entry. Our devoted balls, eager and chivalrous, refuse to be confined within the rigid confines of the croquet lawn. They seek honor in the far reaches of the earth; they end up in flower beds, conservatories, front gardens, and even in neighboring streets. No, Parkinson! A skilled painter possesses technique; it is the unskilled painter who cherishes his art. A proficient musician revels in his craft, while the mediocre musician loves music itself. With a pure and unrelenting passion, I revere croquet. I adore the game for its own sake. I cherish the rectangular patch of grass outlined by chalk or tape, as if its boundaries represent the frontiers of my sacred homeland, the four seas of Britain. I delight in the simple swing of the mallet, while the sound of the balls colliding is like music to my ears. The four colors resonate deeply with me, each symbolizing something profound, akin to the red of martyrdom or the white of Easter Sunday. You miss all of this, dear Parkinson. To comfort yourself for the absence of this vision, you resort to the trivial consolation of successfully navigating hoops and striking the stick.”

With that, I flourished my mallet with a lighthearted elegance.

“Don’t lament my fate too much,” Parkinson replied, his tone tinged with playful sarcasm. “I’ll recover from this in due time. However, it strikes me that the more one enjoys a game, the more one wishes to excel at it. While the pleasure derived from the activity itself is paramount, doesn’t the satisfaction of success naturally follow? Consider your own analogy of the Knight and his Lady-love. Admittedly, the gentleman’s foremost desire is to be in the presence of the lady. Yet, I’ve never heard of a gentleman who would want to appear foolish in her company.”

“Perhaps not; though he often ends up looking foolish,” I countered. “However, therein lies a flaw in the analogy, despite it being my own creation. The joy sought by the lover is a boundless happiness that can expand infinitely. Generally speaking, the more he is loved, the happier he becomes. It’s true that the deeper the love shared between both lovers, the greater the joy will be. Yet, it’s not the case that the better both croquet players perform, the more enjoyable the game becomes. It is entirely feasible—(stay with me here, Parkinson!)—to play croquet too well to actually derive pleasure from it. If you could send that blue ball through that distant hoop as easily as picking it up with your hand, you would not bother to do it; it would lose its significance. If you could play flawlessly, you would cease to play altogether. Once the game reaches perfection, it ceases to exist.”

“I don’t believe, however,” Parkinson interjected, “that you are in any immediate danger of achieving that level of perfection. I doubt your croquet will vanish due to its own excellence. You’re safe for now.”

Once again, I affectionately tapped him with the mallet, sent a ball flying, and picked up where I left off in our discussion.

As the long, warm evening gradually descended, twilight was settling in. By the time I articulated four more fundamental principles, and Parkinson managed to navigate five additional hoops, the encroaching darkness was almost complete.

“We’ll have to call it a night,” Parkinson said, missing a ball for what seemed like the first time. “I can’t see a thing.”

“Neither can I,” I replied, “and it’s somewhat comforting to recognize that I couldn’t hit anything even if I could see it.”

With that, I struck the ball decisively, sending it off into the darkness toward the hazy shape of Parkinson. He let out a loud and dramatic exclamation. The situation indeed warranted such a response, as I had struck the correct ball.

Astonished, I traversed the dimly lit ground and hit my ball again. It rolled through a hoop. I couldn’t see the hoop, but I was certain it was the right one. A shiver ran through my body.

Words escaped me, so I trudged heavily after that incredible ball. Once more, I struck it into the night, believing I was aiming in the general direction of another ball. In the oppressive silence, I heard the dull thud as the ball made contact with the stick.

I dropped my mallet, exclaiming, “I can’t endure this! My ball has gone through the hoop three times. These phenomena are not of this realm.”

“Pick up your mallet,” Parkinson urged, “and give it another try.”

“I refuse to. If I were to accomplish another hoop like that, I’d be confronted by a legion of devils dancing on the blessed grass.”

“Why devils?” Parkinson questioned. “They could be merely fairies having their fun. They’re bestowing upon you the ‘Perfect Game,’ which is no game at all.”

I glanced around, the garden enveloped in an intense darkness, where the faint glimmers appeared almost like flames. I stepped gingerly across the grass, as though it were on fire, retrieved the mallet, and struck the ball somewhere—anywhere another ball might be. I heard the dull clack of the balls colliding and dashed into the house as if I were being pursued.
