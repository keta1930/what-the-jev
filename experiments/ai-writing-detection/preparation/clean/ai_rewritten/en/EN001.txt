The editor of the Star extends an invitation as expansive as the night sky itself, offering me the freedom to select my name from the vast celestial tapestry he has assembled. He envisions my presence as a constant—shining forth every Wednesday evening, whether rain or shine, throughout the changing seasons and in all locales, until such a time arrives when he declares, "Enough!" and extinguishes the light that inevitably comes to all.

This proposal reaches me while I find solace in a quaint village nestled on a ridge of beech-covered hills, where I have sought refuge from the relentless turmoil of war and the oppressive gloom of London’s nighttime streets. Here, darkness holds no dread. Each evening, as the sun dips below the expansive plain before me, our lamps flicker to life. The frenetic pulse of Fleet Street is but a distant memory, and the echoes of war drift toward us like whispers from another realm. The highlight of my day arrives just after dusk, heralded by the sound of a whistle in our narrow lane, announcing the postman’s arrival to gather letters.

In this serene isolation, where one depends heavily on personal ingenuity, I have come to realize just how reliant I am on others—be it people or literature—for inspiration. Even the task of naming proves challenging. The process of naming is rarely an easy endeavor. Anyone who earns a living with words understands the struggle involved. I would much prefer to pen an entire article than to grapple with the title for it. While the creation of a thousand words can flow with relative ease, that succinct five-word summary that must shine like a beacon at the top is a treasure that often emerges from considerable effort and perhaps even despair. I have authored books, yet I have never managed to title one on my own; that honor has always been bestowed upon me by a friend.

Even those blessed with remarkable talent grapple with this deficiency. Consider Goldsmith, who, after crafting the finest English comedy since Shakespeare, found himself at a loss for a title, ultimately leaving it to Johnson to label his work. I find comfort in the thought that Shakespeare himself may have experienced this same creative drought—sitting in frustration, chewing on the quill while wrestling with the daunting task of naming his own creations. The evidence lies within the titles themselves. The phrase "As You Like It" serves as a testament to his frustration, indicating that he had exhausted himself in the futile search for a name. Similarly, "Twelfth Night: or What You Will" exemplifies his inability to settle on a fitting title for one of his most delightful works.

What parent does not share in this struggle? Selecting a name for a child is a weighty responsibility, one that carries implications for a lifetime and must suit the mystery of the individual as time unfolds. Many stumble by starting from misguided motivations—be it trends, personal aspirations, or fleeting interests. A child may arrive at a moment tied to a battle's news or following the release of a popular novel or perhaps when inspired by a particularly austere or noble figure. In forgetting that the child will bear the consequences of these transient impulses, one might unwittingly christen them with unwieldy names like Inkerman Jones or Kitchener Smith, or worse, Milton Spinks.

Thus, the child embarks on a journey burdened by historical associations or comparisons to heroes, real or imagined. It is possible that Milton Spinks grows to be an average individual, embodying none of the greatness of his namesake. Had he been simply called John, he might navigate life happily and unnoticed, but the lofty name of Milton clings to him like a jest from which he can never escape—even in death, as the name endures on his gravestone until time and nature finally grant it the courtesy of obscurity.

I believe it is wise to steer clear of fanciful names. Since so few children will emerge as heroes or sages, we should be cautious about branding them with expectations of greatness from the outset. Horatio was a fortuitous choice for Nelson, but how many Horatios truly achieve immortality or even deserve it? The consequences can be dire if a Horatio turns out to be a scoundrel or a coward. If young Spinks possesses any latent Miltonic brilliance, it is likely to shine through the simple name John more naturally and radiantly than through an inherited grand title. One can be as unassuming as possible, and John will still be a fitting name; conversely, one can be as illustrious as desired, and John will still gleam as resplendently as one's deeds, connecting one to a distinguished lineage represented by figures such as John Milton, John Hampden, and John Bright.

As I pen these thoughts, it dawns on me that I too must select a name and that the postman's whistle will soon pierce the evening air. I step into the orchard, seeking guidance from the stars above. The horizon still glows with a deep red from the day’s last rays, while the full moon rises majestically over the hill, illuminating the night sky, and the firmament twinkles with myriad stars.

In the tranquility of the countryside, the stars feel more familiar and approachable. In the city, our perspective is narrowed to the streets; we perceive only the pavement’s illumination and hear the relentless noise of traffic. The stars appear to be infinitely distant from our lives.

Yet here, they resemble old friends whom we can always rely on, intimate and eternal, companionable despite their distance. Orion makes his appearance over the hill, and the Pleiades sparkle brightly, while the vast expanse of the night sky is marked by the triangle formed by the Pole Star, the golden Arcturus (currently hidden from view), and the icy blue Vega. However, these celestial names fail to resonate with me. I find greater meaning in the everyday sounds that connect the spectacle of the night with the timeless essence of rural life. Arcturus, known as Alpha of the Herdsman—might that be the name I choose?

My gaze shifts westward to the Great Bear, hanging upside down as if poised to consume the earth below. Throughout history, the human imagination has played with this magnificent constellation, giving it names like Charles's Wain, the Plough, the Dipper, and the Chariot of David. I wish to let my imagination dance with it as well. At the head of the Plough, a brilliant star points toward the pole. I will tether my aspirations to that noble symbol. I shall be Alpha of the Plough.
