On this hillside, two distinct voices resonate with me. One belongs to the day, while the other heralds the night. During daylight hours, the robin maintains an unwavering melody, singing his song with relentless enthusiasm. Although his tune may not be particularly remarkable, it radiates a joy that is impossible to ignore. Regardless of the weather—be it rainy or sunny, chilly or warm—his song fills the November air from dawn until dusk. This little creature, adorned in his bright red vest, flits from tree to tree, often landing on the fence before hopping to the garden path, and finally to the door that leads into the kitchen. With a bit of encouragement, he will even come to your hand, feasting with complete trust in your intentions. After his meal, he will flit away, resuming his cheerful serenade from the fence.

Some critics have harsh words for the robin, labeling him selfish and claiming he can be difficult to live with. Yet, in my eyes, he stands as one of nature's most steadfast and uplifting companions. He embodies good news—a philosopher who asserts that we hold the reins of our destiny and that winter is precisely the moment when optimism has its greatest significance. He seems to convey that anyone can adopt a hopeful outlook when days are long, the air is warm, and food is abundant; however, it is in those bleak moments when others begin to grumble that he dons his brightest waistcoat, tunes his best whistle, and comes forth to remind us that the indomitable spirit surpasses any circumstance.

As night falls and the valley below is shrouded in darkness, another voice emerges—the sorrowful lament of the owl from the thicket beyond the orchard. He proclaims the end of the day, suggesting that all hope is lost. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." He insists that he alone remains to recount the conclusion of all matters. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." He has shared this tale countless times, yet it seems no one listens. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." The darkness envelops us, the cold wind bites, and the earth feels like a grave. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." Where have the songs of spring gone? Where are the summer leaves? "Tu-whit, tu-whoo." Where is the rosy-cheeked apple that once hung from the branch, and the butterfly that danced in the sunlight? All of it is vanished. "Tu-whit, tu-whoo … Tu-whit, tu-whoo … Tu-whit, tu-whoo…."

He can be quite a dismal companion. Many find him insufferable. Just the other evening, I was having dinner in London with a woman who owns a home in Sussex, and she admitted that she hadn’t visited in quite some time.

"I used to tolerate the owl," she confessed, "but after the war, I find him unbearable. He hoots all night and fills me with despair to the point where I feel I might lose my mind."

"And you choose to listen to the owl in London?" I inquired.

"The owl in London?" she replied, puzzled.

"Yes," I said, "the owl that hoots in Carmelite Street and Printing House Square."

"Oh," she said, "but that owl is utterly ridiculous. The country owl, on the other hand, carries such a grave demeanor."

"He expresses something rather foolishly, yet with great solemnity," I remarked.

"True, but in the stillness and darkness, there’s no apparent response to his lament."

"Madame," I suggested, "if you look up at the stars, you will discover a rather comprehensive answer."

Personally, I find the owl not only bearable but also invigorating. I appreciate the way he lays bare his pessimism. It is the vague, undefined fears that can immobilize us, yet when this owl articulates the situation in its most dismal light, I begin to feel a sense of defiance and determination. If this is the worst he can conjure, then let us strive to uncover the best and work towards achieving it. "This task is impossible," the pessimist might tell Cobden. "Indeed," Cobden would reply, "then it is all the more urgent that we start tackling it." Oh, dear owl, are you claiming all is lost? Just wait for dawn; listen to what that little fellow in the red waistcoat has to say. He holds a much brighter narrative than yours. I think I’ll retire for the night and leave you to your gloomy thoughts until the sun rises and reveals the truth of your dismal proclamations.

"Tu-whit, tu-whoo," the owl responds to me.

Indeed, dear sir, but you have uttered that lament every night I have known you, and yet the sun consistently rises, bringing spring once more, along with blooming flowers and fields bursting with harvest.

"Tu-whit, tu-whoo."

Oh, bother you! You could easily serve as a sensational headline in the Daily Mail.

There’s little doubt that the owl finds his own version of happiness. Louis XV once articulated the owl’s philosophy when he remarked, "Let us entertain ourselves by making ourselves miserable." I suspect the poor creature finds some twisted joy in his own despair. I’ve long believed that Mrs. Gummidge, in her own way, relished her misery, spreading it to those around her. I've encountered such individuals, and I'm sure you have too—those who cling to their unhappiness with the fervor of a miser. They seem to thrive in these times, exclaiming, "Tu-whit, tu-whoo! The Russians have been defeated again, or if not, they soon will be. Tu-whit, tu-whoo! We're slackers and laggards, and the Germans are overwhelming us. Tu-whit, tu-whoo. All hope is lost." Yet, I notice they enjoy their steak as much as anyone else and do not shy away from their soup, even if it is seasoned with their tears.

I find the anecdote about Stonewall Jackson and the owl particularly telling. During the first battle of Bull's Run, the owl, acting like a general, rushed up to Jackson in a panic, exclaiming, "All is lost! We’re defeated!" To which Jackson calmly replied, "If that’s true, I’d recommend you keep it to yourself." A half-hour later, Jackson's brigade made a charge that turned the tide of the battle. I can only wonder what became of the owl, but I suspect he continued to lament, "Tu-whit-ing" and "Tu-whoo-ing" until the very end. After all, an owl is simply bound to be an owl.

Ah, there is that little red waistcoat once more, singing from the fence. Let’s find him a worm, shall we?
