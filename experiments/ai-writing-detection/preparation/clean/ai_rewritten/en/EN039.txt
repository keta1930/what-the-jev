I find myself seated beneath towering trees, where a fierce wind whips through their crowns, creating a tumultuous symphony of leaves that resonates with a blend of joy and turmoil. It’s almost as if I am submerged deep in the ocean, surrounded by anchors and ropes, while the relentless sound of waves crashes above me, echoing the struggles and wrecks of mighty vessels. The wind pulls at the trees with such vigor that it seems capable of uprooting them as though they were mere tufts of grass. To capture this indescribable force in another way, I envision the trees as a band of dragons, each tethered by its tail, straining against an unseen force.

Observing these immense beings writhing under the influence of an invisible and violent energy brings to mind a memory of a young boy I once knew. During a stroll through Battersea Park on a day much like this, with dark skies and swaying trees, he exhibited a profound dislike for the wind. It blasted into his face, forced his eyes shut, and swept off his favorite hat, which he wore with great pride. If memory serves me right, he was around four years old at the time. After many complaints about the chaotic weather, he finally turned to his mother and exclaimed, “Well, why don’t you take away the trees, and then it wouldn’t wind.”

This innocent misconception is a perfect reflection of human reasoning. To someone encountering trees for the first time, it might appear that these colossal structures are simply enormous fans, agitating the air around them with their movements. It is entirely natural and understandable to assume that the trees are responsible for creating the wind. This belief is so common and justifiable that it mirrors the views of nearly all contemporary philosophers, reformers, sociologists, and politicians in our era. My young friend, in this regard, was much like many leading thinkers today, though undeniably more charming.

In the little parable he inadvertently crafted, the trees symbolize all tangible entities, while the wind represents the intangible. The wind embodies the spirit that moves freely as it wishes; the trees signify the material world that is swayed by this spirit's whims. We recognize the existence of the wind when we observe the trees on a distant hill suddenly thrashing about. Similarly, we acknowledge a genuine revolution only when we see the rooftops of a city trembling in a frenzy.

Just as the jagged silhouette of a tree morphs into wild shapes in the gusts, so too does a city rise under the influence of the spirit, forming precarious towers and unexpected spires. A revolution cannot be directly seen; it manifests only through its consequences. Crowds surging through palaces, blood flowing through the streets, the guillotine raised above the throne, a prison in tatters, a populace revolting—these scenes depict not the revolution itself but rather its aftermath.

The elusive nature of wind parallels that of a revolution; while you cannot see wind itself, you can observe its effects. Likewise, a revolution remains unseen until its impact is evident. Throughout history, no significant and decisive revolution has occurred without first experiencing unrest and the emergence of new ideologies in the realm of the intangible. All revolutions begin as abstract concepts, often intensely so.

The wind swirls above long before a single twig stirs on a tree. Thus, there is always a struggle in the heavens before the earth witnesses its conflicts. If it is right to pray for the arrival of a kingdom, it is equally appropriate to hope for the revolution that will restore that kingdom. We are justified in yearning to hear the whispers of Heaven among the trees, praying for “Thine anger come on earth as it is in Heaven.”

The fundamental human doctrine asserts that the wind is what stirs the trees. In contrast, the profound fallacy lies in the belief that the trees generate the wind. When individuals assert that material conditions solely shape moral circumstances, they negate any potential for genuine transformation. If my circumstances have rendered me entirely foolish, how can I trust that I am even correct in wishing to change those circumstances?

A person who attributes all thought to mere happenstance of their surroundings inadvertently undermines their own reasoning—including that very assertion. Recognizing the human mind as possessing ultimate authority is essential for any meaningful contemplation, including free thought. Without this acknowledgment, no reform can occur in our society or age until we understand that moral truths precede all else.

To illustrate, many of us might have encountered the ongoing debate between Socialists and total abstainers, often discussed in print and at various forums. The abstainers argue that alcohol leads to poverty, while Socialists maintain that poverty drives individuals to drink. I find it perplexing that either side accepts such simplistic, physical explanations. It seems evident that the root cause of poverty among the English working class is intertwined with the same factor that leads to alcoholism—the lack of strong civic pride and an instinctive resistance to degradation.

Understanding why vast English estates were not divided into smaller holdings like those in France reveals the underlying reasons for the greater levels of drunkenness among the English compared to the French. The Englishman, despite possessing numerous admirable traits, is often characterized by a tendency to live “hand to mouth,” where his instinct is to seek self-gratification rather than challenge his oppressor. To claim that the disparity in land ownership in England arises solely from economic factors, or that the nation’s drinking problem is purely economic, is to assert something so absurd that it demonstrates a lack of critical thought.

Yet similarly ludicrous statements abound under the influence of the naive economic theory of history. There are those who argue that all significant historical motivations stem from economic conditions, only to shout at the top of their lungs to persuade modern democracy to act on those very economic principles. The most extreme Marxists in England position themselves as a small, valiant minority, desperately trying to convince the world to act according to a theory they claim governs all behavior. The reality is that a social revolution will occur the moment it transcends purely economic motives. One cannot initiate a revolution solely to create a democracy; rather, a democracy must first exist in order for a revolution to take place.

I rise from my spot beneath the trees, as the wind and gentle rain have subsided. The trees now stand like golden columns bathed in bright sunlight. The tumult of the branches and the gusts of wind have halted together. I can’t help but ponder that there are still contemporary philosophers who might insist that the trees are the ones responsible for the wind.
