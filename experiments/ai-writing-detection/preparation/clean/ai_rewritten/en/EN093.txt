It is quite astonishing to me that there are individuals who genuinely believe that fairy tales are detrimental to children. I do not include in this group a certain man in a green tie, as I cannot truly regard him as human. However, I recently received a heartfelt letter from a woman arguing that fairy tales should not be shared with children, even if they are based on truths. Her assertion is that it is unkind to expose children to such stories because they can be frightening. One might as well argue that it is cruel to provide girls with sentimental novels since these often invoke tears. This line of reasoning stems from a profound misunderstanding of a child's nature, which has fueled numerous misguided educational theories. If we were to eliminate bogeys and goblins from children's experiences, they would simply conjure them from their imaginations. A young child, alone in the dark, can create more terrifying scenarios than the most imaginative adult. A single child can envision monsters so vast and dark that they cannot fit into any illustration and assign them names that no sane person would think to utter. Children, by their very nature, are drawn to horror; they may indulge in it even when they find it unsettling. Identifying the exact moment when true fear begins for them is as challenging as it is for us when we voluntarily step into the emotional agony of a powerful tragedy. The source of fear does not lie within fairy tales; rather, it emerges from the vastness of the human soul.

The apprehension felt by children or primitive peoples is entirely justified; they are understandably alarmed by the world around them, which can be a very intimidating place. Their discomfort with solitude is rooted in the genuine terror of being alone. Just as those without faith fear the unknown, so do the untamed souls who recognize its reality. Thus, fairy tales do not instigate fear in children nor do they introduce them to the concepts of evil or ugliness; these notions already exist within them, as they exist in the world at large. Fairy tales do not provide children with their first encounter with bogeymen. Instead, what these stories offer is a clear understanding of the possibility of vanquishing such fears. From the moment a child begins to imagine, they are already acquainted with dragons. Fairy tales introduce them to a St. George, who can slay the dragon.

The essence of fairy tales lies in their ability to present a series of vivid images that teach children that these boundless terrors indeed have limits, that for every shapeless foe, there exists a valiant knight of God ready to confront it. There is something in the universe that transcends darkness and is more potent than the most intense fear. As a child, I would gaze into the dark until it morphed into a colossal figure, a giant more enormous than the sky. If a single star shone above, it transformed that figure into a Cyclops. However, fairy tales restored my mental equilibrium, for the following day, I would encounter a tale about a giant with a single eye who was outsmarted by a young boy, like myself, who, despite his inexperience and lower social standing, triumphed with a sword, some clever riddles, and a courageous heart. At times, the ocean at night appeared as daunting as any dragon, yet I was familiar with many youngest sons and little sailors who found dragons as commonplace as the sea itself.

Consider, for instance, one of the most disturbing tales from the Brothers Grimm, “The Boy Who Could Not Shudder.” This story is filled with unsettling imagery and incidents. I distinctly remember the part where a pair of legs tumbles down the chimney on their own and walks around the room until they are reunited with the head and body that later fall in after them. It’s a chilling moment, but the true significance of the story—and the reader's emotional response—does not arise from the terror itself, but rather from the astonishing fact that the protagonist remains utterly unafraid. The most remarkable aspect of this story is the hero's complete absence of fear. He playfully engages with the bogeys and invites devils to share a drink with him. Many times during my youth, when I felt overwhelmed by the darkness of modern life, I wished for a double portion of his fearless spirit. If you have yet to discover the conclusion of his tale, I urge you to do so; it contains profound wisdom. Ultimately, the hero learns to experience fear through an encounter with a wife who douses him with cold water. In that succinct moment, there is more truth about the essence of marriage than in countless treatises on sex that span Europe and America.

Guarding a child's bedside are the figures of Perseus, Roland, Sigurd, and St. George. If we remove these heroic protectors, we are not helping the child grow rationally; instead, we are leaving them to combat the devils on their own. Tragically, we have always believed in these devils. While the elements of hope in our universe have been continuously challenged and reaffirmed in modern times, the existence of hopelessness has never been questioned. As I mentioned to “H. N. B.” (whom I take this moment to wish a Happy Christmas in its most superstitious sense), the one belief that modern society seems to share is that of damnation. The most significant modern poets encapsulated this sentiment with the profound line:

“There may be Heaven; there must be Hell.”

The pessimistic view of existence has been a longstanding tradition, and new forms of spiritual exploration tend to begin with a sense of gloom. Not too long ago, people believed in no spirits. Now, there is a slow resurgence in the belief in spirits, albeit at a cautious pace.

Some have criticized spiritualism, table-tapping, and similar practices as lacking dignity, claiming that the spirits involved engage in trivialities, such as cracking jokes or dancing with furniture. I do not share this viewpoint at all. I would prefer that these spirits exhibit even more humor than they do. It is my belief that they should embrace levity and produce better jokes. Much of contemporary spiritualism, particularly its newer expressions, tends to be somber and heavy. Some ancient deities were known for their wildness, and some saints came across as overly serious; yet the spirits of today often embody a disturbing combination of both lawlessness and solemnity. Indeed, the spirits commonly encountered today are not merely devils; they are also blue devils. This encapsulates the true essence of Christmas; as long as the mythology endures, it serves as a form of joyful mythology. Personally, I maintain my belief in Santa Claus, but I am willing to extend forgiveness to those who do not share this belief during this season of grace. 

For anyone who fails to grasp the shortcomings of our world, which I am striving to address, I would recommend reading Henry James's tale, “The Turn of the Screw.” It stands as one of the most impactful works ever penned, though I often question whether it should have ever been published. The narrative depicts two innocent children gradually becoming both all-knowing and foolish under the influence of the vile spirits of a groom and a governess. My reservations about whether James should have made this work public are profound (and no, it is not indecent; it pertains to spiritual matters), yet my uncertainty is so significant that I am willing to give this truly great author the benefit of the doubt. I would wholeheartedly support this work and admire it if he could write another equally compelling story featuring children and Santa Claus. If he finds this impossible, then it becomes evident that we have the capacity to tackle dark mysteries, but we struggle with those filled with joy. In this way, we are not rationalists; rather, we are diabolists.

As I pondered these thoughts while gazing into a large, fiery red blaze that stood before me like a great red angel, I realized that you may not be familiar with the concept of a red angel. However, I am certain you have encountered a blue devil—this is precisely the distinction I seek to articulate.
