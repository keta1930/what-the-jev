When the eighteen-handicap golfer peruses the impressive achievements of renowned players, he feels no jealousy. Over time, he has unraveled the profound allure of golf. Initially, he pondered what made it so captivating; now, he understands that golf enjoys its popularity because it is the ideal game for those who struggle with it.

Take, for instance, the experience of being poor at cricket. You've invested in a new bat, perfectly balanced; a pristine pair of pads; and gloves featuring the latest innovations. Are you allowed to showcase them? Certainly not. After just one delivery, during which your bat, pads, and gloves remain untouched, you’re sent back to the pavilion to endure an afternoon filled with tedious tales from an elderly gentleman who once knew Fuller Pilch. When your team fields, where are you positioned? Likely at long leg, exposed to the audience as possibly the worst fielder in the city. The emotional toll is crushing—regret, frustration, humiliation, and above all, envy of those fortunate souls who play effortlessly on the hallowed grounds of Lord's.

Now, think about being inept at lawn tennis. While you are permitted to hold onto your new racket throughout the match, how frequently do you get to use it effectively? Your partner often shouts “Mine!” and shoves you aside. Is there any joy in playing football poorly? You might wear your new boots for the entire match, yet your interactions with the ball remain minimal. In football, the ball doesn’t belong to you.

In stark contrast, golf welcomes the less skilled player. In golf, the less proficient individual receives the most strokes. Regardless of the opponent’s skill level, the novice golfer has the right to complete each hole, thus enjoying more of the game. There’s no need to worry that their new driver will sit idle; they are guaranteed ample opportunity to swing it, perhaps even more than the proficient player if they miss the ball entirely on a tee. A new niblick promises immediate enjoyment right from the first round.

Moreover, there's a unique aspect of golfing mediocrity—the poor player can occasionally replicate the feats of the skilled player. While a struggling cricketer may never have scored fifty runs in his life, the moment he steps up to bat, he is acutely aware that today will not be his day. In contrast, the eighteen-handicap golfer has at some point played every hole on the course flawlessly. He has struck a ball 250 yards, executed remarkable approaches, and drained long putts. Any of those impressive moments could happen again at any time. Thus, he doesn't have to languish in the club’s smoking room after a second round, listening to others recount their remarkable exploits. He can join the conversation too, truthfully claiming, “I once carried the ditch at the fourth with my second shot,” or “I remember when I drove into the bunker guarding the eighth green,” or even “I made a three at the eleventh today,” with five being the bogey score. In contrast, a poor cricketer claiming, “I remember scoring a century in forty minutes off Lockwood and Richardson,” would surely be labeled a liar.

For these reasons and others, golf stands out as the most enjoyable game for those who struggle with it. At times, I feel compelled to suggest that it may even be more rewarding for the less skilled player than for the adept one. The thrill of hitting a straight drive after a week of slicing, the satisfaction of landing a mashie shot precisely where intended, the delight of even a decent stroke with a brassie—most notably, the sheer joy of executing a flawless cleek shot—are experiences that elude the skilled player. Every stroke taken by those of us who are less proficient is filled with hope. While it may not be perfect, it could always be worse, and there’s a constant belief that improvement is just around the corner. When a subsequent stroke is executed well, it fills us with immense joy. We enthusiastically convince ourselves that soon, all our strokes will resemble that one.

What does a player of Vardon’s caliber know of such delight? If he completes a five-hole in four, he chastises himself for not achieving it in three; if he finishes in five, he is despondent. He will never experience the pleasant surprise that accompanies a well-executed stroke. Instead, it’s only his missteps that surprise him, and one can only assume that this does not bring him joy. His precision and distance are mechanical, lacking the spontaneity that often characterizes our best moments, which might stem from a newly realized principle or a sudden revelation. The only variable in his game is his putting, and putting is, after all, a game more akin to croquet than golf.

Nevertheless, we too aspire to reach Vardon’s level one day. We merely delay this pursuit because, for now, it is so enjoyable to be less than perfect. There’s an enchanting element in being an average golfer: in an instant, overnight, one might transform into a skilled player. If a struggling cricketer approached a proficient one and asked, “What am I doing wrong?” the response would likely be blunt: “Nothing specific, except that you can’t play cricket.” However, if I were to ask our scratch golfer friend, “What am I doing wrong?” he would immediately offer guidance—"You’re moving your head," or "You need to drop your right knee," or "You’re not getting your wrists in quickly enough." By the next day, we could emerge as different players. The path to excellence in golf, as perceived by the eighteen-handicap player, seems to hinge on such small adjustments.

Thus, we contentedly embrace our current shortcomings while being optimistic about our potential for improvement. Perhaps a bit more certainty in getting the ball off the first tee would be nice; perhaps at the fourteenth hole, where the public often encroaches, we would prefer to avoid the embarrassment of topping the ball; and perhaps— 

Well, perhaps we could aim to lower our handicap to fifteen this summer. But no lower; surely, no lower.
