The enduring jest about the Christmas edition of magazines is that it is concocted in July and finalized in September. This planning allows for its release in mid-November, ensuring that it reaches New Zealand in time for Christmas. If it were to be published in England during the holiday season, New Zealand would have to wait until February for its arrival. Apparently, it is deemed more crucial for the colonies to receive their copies punctually than for those in England to have theirs.

Regardless of when the publication is completed, journalists universally dread the Christmas issue. Their disdain stems from one primary reason: they must simultaneously produce the regular weekly edition. As a journalist myself, I often wish to dedicate the entire autumn season solely to crafting the Christmas number, and as a member of the public, I would eagerly anticipate its arrival. Since I haven't been tasked with creating such an edition, I can entertain myself by outlining my vision for it. I adhere to the cherished tradition of storytelling. Let’s start with the first narrative. 

The opening story revolves around an escaped convict. The protagonist, a young woman returning home from a country house ball where she has received several marriage proposals, suddenly encounters a figure emerging from the shadows of the snow-covered moor, brandishing a pistol at the coachman. Panic ensues. “Oh, sir,” she pleads, “spare my aunt, and I will give you all my jewels.” To her astonishment, the convict steps back in recognition. “Lucy!” he exclaims. “Harold!” she breathes. The aunt, overwhelmed by the drama, faints. At this juncture, the narrative pauses to clarify Harold’s unusual attire of knickerbockers. He could either have been wrongfully accused or perhaps he was a disgraced solicitor. Regardless, he has suffered enough for his past mistakes, and Lucy's love for him endures. “Get in,” she insists, and they drive away together, with Harold departing for New Zealand the following day in a standard lounge suit. As for Lucy joining him later, that conclusion is left to your imagination. The End.

Now, let’s move on to the second story, a nostalgic tale steeped in the charm of bygone days. Although it may not fit the seasonal theme perfectly—most of its action unfolds in an aristocrat’s garden, filled with the fragrance of blooming roses—it evokes the romantic past when battle and bravado held a more picturesque allure and women busied themselves with needlework. This type of story is best enjoyed by the warmth of the Christmas fire; it speaks of yesteryears, making it a fitting addition to a Christmas edition. I won’t delve into the specifics of the plot, as they are secondary; what truly matters are the quaint expressions and the genteel manners depicted. However, I can reveal that the couple does marry in the end, and he embarks on his adventures in the military with a joyful heart.

Next, we require another tale. What theme should this one explore? It could feature an amateur burglar, a small child helping old Sir John accept his daughter's marriage, a ghost haunting Enderby Grange, a millionaire's lavish Christmas feast, or a mishap involving the Scotch express. Personally, none of these resonate with me; I favor the story set on a deserted island. Lady Julia, aloof and proud, accidentally tumbles off the deck of a ship, and Ronald, who had been rejected by her that very morning, plunges into the water to rescue her as she sinks for the third time. The night is foggy, and their absence goes unnoticed. When dawn breaks, they find themselves stranded on a small coral reef. Although they are not in peril—several ships are expected to pass by soon, and Ronald’s pockets are stuffed with biscuits and chocolate—the situation is awkward for Lady Julia, who had hoped to avoid him. Thus, they sit back to back on the beach, exchanging sarcastic remarks. Ultimately, Ronald manages to soften her haughty demeanor—perhaps by cleverly hiding the turtle eggs from her—and the next passing liner returns the newly reconciled couple to civilization.

But let’s not forget the poetry. I propose to include one serious poem focused on robins, accompanied by a whimsical double-page illustration of humorous verses. I believe the playful verses should center around hunting. While humor and hunting don’t always mix well—typically, there are only two hunting jokes: one about the horse that fell at the brook and another involving a Cockney rider who overshot the hounds—there are traditions that demand preservation, and artists have a fondness for the theme. Thus far, we haven’t given enough thought to the artist’s contributions. I envision four full pages dedicated to illustrations: one depicting lovely young women hanging mistletoe, another showcasing the squire and his family trekking to church through the snow, a third illustrating a broken coach with highwaymen approaching, and the last featuring the postman delivering a multitude of parcels. Together, these four images encapsulate the essence of Christmas. However, there is space for one more page—a color page filled with a handful of sketches, styled in early English lettering. “Ye Baron de Marchebankes calleth for hys varlet.” “Ye varlet cometh righte hastilie—-” You can picture the charming essence of it.

I must admit that this is the kind of Christmas edition I truly cherish. You might argue that it’s a familiar concept, but that familiarity is precisely what endears it to me. The beauty of Christmas lies in its ability to evoke memories of past celebrations, and Christmas publications should take pride in their capacity to remind us of those earlier editions.

Although I remain skeptical about receiving everything I desire from any single publication this year, I am confident that the collective offerings will adequately evoke the spirit of Christmas. During the drab days of November, it is comforting to be reminded that the holiday season is approaching. Perhaps it is fortunate that the needs of the colonies prompt such early release of Christmas issues. Yet, I find it perplexing why New Zealand requires a Christmas edition at all. As I reflect on the blueprint of my ideal magazine, I am increasingly aware of how delightful it would be—but, oh, certainly not with the thermometer soaring to a hundred degrees in the shade.
