I recently came across a rather serious article discussing the outlook for the upcoming year. This piece included insights from various industry experts, featuring even a forecast from a meteorologist who, unsurprisingly, predicted a rainy summer. It concluded with a general overview of the year by Old Moore or perhaps one of the lesser-known prophets. Regrettably, Old Moore’s musings did not resonate with me.

I wish I could embrace astrology, but I find myself unable to do so. The notion that celestial bodies align in specific formations to guide figures like Zadkiel in predicting the future seems unfathomably grand. The thought of a star diverting its course by millions of miles simply to signify a “scandalous divorce in high society” is just too much to take seriously. Truth be told, I don’t believe the stars concern themselves with such matters. Their purpose and nature remain a mystery to me; however, I doubt that a starlit sky serves merely to caution some unpleasant financier about an impending upheaval in South Africa. While an ordinary person gazes at the cosmos and contemplates their own insignificance, a believer in astrology looks upward and feels an overwhelming sense of importance. In a way, I suppose I am relieved that I do not share that belief.

Navigating life must be quite challenging for those who are superstitious. Just the other evening at dinner, I mentioned that I had never faced the peril of drowning. I can’t say for certain if that was entirely accurate, but I still think it was an innocuous remark. Before I could elaborate on my point (whatever it was), I was abruptly instructed to touch wood. I argued that both my feet were planted firmly on polished oak and my elbows rested on polished mahogany—one of those good habits that seem to stem from instinct. Nevertheless, since arguing at the dinner table is generally frowned upon, I tapped the surface a few times, and now, I suppose, I am protected from misfortune. Still, I am curious about who exactly I have appeased.

This wood-touching superstition suggests that an ominous spirit is always lurking nearby, keenly listening for any boastful claims. “I have never had the mumps,” one might declare nonchalantly. “Oh really?” the spirit might retort, “Just you wait until next Tuesday.” Without realizing it, we attribute our human flaws to Fate. If someone were standing at the edge of a pond and declared, “I have never fallen into a pond in my life,” the urge to give him a little nudge would be nearly irresistible. The impulse would come from us, of course; yet, one must assume that Providence has gained enough self-control by this point.

In reality, it’s unlikely that anyone actually believes that good or evil spirits have a particular affinity for wood, or that they require it to be stroked. Even the most devoutly superstitious individuals probably don’t think that Fate is overly sensitive about salt or ladders. Likewise, many who toss spilled salt over their shoulders are not superstitious at all but merely displaying a readiness for social situations—a trait often associated with good manners. However, a considerable number of people feel the need to appease the unknown and acknowledge the forces that may bring harm. They engage in these little rituals hoping to secure protection from misfortune in return. While the tribute they pay is minimal, it nevertheless represents a recognition of the unseen powers at play.

A balanced perspective leaves little room for superstition. When someone says, “I have never been in a shipwreck,” they often feel compelled to touch wood. But what prompts this anxiety? Perhaps it’s the memory of a news article that read, “Among the deceased was Mr. —. By a curious coincidence, this gentleman had mentioned only days prior that he had never been in a shipwreck. Little did he know that his next voyage would tragically prove him wrong.” It’s likely that he has encountered such accounts repeatedly. Yet, he has never seen a headline stating, “Among the deceased was Mr. —. By a curious coincidence, this gentleman never claimed he hadn’t been in a shipwreck.” The truth is, that could have been truthfully recorded countless times. A sense of proportion would reveal that when only one side of a story is chronicled, that side gains undue significance. Ultimately, Fate does not seek to be dramatic; if we had the power to dictate life and death, we might orchestrate remarkably vivid narratives. Someone who carelessly spills salt might meet an unfortunate end in the Dead Sea, and a couple who marries in May could simultaneously perish the following May. However, Fate does not concern itself with crafting clever twists and turns. It functions according to the mundane laws of chance, occasionally yielding something astonishing and romantic. Superstition flourishes because only the rare, coincidental dramas are reported.

Yet, there are also charms believed to promote happiness, not just to ward off misfortune. I find myself a firm believer in these. I don’t necessarily think that hanging a horseshoe in one’s home will magically bring good fortune; rather, I believe that if someone genuinely believes in its power, then the act of displaying that horseshoe will likely lead to good luck. When one believes they will encounter fortune, they approach their tasks with a smile, facing setbacks with optimism and beginning anew with a positive attitude. This mindset puts them on the path toward happiness.
