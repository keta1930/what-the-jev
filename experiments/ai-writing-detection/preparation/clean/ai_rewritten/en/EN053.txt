There was a time when I stumbled upon Samuel Butler—not the other two, mind you, but the one who crafted The Way of All Flesh, a novel I deem the second-best in the English literary canon. I use the term "second-best" to grant myself the opportunity to concede that should you mention Tom Jones, The Mayor of Casterbridge, or any of your favored works, I could easily agree that those might hold the top spot. My discovery of Butler was akin to Voltaire's finding of Habakkuk, or perhaps like a young child uncovering Shakespeare for the first time. I even shared my excitement in two enthusiastic articles, but, unsurprisingly, the world remained indifferent. It was already well acquainted with Samuel Butler.

Recently, I came across a French author named Claude Tillier, who penned a book titled Mon Oncle Benjamin in the early part of the last century. The title may be translated as My Uncle Benjamin, although I read it in translation. Although I am eager to sing its praises, I will exercise restraint. I think I can navigate my admiration for Tillier without the same risk I took with Butler, but I hesitate. The prospect of your derision at my previous ignorance of the illustrious Tillier, or your amused disdain at my belated borrowing of this classic work that informed your own upbringing, weighs heavily on me. Let's leave it at that. Claude Tillier—who hasn’t heard of him? Mon Oncle Benjamin—who hasn’t read it, either in its original French or (as I did) in American English? Let’s move on to discuss another literary gem.

I am eager to share another discovery, a book that should hold the status of a classic but unfortunately does not. It is a work that remains largely unknown, except perhaps through my own recommendations. This book was released around twelve years ago and is the final publication from a well-known author. When I mention his name, you will likely exclaim, "Oh yes! I adore his works!" and proceed to name his other celebrated titles. However, when I inquire if you’ve read my book, you will look surprised and admit to never having encountered it. You may ask, "Is it as good as SO-AND-SO and SUCH-AND-SUCH?" with skepticism in your voice. I would respond, "Much better," and if the circumstances were just right, that might add a little something to my pocket. However, I would be perfectly satisfied with just your appreciation. The author of my treasured book is none other than Kenneth Grahame. You’ve heard of him? Excellent, I suspected as much. The works you’re familiar with are The Golden Age and Dream Days, am I correct? Thank you for confirming. But the title you haven’t explored—my book—is The Wind in the Willows. Am I right again? Ah, I feared so.

The reason I am confident in your unfamiliarity with it is precisely why I refer to it as "my" book. For the past ten to twelve years, I have been promoting it. Typically, I bring it up when I meet someone new, much like how others might comment on the weather. If I fail to mention it at the outset, I find a way to introduce it by the conversation's end. The stranger must hear about it at some point. Should I ever find myself in legal trouble, and one can never be certain, my response to the query of whether I have anything to say would surely be, "If I may, I’d like to recommend a book to the jury before I conclude." Mr. Justice Darling might feign familiarity, but I wouldn’t be fooled.

Recommending a book to countless individuals over the years has revealed the astonishing fact that none of them had previously heard of The Wind in the Willows until I enlightened them. Some were unaware of Kenneth Grahame entirely; well, one does not need to see such people again. But the majority were in your position—a great admiration for the author and his two earlier works, yet oblivious to this later masterpiece. I secured their promises to read it before they departed and anxiously awaited their expressions of gratitude. It is quite possible that they, in turn, shared the good news, and perhaps that information eventually reached you, but the acknowledgment was due to me nonetheless. For instance, you might have caught a couple of casual mentions of it, as if it were a universally acknowledged classic, in a well-known novel published last year. I was the one who introduced that author to the book six months prior. At times, I feel as if I wrote The Wind in the Willows and recommended it to Kenneth Grahame himself, though I may be mistaken, as I have yet to meet him. Nor, as I have lamented, do I have any financial stake in its sales—a clarification that suspicious newcomers occasionally demand from me.

I won’t attempt to describe the book, as no words can do it justice. What I will say is this: it is what I term a Household Book. A Household Book is one that everyone in the home cherishes and frequently quotes long after reading. It is the kind of book that is read aloud to every new visitor, becoming a benchmark for their worthiness. Yet, despite its universal appeal, it makes you feel that although everyone in the household loves it, only you truly grasp its significance and that others barely deserve it. You convince yourself that the author had you in mind while writing it—"I hope this pleases Jones," you imagine were his last thoughts as he set down his pen.

Naturally, you will rush to order the book immediately. However, I feel it’s only fair to offer a word of caution. When you finally sit down with it, do not be absurd enough to think that you are judging my taste or even Kenneth Grahame’s genius. No, you are merely evaluating yourself. … You may be worthy; I cannot say for certain. But make no mistake, it is you who is on trial.
